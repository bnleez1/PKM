# Unit I: Theoretical Framework (backup)

# Stage 1 - Desired Results

## Established Goals

Draft a theoretical framework in support of a recent research project to be published in English to a peer reviewed journal.

### Transfer 

- Learners will be able to independently use their knowledge of writing in Spanish when comparing and contrasting their writing of an academic text in English.

### Meaning 

---

### Understandings: 

*Learners will understand that…*

- Writing an article depends on personal motivations as well as a target audience and journal guidelines.


---

### Essential Questions:

- Where should I publish my work?

- How is publishing my work in English similar and different from publishing in Spanish?


---

### Acquisition 

---

### *Learners will know…* 

- What the guidelines are for their chosen journal

    - what the problem, purpose, target audience, and thesis statement are 


---

### *Students will be able to…*

- Write an academic (formal) text using standard English that is coherent, cohesive, and on topic.


---

    - Writing checklist of Dos and Don'ts

        - Avoid phrases like, "it is important", "it is essential", "it is vital", etc.

        - Active voice is preferred.

        - Avoid phrases with the nonreferential "it" in the passive voice: "it is known", "it has been determined", etc. - Note: Anytime you begin a sentence with the subject pronoun, "it", consider rewording as to avoid the subject pronoun in the main clause.

        - As you add citations to your text, add respective references (our endnotes) at the end of your document.

        - Try to avoid mixing your original ideas with ideas that come from an outside source within the same sentence.

        - Begin each body paragraph with a topic sentence that captures the main idea or the main point of your paragraph. In the topic sentence, try to avoid linking verbs (e.g., verb "to be") and instead try to use more dynamic verbs that show action. See [Linking Verbs: The Hidden Connectors of English](Linking%20Verbs%20The%20Hidden%20Connectors%20of%20English.md) & [[Differences between a topic sentence and thesis statement]].

        - When developing each body paragraph consider [Organizing an effective argument: Methods and examples](https://app.capacities.io/f1711151-3e8c-449c-8203-f47df61f6ea2/db44bc1c-da4b-477d-b7c6-b0ee97af6621?bid=9328ef97-fcb6-4366-b8f5-e687f17022d4). The Toulmin Method uses grounds (i.e., evidence) to support claims (e.g., topic sentences that are the main ideas of each body paragraph). Thus, consider the role of each sentence and paragraph as you layout your original ideas that are supported by evidence that comes from outside sources. See also [[(MEAL) Plan - Body Paragraph Development]].

        - Add indentations, spacing, and font size and styles to your draft in Word according to the publication guidelines.

        - For the most part, avoid signposting that refers to language you use to refer to your study (e.g., this study seeks to..., etc.) and that refers to what you plan to do as the writer (e.g., I will discuss this, this, and this.). Signposting that references your study is more likely in the method and/or results and discussion sections of your article.
