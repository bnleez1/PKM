The convergence among these works is quite strong. They use different terminology and sometimes order elements differently, but they repeatedly return to the same relationships: **a worthwhile problem, informed by existing knowledge, leads to purposeful research questions; those questions determine the design and methods; and the entire study must be coherent, ethical, feasible, and defensible.**

## Assessment of your original process

Your original diagram was:

**Choose topic → Narrow it → Identify problem → Develop research questions → Create research design → Write proposal**

I would keep the underlying logic, but modify each stage somewhat.

|Original stage|Assessment from the synthesis|Recommendation|
|---|---|---|
|**1. Choose your research topic**|Strongly supported|**Keep it.** Treat this as a broad area of interest rather than a final topic.|
|**2. Narrow it down and find your niche**|Supported, but incomplete|**Add preliminary literature searching.** Researchers cannot reliably identify a “niche” simply through brainstorming.|
|**3. Identify a research problem**|Essential|**Expand it to problem/gap + significance.** Learners should explain both what is problematic/unknown and why it matters.|
|**4. Develop clear research questions**|Essential and strongly supported|**Keep, but add purpose before it.** Purpose connects the problem to the questions.|
|**5. Create a research design to answer them**|Correct but too compressed|Separate **overall design/framework** from concrete **methods** such as participants, sampling, data collection, and analysis.|
|**6. Write your research proposal**|Correct|Change to **write, evaluate, revise, and refine**, because proposal development is iterative rather than simply the final linear step.|

### The biggest weakness: literature appears too late—or not at all

This is the most important modification I would make.

Booth et al. explicitly connect focused topics and questions with research problems and source investigation. Creswell gives the literature review its own early stage. Kumar includes literature review as part of moving from a problem toward design. Yin describes using the literature to narrow an interest and sharpen potential research questions. Leedy and Ormrod similarly place the review of related literature immediately after establishing the research problem. ([University of Chicago Press](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/C/bo215874008?utm_source=chatgpt.com "The Craft of Research, Fifth Edition, Booth, Colomb, Williams"))

So:

> **“Narrow it down and find your niche” should become something like “Explore the literature and narrow your focus.”**

That distinction matters pedagogically. A learner should not simply _invent_ a research gap. The preliminary literature helps the learner determine what is already known, what remains uncertain, and whether the proposed problem is actually worth investigating.

For your Academic Writing course, I would call this a **preliminary literature scan** rather than a full literature review. That fits particularly well with your syllabus: Unit I already includes narrowing a topic and AI-supported literature mapping, while Units III and IV subsequently develop the annotated bibliography and full literature review.

## The second important addition: significance

A research problem alone is not enough.

Booth repeatedly brings researchers back to **“So what?”** Punch frames proposal development around _what the study asks, how it will answer it, and why the research is worth doing_. Maxwell similarly begins his model with the goals of the study—why it should be conducted and why anyone should care about its results. ([University of Chicago Press](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/C/bo215874008?utm_source=chatgpt.com "The Craft of Research, Fifth Edition, Booth, Colomb, Williams"))

Therefore I would change:

**Identify a research problem**

to:

### **Identify the research problem and explain its significance**

For your students, that can remain very simple:

**What is happening? → What do we still need to understand? → Why does understanding it matter?**

That is a stronger progression than simply telling students to “find a gap.”

## The third modification: add purpose

Creswell separates the **purpose statement** from the research questions, while Maxwell distinguishes the study's goals from its questions. ([SAGE Publishing](https://www.sagepub.com/shop/buy-a-book/research-design-6-270550?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Research Design"))

That distinction is useful for novice researchers:

**Problem:** What needs to be understood?  
**Purpose:** What will this study attempt to understand?  
**Research questions:** What exactly must the study answer?

This gives students a much clearer logical chain:

> **Problem → Purpose → Questions**

I would definitely include this in the infographic.

## The fourth modification: distinguish research design from methods

Your statement **“Create a research design to answer them”** is conceptually correct. In fact, one of the strongest findings across the ten sources is that **research questions should drive methodological choices**, not the other way around. Punch explicitly says methods depend on research questions; Maxwell similarly argues that methods must enable researchers to answer their questions. Creswell recommends selecting an approach that best fits the research question. ([SAGE Publishing](https://us.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/upm-binaries/11390_03_PunchDERP_Ch03.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com "PunchDERP-3440-Ch-03.qxd  7/4/2006  6:21 PM  Page 19"))

But novice researchers benefit from distinguishing two levels:

**Research design** = the overall strategy or logic of the study.

**Methods** = what researchers will actually do: whom or what they will study, how participants or cases will be selected, how evidence will be collected, and how it will be analyzed.

Babbie, Kumar, Yin, Leedy and Ormrod, and Robson all make these elements much more explicit than your original diagram. ([Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Practice_of_Social_Research.html?id=Oq-nswEACAAJ&utm_source=chatgpt.com "The Practice of Social Research - Earl R. Babbie - Google Books"))

## The fifth modification: the process should not look completely linear

Maxwell is especially important here. His model explicitly treats goals, conceptual framework, questions, methods, and validity as **interactive components**. A change in one can require reconsidering another. Punch likewise describes proposal development as thinking, planning, justifying, drafting, and refining rather than simply completing a template. Marshall and Rossman emphasize flexibility as well. ([SAGE Publications](https://uk.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/upm-binaries/5055_Maxwell_Chapter_1.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com "01-Maxwell.qxd  10/1/2004  3:13 PM  Page 1"))

So the arrows should primarily move downward for pedagogical clarity, **but the graphic should include a return arrow indicating that researchers frequently revisit earlier stages**.

For example, a student may develop an excellent question and then discover that there is no feasible way to obtain the required participants. That discovery should cause the student to revise the question—not press forward simply because Step 4 has already been “completed.”

# Recommended final process

Based on the synthesis, I would now recommend **eight stages** for your students:

1. **Choose a broad area of interest**  
    Begin with a phenomenon, issue, experience, population, or educational situation worth exploring.
    
2. **Explore the literature and narrow the focus**  
    Conduct a preliminary search to discover what is known, identify important concepts, and determine a manageable focus.
    
3. **Define the research problem and significance**  
    Explain what is happening, what remains uncertain or insufficiently understood, and why investigating it matters.
    
4. **State the purpose of the study**  
    Clearly state what the proposed research intends to explore, understand, describe, explain, or investigate.
    
5. **Develop focused research questions**  
    Create questions that follow directly from the problem and purpose and are specific enough to guide the study.
    
6. **Select an appropriate research design and conceptual lens**  
    Determine the broad research approach and the concepts or theoretical perspective that will guide the inquiry.
    
7. **Plan the methods**  
    Identify the context, participants/cases, sampling or selection strategy, data collection procedures, and anticipated approach to analysis.
    
8. **Write, evaluate, and refine the proposal**  
    Demonstrate that the problem, purpose, questions, literature, design, and methods form one coherent and defensible study.
    

I would then place a band **underneath all eight stages**, rather than adding more numbered steps:

**THROUGHOUT THE PROCESS:**  
**Ethics • Feasibility • Alignment • Quality/Rigor • Reflection & Revision**

That reflects Creswell's treatment of ethics, Maxwell's concern with validity, Marshall and Rossman's attention to trustworthiness and practical constraints, Babbie's treatment of research ethics, Kumar's treatment of ethics and validity/reliability, and Robson's emphasis on ethical and real-world feasibility considerations. ([SAGE Publishing](https://www.sagepub.com/shop/buy-a-book/research-design-6-270550?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Research Design"))

### One change I would make to the image I generated earlier

The previous revised graphic was already much closer to the research-methods literature, but after this more systematic comparison I would **change its wording and ordering slightly**.

Instead of:

**Narrow focus → Problem/gap → Purpose/objectives → Questions → Framework/design → Methods**

I recommend:

**Explore literature & narrow focus → Problem + significance → Purpose → Questions → Design + conceptual lens → Methods**

I would also **drop “hypotheses” from the main graphic for your Academic Writing students** if their initial proposals remain qualitative. Hypotheses are appropriate for some quantitative designs, but they should not appear to be a universal research requirement. Creswell explicitly distinguishes questions and hypotheses according to methodological approach, and Maxwell's qualitative model centers research questions rather than hypotheses. ([SAGE Publishing](https://www.sagepub.com/shop/buy-a-book/research-design-6-270550?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Research Design"))

That final eight-stage version is the one I would use instructionally. It keeps the **simplicity of your original diagram**, but it also captures the central consensus of these ten methods authorities without overwhelming seventh-semester Academic Writing students.
