# (UN) improper paragraph alignment

# Finding Your Focus: What the "UN" (Unclear or Unaligned) Code Means

When you get an essay back from your instructor, finding grammar corrections like "spelling" or "wrong word" is usually straightforward to fix. But what happens when you see the letters **UN** written next to an entire paragraph?

**UN** stands for **Unclear Main Idea** or **Improper Paragraph Alignment**.

Unlike a grammar mistake, a "UN" error means the *structure* of your thoughts is confusing the reader. Even if your English grammar is 100% perfect, a paragraph can still receive a "UN" code if the reader cannot figure out the main point, or if the ideas do not connect logically to each other.

Here is a breakdown of the two distinct problems the "UN" code highlights and how to fix them.

## Problem 1: The Main Idea is Unclear (The "What is this about?" Error)

In English academic writing, paragraphs are expected to be highly direct. The reader expects to know exactly what a paragraph is about within the very first sentence. If the main idea is hidden, missing, or mixed up with too many other ideas, it is marked as unclear.

- **The Error:** You write a paragraph full of interesting facts, quotes, and statistics, but you never actually state the main point you are trying to make.

- **The Reality:** The reader is forced to guess your point. In English writing, it is the writer’s job to be clear, not the reader’s job to guess.

- **The Fix:** Every paragraph needs a strong **Topic Sentence**. This is usually the first sentence of the paragraph, and it acts as an "umbrella" for everything underneath it. If a sentence doesn't fit under that umbrella, it belongs in a different paragraph.

## Problem 2: Improper Paragraph Alignment (The "Why is this here?" Error)

"Alignment" refers to how well your ideas line up with one another. A paragraph has bad alignment if it wanders off-topic or fails to connect back to the main thesis of your essay.

- **The Error:** You start a paragraph arguing that electric cars are too expensive (your topic sentence), but halfway through, you start talking about how electric car batteries are bad for the environment.

- **The Reality:** You have accidentally merged two different paragraphs into one. The environmental impact is a great point, but it does not *align* with the topic of cost.

- **The Fix:** One paragraph = one controlling idea. When you introduce a new main idea, you must hit the "Enter" key and start a new paragraph.

## The 3-Step Strategy to Fix "UN" Errors

If your teacher marked a paragraph with "UN," do not just rewrite the same sentences. Use this structural checkup to find the focus:

### 1. The "First Sentence" Test

Look at the paragraph your teacher marked. Read *only* the first sentence. If someone walked into the room and you read that one sentence to them, would they know exactly what the rest of the paragraph is going to be about? If not, rewrite your topic sentence so it acts like a clear road sign.

### 2. The Reverse Outline (Highlighting)

Take a highlighter and read your "UN" paragraph sentence by sentence.

- Highlight the main claim in **yellow**.

- Highlight your evidence or examples in **green**.

- Highlight your analysis (your explanation) in **blue**.
If you find a sentence that doesn't fit any of those colors, or if it belongs to a completely different topic, delete it or move it somewhere else.

### 3. Check the Bridge (Transitions)

Sometimes a paragraph is marked "unaligned" simply because the reader got lost moving from sentence A to sentence B. Use transition words (*Furthermore, However, Consequently, For instance*) to build a bridge between your ideas so the reader can see how they connect.

> **Key insight:** A "UN" mark is actually a compliment to your developing language skills. It means your vocabulary and grammar are strong enough to express complex ideas—now, you just need to organize those ideas into a shape the reader can easily follow.
