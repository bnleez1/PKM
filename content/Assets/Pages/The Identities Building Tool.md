# The Identities Building Tool

# Performing the Self: A Guide to James Paul Gee’s "Identities Building Tool"

When we think about communication, we often imagine a simple exchange of ideas: one person transmits a message, and another receives it. However, every time we speak or write, we are doing much more than transferring data. We are actively constructing who we are in that exact moment, and simultaneously telling our audience who we think *they* are. In his comprehensive framework for discourse analysis, James Paul Gee introduces the "Identities Building Tool," a critical lens that examines how language is used to enact, project, and recognize social identities.

By shifting our focus from the content of a message to the persona delivering it, this tool reveals that identity is not a static internal state, but a dynamic performance. The words we choose, the grammar we deploy, and the tone we adopt are the costumes and props we use to build our social reality.

## What is the Identities Building Tool?

The Identities Building Tool requires the analyst to look at a text and ask: *What recognizable identity or persona is the speaker or writer trying to enact? And what identity are they projecting onto their audience?*

Gee argues that we do not have a single, monolithic identity. Instead, we possess a "core identity" that is fractured into multiple "social identities" depending on the context. You do not speak to your department head the same way you speak to your spouse, your students, or your dog. In each interaction, you use language to embody a different role: the deferential employee, the equal partner, the authoritative expert, or the affectionate caretaker. Gee refers to these recognizable ways of speaking, acting, and valuing as "Discourses."

Language is the primary mechanism for signaling which Discourse you are currently operating within. If a speaker uses heavy academic jargon, passive voice, and abstract nouns, they are not just sharing research; they are using the Identities Building Tool to enact the identity of a "rigorous scholar." Crucially, identity building is reciprocal. By enacting the identity of a "scholar," the speaker simultaneously positions the audience as "peers" (who are expected to understand the jargon) or "novices" (who are expected to defer to the speaker’s expertise).

## Common Misconceptions

Because our cultural understanding of "identity" is often tied to modern psychology and concepts of a "true self," applying this tool to linguistic analysis often requires unlearning a few persistent myths.

**Misconception 1: Identity is a static, internal truth.**
The most common mistake is treating identity as something you simply *are*, regardless of the situation. The Identities Building Tool insists that social identity is something you *do*. It is an active performance that must be recognized by others to be successful. If you try to enact the identity of a "tough, authoritative boss" but use hesitant language, excessive apologies, and upward inflection, your audience will not recognize that identity. Identity exists in the social negotiation between speaker and listener, not solely inside the speaker’s head.

**Misconception 2: We have one authentic voice.**
We frequently hear advice to "find your authentic voice" in writing or speaking. This implies that altering your language for different audiences is somehow fake or manipulative. Gee’s framework shows that shifting identities is a fundamental, necessary feature of human communication. A doctor who speaks to a terrified patient using the exact same clinical terminology they use with a fellow surgeon is not being "authentic"; they are failing to recognize the social context. All of our social identities are authentic parts of who we are, calibrated for different Discourses.

**Misconception 3: Identity building is always a conscious choice.**
Analysts sometimes assume that writers meticulously select every pronoun and adjective to manipulate how they are perceived. While politicians and copywriters certainly do this, the vast majority of our identity building is entirely unconscious. We are socialized into our Discourses so deeply that enacting them feels natural. A university administrator writes in heavily bureaucratic, nominalized language not because they are consciously trying to sound impersonal, but because that is the habitual language required to successfully inhabit the identity of an "administrator."

## Applying the Tool: A Practical Example

To understand how the Identities Building Tool functions in practice, let us examine a common scenario in higher education: an instructor providing written feedback on a student’s research paper.

Consider two different ways an instructor might write a comment regarding a flawed methodology section.

**Text A:**

> "The methodology section is fundamentally flawed. You failed to account for selection bias in the sampling process. This invalidates the subsequent data. Revise and resubmit."

**Text B:**

> "You’ve tackled an incredibly ambitious topic here! As I read through your methodology, I found myself wondering how we might tighten up the sampling process to avoid selection bias. Let's brainstorm some ways to strengthen this section for the next draft."

Both texts deliver the exact same informational payload: the methodology has a bias problem and needs to be rewritten. However, applying the Identities Building Tool reveals that the instructors are constructing entirely different social realities.

**Analyzing Text A:**

- **The Instructor's Identity:** This text builds the identity of the *Gatekeeper* or the *Objective Authority*. By using the passive voice and abstract nouns ("The methodology section," "the subsequent data"), the instructor removes themselves as a human reader and speaks with the voice of institutional correctness. The blunt syntax and imperative verbs ("Revise and resubmit") enact unquestioned authority.

- **The Student's Identity:** The student is positioned as a subordinate offender. The use of the direct "You failed" casts the student in the identity of someone who has broken an academic law and must be corrected.

**Analyzing Text B:**

- **The Instructor's Identity:** This text builds the identity of the *Collaborator* or *Mentor*. The instructor uses first-person pronouns ("I found myself wondering") to present themselves as a human reader experiencing the text, rather than an objective judge. The language is informal ("tighten up," "brainstorm").

- **The Student's Identity:** The student is positioned as a capable, albeit developing, peer. By using inclusive pronouns ("how *we* might tighten up"), the text casts the student as a partner in the academic process. The praise at the beginning ("ambitious topic") validates the student's identity as a scholar before offering the critique.

Through this analysis, we see that grammar and vocabulary are not just delivering a grade; they are actively building the social relationship between the two individuals.

## Conclusion

James Paul Gee’s Identities Building Tool transforms how we read by reminding us that every text is a mirror reflecting the social world of its author. It forces us to ask not just what is being said, but *who* is saying it, and who they are asking us to be in response. For educators, researchers, and critical thinkers, this tool is invaluable. It demystifies the ways in which authority is claimed, solidarity is built, and power is exercised through the subtle, everyday choices we make in our grammar and vocabulary. Language is never just a neutral tool for describing the world; it is the very material we use to build ourselves.
