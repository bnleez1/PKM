# How Does Deixis Relate to Referencing?

Referencing (anaphora, cataphora, and exophora) and deixis are closely related, but they are not identical.

**Deixis** is the broader idea that some expressions depend on the **context of the speech situation** for their interpretation. Typical deictic expressions include **I, you, here, there, now, then, this,** and **that**. Your Week 2 materials define deixis as context-dependent expressions such as _here, now, this,_ and _you_.

The clearest connection is with **exophoric reference**. Exophoric reference points **outside the text** to something in the physical or situational context. For example:

> “Put **that** over **there**.”

To interpret **that** and **there**, we need to know what the speaker is pointing to and where the interaction is taking place. In that sense, the expressions are both **deictic** and **exophoric**. Celce-Murcia and Olshtain explicitly describe this kind of situational reference as **deictic reference**.

By contrast, **anaphoric** and **cataphoric** reference are usually **textual** rather than situational. Your Chapter 3 materials make this distinction directly: anaphora points backward in the text, cataphora points forward, while exophoric reference depends on the situation.

A useful way to show the relationship is:

|Concept|Where meaning comes from|Example|
|---|---|---|
|**Anaphoric reference**|Earlier text|_Maria arrived. **She** sat down._|
|**Cataphoric reference**|Later text|_When **he** arrived, **Carlos** sat down._|
|**Exophoric reference**|Outside the text, in the situation|_Put **that** over **there**._|
|**Deixis**|Speaker-centered context: person, place, time, discourse, etc.|_I, you, here, now, this, that_|

The most important distinction is:

**Exophoric reference is often deictic, but deixis is a broader category.**

For example, **here** is spatial deixis, **now** is temporal deixis, and **I/you** are person deixis. All depend on the context of speaking. When one of these forms points to something outside the text, it is functioning exophorically.

So to summarize...

**Anaphoric = look back in the text**  
**Cataphoric = look forward in the text**  
**Exophoric = look outside the text**  
**Deixis = interpret from the speaker’s context**

That last distinction is especially useful because it helps learners see that reference is about **what an expression points to**, while deixis is about **how interpretation depends on the context of the speaking event**.

**Exophoric reference** means that the referent must be recovered from **outside the text**. **Deixis** is a particular kind of context-dependent reference whose interpretation depends on the **speech situation**—especially speaker, addressee, time, place, or discourse location.

So an exophoric expression can be **non-deictic** when its referent comes from shared background knowledge or the broader cultural/institutional context rather than from the immediate "who/where/when" of the utterance.

|Example|Exophoric expression|Why exophoric?|Why not (primarily) deictic?|
|---|---|---|---|
|**The president** will address the nation tonight.|_the president_|The text does not identify which president; outside knowledge supplies the referent.|_the president_ does not inherently encode speaker, location, or time.|
|**The university** has changed its admissions policy.|_the university_|In a university community, participants may know which institution is intended.|The expression itself does not point to the speaker's spatial/temporal position.|
|**The government** announced new regulations.|_the government_|Shared social/national context identifies the government.|It is a definite NP whose interpretation relies on background knowledge, not a deictic expression such as _this_ or _here_.|
|**The principal** wants to see you.|_the principal_|Speakers within a school may share knowledge of who occupies that role.|The referent is recovered through institutional knowledge.|
|**The final exam** is worth 30% of your grade.|_the final exam_|Students in the course know which exam is intended, although it has not been mentioned in the text.|Identification comes from shared course knowledge rather than the immediate speech coordinates.|
|**The cafeteria** closes at six.|_the cafeteria_|Members of a campus community may know which cafeteria is intended.|No inherently deictic form identifies it; shared situational/institutional knowledge does.|

Compare these with clearly **deictic exophoric** examples:

- "**That** book is mine." → _that_ requires the physical/situational context.
- "Put it **there**." → _there_ depends on the speaker's spatial perspective.
- "I'll do it **tomorrow**." → _tomorrow_ depends on the time of speaking.
- "**She** is my professor." [speaker points to someone] → _she_ is interpreted through the immediate situation.

Review:

> **Exophoric reference = look outside the text for the referent.**  
> **Deixis = look to the speaker's contextual coordinates to interpret the expression.**

Therefore: **"The dean has approved the proposal."**

If everyone at UAA knows which dean is meant, **the dean** can function exophorically through shared institutional knowledge without _the dean_ itself being a deictic expression.

By contrast: **"She approved this yesterday."**

_she_, _this_, and _yesterday_ can all function exophorically, but their interpretation is tied much more directly to the immediate communicative context and is therefore **deictic**.

**One caveat**: terminology varies among discourse analysts. Some use **deixis very broadly**, to the point that much situational/exophoric reference can be described as deictic. Under the narrower linguistic definition, however, **shared-knowledge definite descriptions** such as _the dean, the government,_ and _the final exam_ are especially useful examples of **exophoric reference without inherently deictic forms**.
