# The Deixis Tool

# Pointing at Power: Unpacking James Paul Gee’s Deixis Tool

Language is never a closed system hovering in a vacuum; it is permanently tethered to the physical, social, and ideological world in which it is spoken. In *How to Do Discourse Analysis: A Toolkit*, linguist James Paul Gee introduces "The Deixis Tool" (Tool #1 in his framework) to help analysts map exactly how these tethers work.

Derived from the Greek word for "pointing" or "indicating," deixis refers to the class of words whose specific meaning cannot be understood without immediate contextual information. If you find a note on the ground that reads, "I need you to bring that here tomorrow," the sentence is grammatically flawless, yet entirely meaningless. Who is *I*? Who is *you*? What is *that*? Where is *here*? When is *tomorrow*?

The Deixis Tool trains us to look at these "pointing words" not just as grammatical placeholders, but as powerful instruments of social engineering. By analyzing how a speaker uses deixis, we can uncover hidden assumptions, map social hierarchies, and reveal who is included—and who is excluded—in a given interaction.

## What is the Deixis Tool?

In discourse analysis, applying the Deixis Tool involves scanning a text or transcript specifically for deictic markers and asking: *How is the speaker using these words to tie what they are saying to the immediate context, and how are they positioning themselves relative to their audience and the topic?*

There are three primary categories of deixis to look out for:

1. **Person Deixis:** Pronouns like *I, you, we, they, us, them*. These point to the participants in the discourse.

2. **Spatial Deixis:** Words like *here, there, this, that, up, down*. These point to physical or ideological locations relative to the speaker.

3. **Temporal Deixis:** Words like *now, then, today, yesterday, soon*. These anchor the utterance in time relative to when it is spoken.

When we use the Deixis Tool, we are not simply circling pronouns. We are asking critical questions: How close or far is the speaker placing the subject? Who gets to be part of the "we"? Are certain groups being distanced as "them"? What is being treated as "given" or assumed knowledge based on the context?

## Common Misconceptions About Deixis

Because deixis is often taught in introductory grammar classes as a structural mechanism, discourse analysts—especially those in language teaching programs—often fall into a few conceptual traps.

### Misconception 1: Deixis is Just Grammar

The most common mistake is treating the Deixis Tool as a mere grammar-spotting exercise. Trainee analysts will often highlight all the pronouns and demonstratives in a transcript and think the job is done. However, in Gee’s framework, grammar is just the vehicle. The Deixis Tool is about *social positioning*. A pointing word is a choice. Choosing to say "those people" instead of "these people" is not just a grammatical shift from plural-far to plural-near; it is an act of social distancing and "othering."

### Misconception 2: Deixis Only Points to Physical Reality

It is easy to assume that *here* always refers to a room and *that* always refers to an object. In reality, deixis is frequently used to map **ideological and cultural space**. If a politician says, "We will not tolerate that kind of behavior here," the word *here* might mean "in this room," but it more likely means "in this nation" or "within our shared moral framework." *That* does not point to a physical object, but to an abstract set of values the speaker is pushing away.

### Misconception 3: The Meaning of "We" is Static

"We" is arguably the most dangerous and powerful word in the English language, yet analysts often assume its meaning is fixed. In reality, "we" is entirely fluid. There is the **inclusive "we"** (the speaker and the listener: "We need to work together") and the **exclusive "we"** (the speaker and their group, excluding the listener: "We have decided to let you go"). Speakers often slide between different versions of "we" within the same sentence to manipulate intimacy, authority, and consensus.

## Applying the Deixis Tool: A Classroom Example

To see the Deixis Tool in action, let’s apply it to a common context for English Language Teaching (ELT) trainees: a classroom transcript.

Imagine a scenario where a high school student is interrupting a lesson with off-topic jokes. The teacher stops the lesson, looks at the student, and says:

> *"Okay, stop. We don't do that here. You need to focus on this right now, or you can go over there."*

A novice might look at this and see a standard classroom management technique. But by applying the Deixis Tool, we can expose the intricate social reality the teacher is building.

- **"We" (Person Deixis):** Who is the "we"? It is an exclusive, institutional "we." The teacher is not including the misbehaving student in this "we"; rather, the teacher is aligning themselves with the institution, the rules, and the "good" students. The "we" creates a boundary line, and the student is currently on the outside of it.

- **"That" (Spatial/Conceptual Deixis):** The teacher does not name the behavior (e.g., "telling jokes"). By using "that," the teacher distances the behavior. It pushes the student's action away, labeling it as a foreign, undesirable object not worthy of a specific name.

- **"Here" (Spatial/Ideological Deixis):** "Here" does not just mean "in room 204." It means "within the academic culture I control." The teacher is using deixis to build a shared understanding of compliance that the student is violating.

- **"You" vs. "This" (Person and Conceptual Deixis):** "You need to focus on this." The teacher isolates the student ("you" singular) and demands alignment with "this" (the immediate academic task, positioned as close, central, and important).

- **"Over there" (Spatial Deixis):** If the student does not comply, they are threatened with physical and social exile. "There" (the principal's office, the hallway) represents the space for those who refuse to align with the "we" and the "here."

- **"Right now" (Temporal Deixis):** The teacher uses "right now" to anchor the command to the immediate present, leaving no room for negotiation or delay.

## Conclusion

By systematically applying the Deixis Tool, what initially appeared to be a simple behavioral correction is revealed to be a complex negotiation of power, inclusion, and institutional identity.

James Paul Gee’s Deixis Tool reminds us that words never just point at things; they point at power. For discourse analysts, language teachers, and students of applied linguistics, mastering this tool means developing the ability to see the invisible lines of connection and division that speakers draw in the air every time they open their mouths.
