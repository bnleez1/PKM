# (WC) Word Choice

# Sounding Natural: Decoding the "WC" (Word Choice) Code

When you get your writing back and see the letters **WC** in the margin, it can be a confusing experience. You look at your sentence: the grammar is perfect, the spelling is flawless, and your teacher understood exactly what you meant. So, what is the problem?

**WC** stands for **Word Choice**. It means that while your sentence is entirely intelligible (it makes sense), the specific words you chose sound awkward, unconventional, or atypical to a native English speaker.

Unlike a "Wrong Word" (WW) error—where the word completely changes or ruins the meaning of the sentence—a "WC" mark means you successfully communicated your idea. You just didn't say it the way it is traditionally said in English.

Here is a breakdown of why Word Choice errors happen and how you can start smoothing them out.

## The 3 Reasons Your Word Choice Sounds "Awkward"

When an instructor flags a sentence with a WC, it usually falls into one of three traps:

### 1. The Direct Translation (L1 Interference)

Every language has its own unique way of phrasing everyday concepts. When you translate an idea word-for-word from your native language (L1) into English, the grammar might be correct, but the phrasing sounds unnatural.

- **The Error:** "I have a big *doubt* about the homework assignment."

- **The Reality:** In many languages, you express confusion by saying you have a "doubt" (e.g., *tengo una duda* in Spanish). In English, we simply say, "I have a *question*."

- **The Fix:** Start noticing the "chunks" of language native speakers use to express everyday ideas, rather than translating your thoughts word-by-word.

### 2. The Thesaurus Trap (Over-complicating)

Many students believe that academic writing requires big, complicated words. They use a thesaurus to replace simple words with complex ones, resulting in sentences that sound forced or robotic.

- **The Error:** "The students *perambulated* to the library to *utilize* the books."

- **The Reality:** While *perambulated* technically means to walk, and *utilize* means to use, writing this way distracts the reader. "The students walked to the library to use the books" is much stronger.

- **The Fix:** Never use a ten-dollar word when a one-dollar word does the job perfectly. Clarity is always more academic than complexity.

### 3. The Near-Miss Collocation

As you learned with the "Wrong Word" code, words have natural partners (collocations). A WC error often happens when you choose a word that is *almost* the right partner, but not quite.

- **The Error:** "Because of the *strong* rain, traffic was terrible."

- **The Reality:** Rain can certainly be physically strong, but in English, the conventional phrase is *heavy* rain. Wind is *strong*; rain is *heavy*.

- **The Fix:** When your word choice is flagged as awkward, check a collocations dictionary to see which adjectives and verbs traditionally pair with your main noun.

## Why Getting a "WC" is Actually a Good Thing

If your essays are coming back with "WC" marks instead of basic grammar corrections, you should celebrate.

A Word Choice error is a high-level mistake. It proves that you have mastered English grammar well enough to put complex thoughts on paper, and that your vocabulary is expanding. You have crossed the bridge from *learning how to be understood* to *learning how to sound natural*.

## How to Fix "WC" Errors on Your Next Draft

To move past Word Choice errors, you need to transition from studying vocabulary lists to studying how language is actually used in the wild.

1. **Read your writing out loud.** If you have to pause, or if a sentence feels "clunky" in your mouth, the word choice is probably off.

2. **Use a Corpus, not a Dictionary.** Instead of a standard dictionary, try using a tool like YouGlish or Google Books Ngram Viewer. Type your phrase in to see if native speakers actually use those words together in published writing or speeches.

3. **Read extensively.** The only way to truly absorb the natural rhythm and conventional phrasing of a language is massive amounts of input. Read articles, essays, and books in English to build your intuition for what "sounds right."
