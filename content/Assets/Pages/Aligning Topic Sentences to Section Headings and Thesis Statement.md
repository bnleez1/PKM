# Aligning Topic Sentences to Section Headings and Thesis Statement

> [!info] See also
> [[Research Matrix]] · [[Thesis statement and skeleton outline]] · [[Second-language writing]]

## How to align topic sentences to sections and sections to a thesis statement

The thesis states the central argument and identifies its main supporting points. Each section develops one supporting point, and each paragraph’s topic sentence advances a specific claim within that section.

> [!note]
> The statements below form an illustrative argument outline. A full academic paper would need research evidence to support its claims.

## Thesis statement

To support second-language learners’ academic writing development, English language teachers can help student practice writing by providing focused written feedback, guiding revision, and encouraging self-assessment.
## Sentence Outline - Example of 13 paragraphs

### Providing Focused Written Feedback to Practive Academic Writing

1. Teachers direct students’ attention to recurring language errors through focused written feedback.
2. Selective comments help students address a manageable number of writing problems.
3. Specific explanations connect suggested corrections to the writer’s intended meaning.
4. Reader-focused questions prompt students to clarify claims and strengthen supporting details.
5. Feedback on successful passages encourages students to reuse effective writing strategies.
### Guiding Revision to Practice Academic Writing

6. Teachers demonstrate revision strategies through annotated examples of student writing.
7. Revision plans help students prioritize changes to content, organization, and language.
8. Guided rewriting activities challenge students to apply feedback when developing their arguments.
9. Comparisons between successive drafts help students evaluate how their revisions improve clarity.
### Encouraging Self-Assessment to Practice Academic Writing

10. Self-assessment checklists guide students through systematic reviews of their drafts.
11. Personal error logs help students identify recurring patterns in their second language writing.
12. Individual writing goals focus students’ attention on specific areas for improvement.
13. Reflective commentaries encourage students to justify their writing choices and assess their progress.

## Organizational Patterns

The sections mainly use **process organization**, though each follows a slightly different progression.

|Section|Best description|Progression|
|---|---|---|
|**Providing Focused Written Feedback**|**General-to-specific organization, followed by reinforcement**|Identify recurring errors → limit the feedback scope → explain corrections → address meaning and support → reinforce successful strategies.|
|**Guiding Revision**|**Process / instructional sequence**|Demonstrate revision → establish priorities → apply feedback → evaluate changes across drafts.|
|**Encouraging Self-Assessment**|**Process / reflective learning cycle**|Review a draft → identify recurring patterns → set improvement goals → reflect on choices and progress.|

**Least-to-most-important** would not accurately describe these sections because the topic sentences do not establish a ranking.

Across the three sections, the broader pattern follows a **gradual transfer of responsibility**: teacher feedback → guided revision → student self-assessment.

## Examples of [[Organizational Patterns - Text Structure]]

Common organizational patterns to consider for sections and paragraphs include:

|Pattern|How it organizes ideas|
|---|---|
|**Chronological order**|Follows events in the order they occurred.|
|**Process or sequence**|Explains steps or stages in an activity.|
|**Order of importance**|Moves from least to most important, or the reverse.|
|**General to specific**|Moves from a broad idea to details or examples.|
|**Specific to general**|Builds from examples or observations toward a broader conclusion.|
|**Comparison and contrast**|Examines similarities and differences, point by point or subject by subject.|
|**Cause and effect**|Explains causes, consequences, or a chain connecting them.|
|**Problem and solution**|Introduces a problem, examines possible responses, and supports a solution.|
|**Classification or division**|Groups a subject into categories or separates it into components.|
|**Definition and exemplification**|Explains a concept through its characteristics, boundaries, and examples.|
|**Spatial order**|Describes elements according to location or physical arrangement.|
|**Claim, evidence, and analysis**|States a claim, provides support, and explains how the support establishes the claim.|
|**Argument and counterargument**|Develops a position, considers objections, and responds to them.|
|**Thematic organization**|Groups ideas around recurring themes or issues.|
|**Synthesis across sources**|Organizes sources around shared findings, disagreements, or gaps.|

You can combine patterns at different levels: an essay might use **problem–solution**, a section might use **comparison–contrast**, and individual paragraphs might follow **claim–evidence–analysis**.
