# Grammatical Cohesion vs. Lexical Cohesion

**Cohesion** refers to the linguistic connections that help the parts of a text “hang together.” Celce-Murcia and Olshtain (2000) distinguish **four types of grammatical cohesive ties—reference, ellipsis, substitution, and conjunction—from lexical cohesive ties**. Together, these devices connect words, clauses, and propositions across a text.

| Type                 | How it creates cohesion                                                           | Example                                                                                                               |
| :------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Reference**        | Uses an expression such as a pronoun to point to another element.                 | *Maria submitted the paper.* ***She*** *finished it yesterday.*                                                       |
| **Ellipsis**         | Omits information that can be recovered from the surrounding discourse.           | A: *Who finished?* B: *Carlos* ***did*** *[finish].*                                                                  |
| **Substitution**     | Replaces an earlier linguistic item with another form such as *one, do,* or *so*. | *I need a marker. Do you have* ***one****?*                                                                           |
| **Conjunction**      | Explicitly signals relationships such as addition, contrast, cause, or time.      | *The task was difficult,* ***but*** *the students completed it.*                                                      |
| **Lexical cohesion** | Connects ideas through relationships among vocabulary items and their meanings.   | *The* ***professor*** *entered the classroom. The* ***teacher*** *greeted the* ***students*** *and* ***learners****.* |

The main difference, therefore, is **where the connection comes from**. **Grammatical cohesion relies primarily on grammatical resources and structural relationships**, whereas **lexical cohesion relies on relationships among words and meanings**. The lexical-cohesion reading identifies repetition, synonymy, antonymy, hyponymy/superordination, meronymy, and collocation as important ways vocabulary can establish these relationships. Across longer texts, related vocabulary may form **lexical chains** that maintain a topic or semantic thread.

For example:

> *The* ***students*** *entered the classroom.* ***They*** *opened their books while the* ***professor*** *prepared the lesson. The* ***learners*** *knew* ***she*** *would discuss the assignment.*

Here, **students → they** and **professor → she** are examples of **grammatical cohesion through reference**, while **students → learners** and **classroom → books → professor → lesson → assignment** create **lexical cohesion through semantic relationships**. As both readings emphasize, real texts normally use grammatical and lexical cohesion **together**, rather than relying exclusively on one type.

**Reference:** Celce-Murcia, M., & Olshtain, E. (2000). *Discourse and context in language teaching: A guide for language teachers*. Cambridge University Press.
