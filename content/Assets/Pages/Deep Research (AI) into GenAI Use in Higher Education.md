# Top Authorities and Dominant Standards for Generative AI in Higher Education

## Executive summary

The authority landscape for generative AI in higher education is not controlled by a single field leader or a single standard. It is a layered ecosystem. At the scholarly and pedagogical layer, the most authoritative figures are those whose work directly addresses teaching, assessment, academic integrity, and institutional adoption in universities. On that evidence base, the strongest current individual authorities are Mike Sharples, Wayne Holmes, Rose Luckin, Phillip Dawson, Ethan Mollick, Helen Crompton, Mike Perkins, Maha Bali, Sarah Elaine Eaton, and Leon Furze. They matter for different reasons: Sharples, Holmes, and Luckin are unusually strong at the intersection of scholarship and policy; Dawson and Perkins have become central in assessment reform; Mollick is the most visible public-facing university voice on day-to-day GenAI use; Crompton anchors the “state of the field” literature; Bali, Eaton, and Furze shape critical literacies, ethics, and practitioner uptake. [[1]](https://scholar.google.com/citations?hl=en&user=0KzFF40AAAAJ&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The standards landscape is also layered. The standards that most visibly dominate university practice are not only ethical guidelines. They include legal and privacy baselines, procurement instruments, interoperability specifications, and evaluation practices. The strongest evidence of real operational dominance points to a stack that includes UNESCO’s guidance for GenAI in education and research, NIST’s AI RMF and GenAI Profile, FERPA and GDPR, the EU AI Act for European institutions, HECVAT for vendor review, and 1EdTech’s LTI 1.3 and related TrustEd Apps frameworks for LMS integration and edtech procurement. In assessment, the most influential sector frameworks are TEQSA’s assessment reform resources in Australia, the Russell Group principles in the UK, and the AI Assessment Scale as a practical implementation framework. [[2]](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research)

The most mature, de facto technical standards in university operations are HECVAT and LTI 1.3. HECVAT is repeatedly embedded in real university procurement and security review workflows across U.S. campuses, while LTI 1.3 is supported across major LMS platforms and is the default direction of travel for integrations. That makes them more concretely “dominant” in day-to-day higher-ed operations than many broader AI ethics documents. [[3]](https://www.montclair.edu/information-technology/security/hecvat/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The biggest weakness in the current higher-ed GenAI standards environment is that governance is moving faster than validated practice. Universities now have many AI guidelines, but internal capability for model evaluation, red-teaming, privacy risk assessment, and post-deployment monitoring remains uneven. The strongest university-facing evaluation references are NIST’s red-teaming guidance, Stanford’s HELM transparency benchmark, and OWASP’s GenAI application security guidance, but these are still emerging as campus operational practice rather than universally embedded university standards. [[4]](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf)

A university that wants best practice should not start with a generic “AI policy” alone. It should build a governance stack: approved enterprise tools and data rules, procurement review using HECVAT and AI-specific rubrics, a risk-management layer aligned to NIST and privacy law, LMS and identity integration through 1EdTech standards, assessment redesign at program level instead of detector-driven enforcement, and continuous evaluation using red-teaming, privacy impact assessment, and human oversight. [[5]](https://policy.usc.edu/generative-ai-general-policy/)

## Scope and method

Because the geographic scope was unspecified, this report uses a global but English-language evidence base, emphasizing authorities and standards with visible influence across UNESCO-linked global policy, the U.S., the U.K., the EU, and Australia. The report prioritizes primary and official sources: university profiles, regulator guidance, standard-setting bodies, official framework documents, and original papers. Where citation counts are included, they come from public Google Scholar profile snippets surfaced by search results and should be read as approximate, time-sensitive indicators rather than fixed audit values. [[6]](https://scholar.google.com/citations?hl=en&user=0KzFF40AAAAJ&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The ranking of individuals below is an analytical synthesis, not a universal league table. I weighted four factors: relevance of output to GenAI in higher education, scholarly impact, direct role in shaping standards or policy, and visible institutional or public uptake. This method matters because the GenAI higher-ed field is unusually hybrid: a highly cited scholar without institutional uptake is less authoritative in practice than someone whose work directly shaped regulator guidance, procurement practice, or assessment design. That is why the report treats policy roles and adoption evidence as seriously as citations. [[7]](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

## Gaps, contested areas, and emerging standards

The most contested area is assessment. The field now broadly agrees that traditional written take-home tasks are vulnerable, but there is no universal standard for how much GenAI assistance should be allowed, how use should be disclosed, or how evidence of learning should be collected. TEQSA’s 2025 resource is especially important because it explicitly says institutions now cluster around three approaches: program-wide reform, unit-level assurance of learning, or combinations of both. That is a sign of convergence around principles, but not yet a settled technical or policy standard. [[41]](https://www.teqsa.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-09/enacting-assessment-reform-in-a-time-of-artificial-intelligence.pdf)

A second contested area is AI detection. TEQSA’s 2025 guidance states that detecting GenAI use with certainty in assessments is, for now, “all but impossible,” which is one of the most consequential official acknowledgments in the sector. This pushes universities away from detector-centric enforcement and toward assessment redesign, supervised performance, and transparent use policies. In practice, that is why postplagiarism frameworks and authentic-assessment models are gaining traction. [[42]](https://www.teqsa.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-09/enacting-assessment-reform-in-a-time-of-artificial-intelligence.pdf)

A third gap is privacy and data governance for LLM deployment. Universities increasingly distinguish between public consumer AI tools and contract-protected enterprise tools. USC, for example, explicitly permits some categories of internal data only in university enterprise AI tools, while restricting confidential and regulated data unless additional approval exists; Clemson similarly directs users to prefer university-approved, contract-protected services and to ground decisions in data classification. This is emerging as a cross-institutional norm, but the exact controls differ from campus to campus. [[43]](https://policy.usc.edu/generative-ai-general-policy/)

A fourth gap is higher-ed-specific evaluation capability. NIST, EDPB, HELM, and OWASP all provide pieces of the validation puzzle: red-teaming, privacy-risk assessment, transparency benchmarking, and application security. But few universities have translated those into stable internal operating procedures for teaching tools, student-facing assistants, or agentic systems. Many institutions are still relying on vendor claims, generic cybersecurity reviews, or ad hoc faculty experimentation rather than a repeatable TEVV process. [[44]](https://ai-challenges.nist.gov/uassets/6)

A final emerging area is AI literacy as an enforceable governance expectation. The EU AI Act introduced AI literacy obligations, and these are now active ahead of full application of the broader framework. That matters for universities because staff and students are not only users of AI; in many cases universities are also deployers, procurers, or co-developers. AI literacy therefore stops being just a curricular aspiration and becomes part of institutional compliance and operational readiness. [[45]](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

## Recommendations for a university seeking best practice

A university seeking best practice should adopt a layered governance model rather than a single stand-alone AI policy. The first step is to distinguish **public tools**, **enterprise-approved tools**, and **restricted-use cases**. USC and Clemson already provide strong, practical examples of this approach: use data classification to decide what can enter a model; prefer institutionally reviewed services for university work; route exceptions through privacy, legal, cybersecurity, and procurement review. That approach is more sustainable than trying to write a single rule for every classroom and department. [[43]](https://policy.usc.edu/generative-ai-general-policy/)

The second step is to hardwire procurement and vendor review. For U.S.-style procurement, HECVAT should be the default baseline, supplemented by AI-specific requirements drawn from the 1EdTech Generative AI Data Rubric and NIST-style risk questions. The University of California AI Council guide is especially useful because it translates abstract AI governance into concrete supplier questions about governance, third-party assessments, bias testing, transparency, retention, and ongoing monitoring. [[46]](https://www.educause.edu/higher-education-community-vendor-assessment-toolkit)

The third step is to standardize integration around the existing edtech stack. If an AI-enabled product will touch teaching and learning, it should, wherever possible, enter the ecosystem through 1EdTech-compatible channels, particularly LTI 1.3 and the associated grade, roster, and deep-linking services. That reduces security drift, simplifies account provisioning, and makes governance enforceable through known LMS workflows rather than uncontrolled browser-based copy-paste behavior. [[47]](https://www.1edtech.org/standards/lti)

The fourth step is to redesign assessment at program level. TEQSA’s work strongly supports moving beyond detector-based integrity models toward a mix of permitted AI use, AI-irrelevant assessments, and supervised demonstrations of learning. The AI Assessment Scale can be used as a practical task-level taxonomy, but it works best inside a larger program-level assessment strategy rather than as a one-off classroom label. Russell Group principles are useful as an institutional values statement, but they need to be connected to redesigned assessment evidence trails, disclosure expectations, and moderation practices. [[48]](https://www.teqsa.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-09/enacting-assessment-reform-in-a-time-of-artificial-intelligence.pdf)

The fifth step is to establish an evaluation and monitoring protocol for GenAI systems already in use. At minimum, that protocol should include: privacy-risk assessment; red-teaming for harmful, biased, or unsafe outputs; human review of domain accuracy; documentation of approved use cases; and periodic re-evaluation when models or vendor terms change. NIST, EDPB, and OWASP together provide a strong starting point. HELM is especially useful when the university needs a vocabulary for transparency and benchmark coverage, even if it is not directly deployed as a campus-wide tool. [[49]](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2025-04/ai-privacy-risks-and-mitigations-in-llms.pdf)

The sixth step is to treat AI literacy as both curricular and operational. The most mature institutions now train faculty, academic support staff, legal and procurement teams, and students differently, because they face different risks and different responsibilities. Jisc’s maturity model is a helpful organizing framework here: it pushes institutions to move from experimentation to operationalization and then to embedded lifecycle management. [[39]](https://www.jisc.ac.uk/ai-maturity-toolkit-for-tertiary-education)

In 2026, that means using legal/privacy controls as the floor, HECVAT and LTI as the operational spine, NIST as the governance logic, TEQSA/Russell Group/AIAS-style frameworks for assessment, and UNESCO/Jisc for the broader institutional values and capability agenda.
