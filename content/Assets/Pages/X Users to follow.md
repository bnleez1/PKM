The people below still sit at the intersection of **AI, ELT, teacher development, and the future of teaching**, and they are worth following because they share practice, not just slogans. Engagement on individual posts varies; the better move is to reply to a specific claim with a classroom detail. 

---

### 1. Joe Dale — [@joedale](https://x.com/joedale)
Languages + tech consultant; AI project lead. Regularly writes up webinars and classroom workflows.

**Recent themes:** co-creating one idea with AI into a lesson, song, dialogue, and worksheet while keeping the teacher in control; ThingLink + AI for interactive language tasks; pedagogy before tools / student agency.

**How to join in:** Reply with one activity you adapted and where the AI draft failed (level, register, cultural fit). Ask how he would constrain the prompt for *your* exam class or low-tech room.

---

### 2. Larry Ferlazzo — [@Larryferlazzo](https://x.com/Larryferlazzo)
Long-time high-school ELL teacher and Education Week columnist. Still posts daily teaching resources.

**Recent themes:** AI literacy units and classroom videos; “post-shame” AI writing and what that means for student work; practical ELL/content resources tied to current events.

**How to join in:** Don’t praise the link dump. Pick one resource and say how you’d scaffold it for newcomers vs. long-term ELLs, or what you’d cut.

### 3. Dan Fitzpatrick — [@theaieducatorx](https://x.com/theaieducatorx)
Runs The AI Educator; school-facing AI policy, tools, and summits.

**Recent themes:** whether schools can trust vendor AI; safety/privacy standards with unions and Microsoft; back-to-school AI updates that put students in charge of their learning.

**How to join in:** Ask what a *language* department should demand that a generic school AI policy usually misses (L2 output quality, accent bias, translation dependence).

### 4. Ethan Mollick — [@emollick](https://x.com/emollick)
Wharton professor; the highest-signal “AI at work and school” account. Not ELT-specific, but educators constantly cite him.

**Recent themes:** experiments with models as tutors/coworkers; what assignments still make humans think; evidence vs. hype.

**How to join in:** Translate one of his workplace findings into an ELT problem: “If AI drafts the essay, what should the *speaking* or *revision* task become?”

## How to contribute without sounding promotional

Use the same move every time:

1. Quote one concrete line, not the whole post.  
2. Add *your* constraint (A2 teens, 45 minutes, no phones, high-stakes exam, multilingual staffroom).  
3. Report what you tried, including the failure.  
4. Ask one question they can answer in two sentences.  
5. Leave your course, newsletter, or tool out of the first two replies.

Example:

> “I tried the ‘one idea → dialogue + worksheet’ workflow with B1 adults. The dialogue was fluent but used *would have been* in a unit that only needed past simple. How do you lock CEFR in the first prompt without killing naturalness?”

That reads like a colleague, not a launch.
