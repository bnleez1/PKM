# Interlocutor

## 1. Expanded Context

While a dictionary might define an interlocutor simply as "someone involved in a conversation," in Discourse Analysis, the term carries significantly more weight. It represents the fundamental idea that language is deeply collaborative. Meaning does not exist solely in the brain of the speaker, nor in the ear of the listener; it is actively co-constructed in the space *between* them.

Drawing on the dialogic theories of Mikhail Bakhtin, every utterance we make is shaped by the person we are speaking to—our interlocutor. We anticipate their reactions, adjust our vocabulary to their perceived knowledge, and read their subtle cues to guide our next words. In language education, shifting the focus from the "speaker" to the "interlocutors" changes everything. It moves our pedagogy away from viewing language as a solo performance (like reciting a monologue) and toward treating it as a dynamic, interactive dance. Making this concept transparent to our community of learners helps them realize that successful communication is a shared responsibility, instantly reducing the anxiety of the solo "spotlight."

## 2. Common Misconceptions

- **Misconception: "Interlocutor" is just a fancy, academic synonym for "listener" or "partner."**

- *The Correction:* Calling someone a "partner" usually just describes a classroom management arrangement (e.g., "work with a partner"). Calling them an "interlocutor" emphasizes their active, linguistic role in shaping the discourse. Furthermore, an interlocutor is never *just* a listener; even in silence, their presence, body language, and backchanneling actively dictate how the speaker constructs their message.

- **Misconception: Only the person currently speaking is responsible for the discourse.**

- *The Correction:* Trainees often evaluate student speaking performance by only looking at the person holding the floor. In reality, a breakdown in communication is often the result of an unsupportive interlocutor who fails to provide the necessary conversational scaffolding (like asking clarifying questions or nodding).

## 3. Strategies for Analysis

When training educators to analyze classroom discourse or authentic transcripts, use this framework to evaluate the "Interlocutor Effect." Have trainees ask these three questions:

1. **Analyze Accommodation:** How does Speaker A shift their language based on Interlocutor B? Look for convergence (adapting to sound more like the interlocutor to build rapport) or divergence (emphasizing differences to establish authority or distance).

2. **Evaluate Backchanneling:** What is the "non-speaking" interlocutor doing? Have trainees circle all the instances of *uh-huh*, *right*, *yeah*, or mark physical nods in a video. How do these micro-signals propel the speaker forward?

3. **Trace the Negotiation of Meaning:** Find a moment of confusion in the transcript. Who initiates the repair? Does the interlocutor ask for clarification, or does the speaker anticipate the confusion and rephrase?

## 4. In-Practice Classroom Examples

Imagine a speaking task where a B2-level English learner is asked to describe a complex hobby, like building custom computers.

First, they describe it to the teacher. Because the teacher is an "expert" interlocutor with evaluative power, the student speaks carefully, using stiff, formal vocabulary, pausing frequently to monitor their own grammar, and taking very few risks. The discourse is rigid.

Next, the teacher asks the student to explain the exact same hobby to a peer who knows nothing about computers. Suddenly, the student's language transforms. Because the peer is a "novice" interlocutor, the student naturally begins using analogies, checking for understanding (*"You know how a car engine works? It's like that..."*), and using a warmer, more animated tone. By explicitly pointing out this shift to the students, the teacher transparently demonstrates how their language choices are powerfully dictated by who is sitting across from them.
