# Academic Writing (Literature Review) Rubric


This writing rubric was designed for [[Academic Writing]].

[[UAA_Lit_Review_Rubric_v4|UAA_Lit_Review_Rubric_v4 - Notes]]

## Problem Framing & Rationale (10 pts)

This criterion evaluates how effectively the writer introduces the "so what?" of their project. In applied linguistics and English Language Teaching (ELT), it is not enough to simply choose a topic; the writer must articulate a specific, meaningful problem or tension that currently exists in the field.

- **The "Hook" and the "Need":** An exemplary paper doesn't just state a topic (e.g., "I am writing about vocabulary acquisition"); it identifies a specific challenge or underexplored area (e.g., "While extensive reading improves vocabulary, the impact of digital, gamified reading logs on incidental acquisition remains unclear").

- **Significance:** The rationale must explicitly connect to the broader ELT community. How will exploring this problem benefit teachers, learners, or curriculum designers? The literature must be used from the very beginning to prove that this problem is real and worth investigating.

## Literature Synthesis & Theoretical Framework (15 pts)

This assesses the writer's ability to map the existing academic landscape. The crucial distinction here is between *synthesizing* and *summarizing*.

- **Thematic Synthesis vs. Annotated Bibliography:** A developing writer will often list sources sequentially ("Author A said X. Author B said Y. Author C said Z"). An exemplary writer groups sources thematically ("Regarding digital reading, Authors A and B highlight motivation, whereas Author C points out cognitive overload"). It is about putting the researchers into a conversation with one another.

- **Theoretical Framework:** This is the conceptual lens through which the student will view their future research. Whether it's Vygotsky’s Sociocultural Theory, Krashen’s Input Hypothesis, or a specific model of communicative competence, the writer must clearly define the theory that will anchor their upcoming methodology and data analysis.

## Critical Evaluation of Sources (10 pts)

This criterion pushes learners to transition from being passive consumers of information to active, critical participants in the academic discourse.

- **Beyond Face Value:** It is not enough to simply report what a study found. The writer must evaluate *how* the study found it.

- **Identifying Limitations:** Does a cited study have a small sample size? Was it conducted in an ESL context that might not apply to a local EFL context? Are there potential biases in the author's methodology? A strong literature review points out these weaknesses to justify why new, better-designed research (the student's future project) is necessary.

## Organization & Cohesion (40 pts)

As the most heavily weighted criterion, this reflects the essential architecture of a compelling academic argument. If the reader gets lost, the research loses its impact.

- **Macro-Structure (The Forest):** The literature review should resemble a funnel. It must start with broad concepts and progressively narrow down to the specific gap the student intends to fill. The thematic sections must follow a logical, sequential order.

- **Micro-Structure (The Trees):** This evaluates paragraph-level cohesion. Does the first sentence of a paragraph clearly state its main idea? Do transition words (e.g., *furthermore*, *conversely*, *nevertheless*) seamlessly guide the reader from one thought to the next without feeling abrupt or disjointed?

## Academic Writing Quality (15 pts)

This focuses on the mechanics, tone, and clarity of the expression. The goal is to write with authority and precision.

- **Discipline-Appropriate Register:** The language should be formal, objective, and scholarly. This means avoiding overly colloquial language, emotional appeals, or sweeping generalizations (e.g., changing "Teachers always hate using textbooks" to "Research indicates that some educators express resistance to strictly mandated textbook usage").

- **Clarity and Conciseness:** Complex academic ideas should be communicated as clearly as possible. Puffy, overly convoluted sentences that obscure the meaning will cost points here. Grammar, syntax, and punctuation fall under this umbrella.

## APA Style & Academic Integrity (10 pts)

This evaluates the strict adherence to the mechanical rules of scholarly attribution, which is the bedrock of academic trust.

- **Formatting and Mechanics:** This includes the correct use of APA 7th edition guidelines for in-text citations (narrative and parenthetical), formatting the references page, and handling direct quotes versus paraphrasing.

- **Academic Integrity:** This is where the originality score comes into play. It ensures ethical source use. It penalizes patchwriting (heavily borrowing sentence structures from a source and just swapping out a few synonyms) and ensures that the student is genuinely generating their own synthesis of the material.
