# Types of Talk
## 1. Interactional Talk

Interactional talk primarily manages **relationships**.

Common examples include:

- greetings and leave-taking;
- small talk;
- personal updates;
- compliments;
- joking;
- supportive responses;
- showing interest;
- thanking and apologizing.

Example:

> A: How did your presentation go?  
> B: Pretty well, but I was nervous.  
> A: I know that feeling. You looked confident, though.  
> B: Thanks.

Although information is exchanged, the main work is interpersonal: showing interest and support.

Typical features include backchannels, personal questions, assessments, laughter, reciprocal disclosure, and flexible topic development.

## 2. Transactional Talk

Transactional talk primarily manages **information or tasks**.

Examples include:

- asking for directions;
- requesting information;
- arranging appointments;
- giving instructions;
- completing purchases;
- solving problems;
- confirming details.

Example:

> A: What time does the office close?  
> B: Five o'clock.  
> A: Can I submit the form here?  
> B: Yes, at the front desk.

Here, success depends mainly on accurate and relevant information.

Transactional talk often includes clarification, confirmation, repetition, explicit reference, sequencing, and summaries.

## 3. Conversations Can Shift Orientation

Most conversations contain both orientations.

> A: Hi! How's your week going?  
> B: Busy, but okay.  
> A: What can I help you with?  
> B: Could I submit my paper on Monday instead of Friday?  
> A: Yes. Send it before noon.  
> B: Thank you. I appreciate it.

**Turns 1–2:** interactional  
**Turns 3–5:** transactional  
**Turn 6:** interactional closing

A strong analysis therefore identifies **where the orientation shifts** rather than labeling the entire conversation once.

## 4. Connecting Modules 7, 8, and 9

Use three questions when analyzing interaction.

### Module 7 — Speech Action

**What is the speaker doing?**

Identify:

- locution;
- illocutionary force;
- direct or indirect speech act;
- uptake.

### Module 8 — Sequence

**How is the action organized conversationally?**

Identify:

- adjacency pairs;
- expected responses;
- turn-taking;
- preference organization;
- pauses or overlap;
- repair.

### Module 9 — Orientation and Success

**What larger purpose are participants pursuing, and does the interaction succeed?**

Ask whether speakers are primarily managing:

- a relationship;
- a task;
- or both.

## 5. Conversational Breakdown

A **conversational breakdown** occurs when participants display difficulty establishing or maintaining shared understanding.

Consider:

> A: Could you open the window?  
> B: Yes, I can.

Both sentences are grammatically correct, but B treats the utterance as a question about ability rather than as a request.

Possible causes of breakdown include:

- speech-act misinterpretation;
- unclear reference;
- incorrect assumptions about shared knowledge;
- unexpected responses;
- orientation mismatch;
- turn-taking difficulty;
- failure to recognize or initiate repair.

## 6. Pragmatic Failure

Pragmatic failure is not simply grammatical error.

A grammatically correct utterance may produce an unintended interpersonal effect.

> Student: Give me your notes.

The message is clear, but depending on the relationship and context, the imperative may be interpreted as overly demanding.

Pragmatic failure may involve misunderstanding:

- the intended speech act;
- participant roles;
- directness;
- entitlement;
- politeness expectations;
- appropriate amounts of information.

Avoid automatically labeling speakers as *rude* or *inappropriate*. Describe the linguistic and interactional evidence first.

## 7. Repair

Conversational trouble does not automatically mean communication has failed.

> A: I'll meet you at the bank.  
> B: The bank downtown?  
> A: No, the one across from campus.  
> B: Got it.

B initiates repair, and A resolves the problem.

Repair strategies include:

- clarification requests;
- confirmation checks;
- repetition;
- reformulation;
- self-correction.

The ability to repair trouble is part of communicative competence.

## 8. Analysis Framework

When analyzing a problematic exchange, ask:

1. **Context:** Who are the participants and what are they trying to accomplish?
2. **Orientation:** Is the talk interactional, transactional, or mixed?
3. **Speech act:** What action is being performed?
4. **Sequence:** What response does the turn make relevant?
5. **Uptake:** How does the listener appear to interpret the action?
6. **Trouble:** Where does misunderstanding or mismatch become visible?
7. **Repair:** How do participants attempt to restore understanding?
8. **Outcome:** Is the task completed and/or the relationship maintained?
