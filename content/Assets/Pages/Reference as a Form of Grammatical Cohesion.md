# Reference as a Form of Grammatical Cohesion

# Reference as a Form of Grammatical Cohesion

**Reference** is one of the major grammatical resources that speakers and writers use to create **cohesion** in a text. Cohesion refers to the linguistic connections that help sentences and clauses work together as a unified text rather than as a collection of unrelated statements. Reference creates these connections when a word or expression cannot be fully understood by itself and the reader or listener must look elsewhere for its meaning.

Consider the following example:

> Maria bought a new laptop yesterday. **She** needs **it** for her classes.

The words **she** and **it** depend on other information for their interpretation. *She* refers to *Maria*, while *it* refers to *a new laptop*. These relationships help connect the two sentences.

Reference can be classified in several ways. One useful distinction concerns **where the reader or listener must look to identify the meaning of the reference**. The most important categories are **anaphoric, cataphoric, and exophoric reference**. Reference can also be classified grammatically as **personal, demonstrative, and comparative reference**.

## 1. Anaphoric Reference

**Anaphoric reference** occurs when a word refers **backward** to something that has already been mentioned in the text. This is probably the most common type of reference in written discourse.

For example:

> Sarah submitted her research proposal on Monday. **She** had spent several weeks revising **it**.

Here, **she** refers backward to *Sarah*, and **it** refers backward to *her research proposal*. The reader does not need the nouns to be repeated because the pronouns create a clear connection with information that has already appeared.

Another example is:

> The students entered the classroom quietly. **They** immediately opened **their** notebooks.

Both **they** and **their** refer back to *the students*.

Anaphoric reference helps writers avoid unnecessary repetition. Without it, the text might sound awkward:

> The students entered the classroom quietly. The students immediately opened the students' notebooks.

The use of reference therefore contributes to both cohesion and naturalness.

## 2. Cataphoric Reference

**Cataphoric reference** works in the opposite direction. Instead of referring backward, the reference points **forward** to information that appears later in the text.

For example:

> When **she** finally arrived, **Professor Martínez** began the presentation.

The reader encounters *she* before discovering who *she* is. The identity of the pronoun is clarified by the later expression *Professor Martínez*.

Another example is:

> **This is what I want you to remember:** good academic writing requires revision.

The word **this** points forward to the statement *good academic writing requires revision*.

Cataphoric reference is less frequent than anaphoric reference, but it is common in introductions, storytelling, speeches, and other situations in which a writer or speaker wants to create anticipation.

For instance:

> **He** was the person everyone had been waiting for: **the new department chair**.

The pronoun *he* directs the reader forward to the expression that identifies the person.

## 3. Exophoric Reference

**Exophoric reference** occurs when the interpretation of a reference depends on something **outside the text itself**. The reader or listener must use the physical situation, shared knowledge, or broader context to understand what the expression means.

Imagine two students sitting in a classroom when one says:

> Can you give me **that**?

The word **that** might refer to a pencil, book, phone, or another object visible to the participants. The meaning cannot be determined from the sentence alone.

Similarly, a teacher might say:

> Put **your bags over there**.

The meaning of **there** depends on the teacher's physical location, gesture, or surrounding environment.

Exophoric reference is particularly common in spoken communication because speakers often share the same physical context. Gestures, eye contact, and pointing can make the intended reference obvious.

Unlike anaphoric and cataphoric reference, exophoric reference does not directly connect two pieces of language within the text. For this reason, it provides contextual meaning but contributes less directly to textual cohesion.

## 4. Endophoric Reference

The general term **endophoric reference** refers to reference relationships that occur **inside the text**. Both anaphoric and cataphoric reference are therefore types of endophoric reference.

We can represent the distinction as:

**Reference**

- **Endophoric** — meaning is found inside the text

    - Anaphoric — refers backward

    - Cataphoric — refers forward

- **Exophoric** — meaning is found outside the text

For example:

> David purchased a bicycle. **He** rides **it** to work.

Both references are endophoric because the reader can identify their meaning from the text itself. More specifically, they are anaphoric because they refer backward.

## 5. Personal Reference

Reference can also be classified according to the grammatical form being used. **Personal reference** typically involves personal pronouns, possessive pronouns, and possessive determiners.

Common examples include:

**I, me, you, he, him, she, her, it, we, us, they, them, my, your, his, our, their**

For example:

> Andrea and Luis are preparing a presentation. **They** will present **their** findings tomorrow.

**They** and **their** both refer to *Andrea and Luis*.

Personal reference is especially important for maintaining participants across a text. Once a person or group has been introduced, pronouns can be used to continue discussing them without repeating their names.

## 6. Demonstrative Reference

**Demonstrative reference** identifies something according to location, proximity, or position in the discourse.

Common demonstratives include:

**this, that, these, those, here, there**

For example:

> Several students requested additional practice. **This** surprised the instructor.

Here, **this** does not refer to a single noun. Instead, it refers to the entire preceding idea that several students requested additional practice.

Another example is:

> Two solutions were proposed. **The first** involved changing the schedule.

Expressions such as *the first*, *the second*, and *the former* can similarly help readers locate information within a text.

Demonstrative reference is particularly useful in academic writing because it allows writers to refer to previous arguments, findings, examples, or sections.

## 7. Comparative Reference

**Comparative reference** creates cohesion by indicating that one item should be interpreted in relation to another item.

Common comparative expressions include:

**same, similar, different, other, more, less, better, worse**

For example:

> Group A completed the activity in twenty minutes. Group B needed **more** time.

The word **more** can only be fully interpreted by comparing Group B's time with Group A's twenty minutes.

Similarly:

> The first experiment produced inconsistent results. A **similar** problem occurred in the second experiment.

The word **similar** encourages the reader to connect *problem* with the previously mentioned inconsistent results.

## Why Reference Matters

Reference is important because cohesive texts continually connect new information with information that is already available. Consider:

> Daniela interviewed six students about online learning. **They** described several difficulties with internet access. **These problems** were particularly serious for students living outside the city. **This** suggests that access to technology may influence participation.

The text contains several cohesive reference relationships. **They** refers to *six students*. **These problems** refers to the previously described difficulties. **This** refers to the broader finding presented in the preceding sentences.

Without these connections, a writer would need to repeat the same information constantly. Reference allows language users to build meaning across sentences efficiently.

When analyzing reference as part of discourse analysis, therefore, it is useful to ask three questions: **What is the referring expression? What does it refer to? Where must the reader or listener look to identify that meaning?** These questions make it possible to identify not only individual pronouns or demonstratives but also the larger network of grammatical connections that gives a text cohesion.
