# Loop Input

**Practicing What We Preach: Loop Input as a Tool for Transparent Teacher Training**

In the evolving landscape of English Language Teaching (ELT), the traditional models of teacher training are undergoing a necessary transformation. For decades, training sessions often suffered from a glaring paradox: trainers standing at the front of a room, delivering top-down, transmission-style lectures on the vital importance of learner-centered, communicative teaching. This dissonance between what is preached and what is practiced creates a barrier not just to learning, but to authenticity.

Modern educators increasingly recognize the need to make teaching and learning processes entirely transparent. By breaking down the walls of the traditional classroom and sharing in-person practices with the public at large, trainers can foster a more engaging, collaborative community of educators. To achieve this level of transparency, the methodologies we use to train teachers must inherently demonstrate their own value. The medium must become the message. This is where Tessa Woodward’s concept of "Loop Input" becomes an indispensable strategy in the teacher trainer’s repertoire.

**Defining the Loop: When Content Meets Process**

First introduced comprehensively in her 1991 book, *Models and Metaphors in Language Teacher Training*, and later refined in her 2003 *ELT Journal* article, Tessa Woodward defined Loop Input as a specific type of experiential teacher training. In a Loop Input session, the content of the training session is aligned precisely with the process used to deliver it.

To understand Loop Input, one must first distinguish it from basic experiential learning. In standard experiential training, a trainer might want teachers to understand what it feels like to be a language learner doing a dictation activity. The trainer gives the teachers a dictation about a generic topic—perhaps a short story or a historical fact—and then they discuss the mechanics of the activity afterward. This is valuable, but it is not a loop.

In a Loop Input session, the trainer gives the teachers a dictation activity, but the *text* being dictated is an explanation of the pedagogical benefits, stages, and potential pitfalls of running a dictation activity in the classroom. The trainees are learning about the activity by doing the activity, and the content they are processing is the theory behind the activity itself. The process and the content are inextricably linked, creating a recursive "loop" of learning.

**The Mechanics of Loop Input**

Woodward (2003) emphasizes that Loop Input operates on two distinct levels of learning. The first level is the immediate, experiential engagement with the task. The trainees are reading, writing, listening, or speaking; they are negotiating meaning and experiencing the cognitive and affective states of a language learner. The second level is the critical, reflective engagement with the content. Because the content is the methodology itself, trainees are forced to evaluate the theory while simultaneously experiencing its practical application.

Consider how this might look across various ELT methodologies:

- **Jigsaw Reading:** To teach trainees how to manage a jigsaw reading activity, the trainer divides an article about the principles and classroom management of jigsaw readings into four parts. The trainees must form expert groups, read their sections, and then regroup to piece together the complete methodology.

- **Task-Based Language Teaching (TBLT):** The trainer sets up a task where the trainees must work together to sequence a series of disordered lesson plan stages into a coherent TBLT framework. The process they go through (pre-task, task cycle, language focus) perfectly mirrors the TBLT framework they are attempting to construct.

- **Error Correction:** The trainer conducts a communicative activity with the trainees but deliberately utilizes various error correction techniques (recasting, delayed correction, peer correction) while discussing the topic of feedback.

Following the experiential phase, the loop must be closed with a robust "decompression" or reflection stage. Trainees must step out of the activity and analyze both what they learned (the methodology) and how they learned it (the experience). This dual-layered reflection is where the deepest cognitive connections are forged.

**Transparency and Community Building**

The true power of Loop Input extends far beyond clever session design; it is fundamentally about radical transparency in education. When we train using Loop Input, we completely demystify the pedagogical process. We are not hiding behind a veil of academic theory, telling teachers what they ought to do in their future classrooms. Instead, we are stepping into the arena with them, demonstrating exactly how a technique feels, functions, and occasionally fails in real time.

This transparency is vital for educators who are committed to sharing their in-person practices with the wider public. When training sessions are recorded, shared online, or opened up to a broader community of educators, traditional lectures often fall flat. They lack the dynamic, lived reality of the classroom. Loop Input, by contrast, provides highly visible, instantly recognizable models of teaching in action. When observing a Loop Input session, external educators can simultaneously grasp the theoretical framework and witness the practical classroom management required to execute it.

By making the "how" and the "why" visible at the exact same moment, trainers invite observers into a shared experience. This shared vulnerability and active demonstration naturally cultivate an engaging, supportive community of practice. Educators are drawn to trainers who are willing to "walk the walk" and expose the inner workings of their instructional design.

**Navigating the Challenges**

While deeply effective, Loop Input is not without its challenges, which Woodward (1991, 2003) readily acknowledges. The primary risk is cognitive overload. Because trainees are simultaneously processing new theoretical information and navigating the rules of a potentially unfamiliar activity, they can easily become overwhelmed.

To mitigate this, trainers must scaffold Loop Input sessions carefully. The activity type chosen for the loop should ideally be one the trainees are at least somewhat familiar with, allowing them to focus more cognitive energy on the methodological content. Alternatively, if the activity itself is entirely new, the content text should be relatively straightforward and accessible.

Furthermore, the reflection stage cannot be skipped or rushed. Without guided decompression, trainees may remember the fun or frustration of the activity without synthesizing the underlying pedagogical lessons. Trainers must guide the conversation explicitly: *How did it feel to wait for that delayed correction? Did the jigsaw format make the theory easier or harder to understand? How would your A2 learners react to this same format?*

**Conclusion**

Tessa Woodward’s Loop Input remains one of the most elegant and impactful strategies in language teacher training. It bridges the persistent gap between theory and practice, ensuring that the way we teach our teachers aligns perfectly with how we expect them to teach their students.

For the modern teacher trainer—especially one dedicated to making learning transparent and building an active, engaged community of educators—Loop Input is more than just a technique. It is a philosophy of practice. It proves that the best way to share our craft with the public at large is not to lecture about it, but to embody it, inviting everyone to step into the loop and experience the learning for themselves.
