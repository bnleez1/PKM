# Source Analysis Focus for the Annotated Bibliography

For the **annotated bibliography**, you should focus primarily on the **Source Analysis** sheet ([[Rubric Literature Review]]). That sheet aligns most closely with Unit III, where you are expected to curate at least 10 valid, reliable, and diverse sources, independently verify them, assess possible bias, and write the annotations themselves. Academic Writing Syllabus 2026

I would complete these parts of **Source Analysis** for each of their 10 sources:

|Workbook field|Why it matters for the annotated bibliography|
|---|---|
|**Source ID**|Gives each source a simple reference code such as S01, S02, etc.|
|**Full APA citation**|Becomes the citation that appears above the annotation.|
|**Purpose / research question**|Helps students explain what the study investigated.|
|**Context / population**|Identifies who or what was studied and where.|
|**Method / design**|Helps students understand how the researchers produced their evidence.|
|**Data / sample**|Provides important context for judging the findings.|
|**Key claim(s)**|Identifies the main argument or conclusion of the source.|
|**Evidence / results**|Helps students distinguish claims from supporting evidence.|
|**Limitations**|Encourages critical evaluation rather than simple summary.|
|**Quality (1–5)**|Requires students to judge the credibility of the source.|
|**Relevance (1–5)**|Requires them to explain its usefulness for their own research problem.|
|**Theme codes**|Begins connecting the source to other studies and prepares for the literature review.|
|**Gap / unresolved issue**|Helps identify what the study does not answer.|
|**How I might use it**|Forces students to articulate why the source belongs in their research project.|
|**DOI / URL**|Supports source verification.|
|**Status**|Helps track whether the source has been fully analyzed.|

### What should an annotation draw from?

Consider the following: **APA citation → Summary → Evaluation → Relevance**

The workbook fields feed those three annotation components:

**Summary** draws mainly from:  
`Purpose → Context → Method → Key claims → Evidence/results`

**Evaluation** draws mainly from:  
`Limitations → Quality → possible bias/source reliability`

**Relevance** draws mainly from:  
`Theme codes → Gap/unresolved issue → How I might use it`

That structure fits the syllabus especially well because Unit III is not simply asking you to summarize ten articles. It explicitly requires you to determine whether sources are **valid, reliable, and unbiased** and to demonstrate independent source verification and bias analysis. Academic Writing Syllabus 2026
### What should they _not_ focus on yet?

I am not asking you to **fully complete the Theme Synthesis sheet yet**. That sheet is more appropriate for Unit IV, when you begin combining evidence across sources into the actual literature review.

I would, however, begin using the **Source–Theme Matrix lightly** after about 4–5 sources. At this stage you only need to identify recurring themes and notice whether sources seem to support, extend, or contradict one another. You do not yet need to formulate polished synthesis claims.

This creates a useful progression:

**Unit III — Annotated Bibliography**  
`Source Analysis → preliminary Theme Codes → individual annotations`

↓

**Transition to Unit IV**  
`Source–Theme Matrix → compare the 10 sources`

↓

**Unit IV — Literature Review**  
`Theme Synthesis → synthesis claims → paragraphs`

That transition is especially important in your course because the syllabus requires you to use **the same sources curated in the Unit III annotated bibliography** when writing the Unit IV literature review. Academic Writing Syllabus 2026

> **For the annotated bibliography, concentrate on understanding, evaluating, and explaining each source individually. Do not try to write the literature review yet. Your theme codes will prepare you to compare and synthesize the sources later.**

Consider the Excel workbook as a genuine **bridge from Annotated Bibliography → Literature Review**, rather than as two separate assignments.

# How to complete the Theme Synthesis Section of the Excel Spreadsheet

Use **one row for each major theme or concept** that appears across several of your sources. The purpose is to move from summarizing individual studies to explaining what the studies collectively show.

- **Theme / concept:** Name the recurring idea you are examining.
- **Working definition:** Briefly explain what the theme means in your review.
- **Central synthesis question:** Write the question you want the sources to answer together.
- **Supporting source IDs:** List sources that show similar findings.
- **Contrasting source IDs:** List sources that disagree, qualify, or complicate the pattern.
- **Convergent pattern:** Explain what several sources generally agree on.
- **Divergence / contradiction:** Explain where findings differ.
- **Method / context pattern:** Note whether differences may be related to participants, setting, research method, or other conditions.
- **Boundary conditions:** Identify when or where the general pattern may not apply.
- **Gap / unresolved issue:** State what the research still does not clearly explain.
- **Synthesis claim:** Write your main conclusion based on comparing the sources.
- **Paragraph topic sentence:** Turn the synthesis claim into a sentence that could begin a literature-review paragraph.
- **Implication / future research:** Explain why the pattern or gap matters.
- **Confidence (1–5):** Rate how strongly the available evidence supports your synthesis claim.
- **Status:** Indicate whether the theme is still developing or ready to draft.

The most important field is the **Synthesis claim**. It should answer:

> **What can I conclude from these sources together that I could not conclude from one source alone?**

For example:

**Theme:** Teacher attitudes toward AI  
**Synthesis claim:** Research generally suggests that teachers develop more positive attitudes toward AI when they have practical experience with the technology and understand how it can support specific teaching goals.
