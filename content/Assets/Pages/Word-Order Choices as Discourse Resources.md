# Word-Order Choices as Discourse Resources

English is often described as a language with relatively fixed word order. In a basic declarative sentence, learners normally expect a pattern such as **subject + verb + object**: _Daniel opened the presentation_. This predictability is important because English uses word order to signal grammatical relationships. However, fixed word order does not mean that speakers and writers always have only one possible way to express the same event. English contains a number of constructions in which more than one ordering is possible, and the choice between alternatives can depend on the surrounding discourse. Celce-Murcia and Olshtain emphasize this distinction: even though English word order is strongly constrained by syntax, some local variations are motivated by context and by the way information is being managed.

From a discourse-analysis perspective, the important question is therefore not simply **“Is this sentence grammatical?”** We should also ask **“Why was this particular ordering chosen here?”** The sample text about Daniel's missing presentation demonstrates two especially useful areas for answering that question: **dative alternation** and **word-order variation with separable phrasal verbs**.

## Dative (indirect object) Alternation: Organizing Participants and Information

Some English verbs describe the transfer of something from one participant to another. With verbs such as _give_, _send_, and _hand_, the same general event can often be expressed in two patterns:

_Professor Reyes gave **Daniel** **her USB drive**._

_Professor Reyes gave **her USB drive** **to Daniel**._

The first is commonly called the **double-object construction** because both the recipient (_Daniel_) and the transferred object (_her USB drive_) follow the verb. The second uses the transferred object followed by a prepositional phrase beginning with _to_.

At the sentence level, learners may be told that these sentences have essentially the same meaning. At the discourse level, however, the alternatives are not necessarily equivalent. Celce-Murcia and Olshtain (1999) use the contrast between forms such as _gave Jim the car_ and _gave the car to Jim_ to demonstrate that the surrounding conversation influences which version sounds more natural. Their analysis connects this variation to <mark style="background:#d3f8b6">the management of **old and new information**, with newer information tending to occur later in the clause.</mark>

The principle helps explain the wording in the presentation text (located at [[DA Module 6_Positioning the Writer and Reader]]). Consider:

_Professor Reyes **gave Daniel her USB drive**._

Daniel has already been introduced as the student experiencing the problem. He is therefore highly accessible in the discourse. The USB drive, however, is being introduced at this point. Placing _her USB drive_ toward the end allows the new information to arrive after the already established participant.

Now compare:

_Another student found the file in the shared course folder and **sent the file to Daniel**._

Here the surrounding discourse is concerned with the missing file. The file is already a central topic, while the endpoint of the transfer becomes important in explaining how Daniel recovered it. The _to_-construction provides another way of packaging those participants.

The point is not that one construction automatically means “new information” and the other “old information.” Context must be examined. Instead, students should notice that grammar offers alternatives and that speakers select among them while constructing a discourse. This is why examining isolated sentences alone can hide important patterns of language use.

## Separable Phrasal Verbs and Information Status
> [!note] Note
> For more, see [[The world of phrasal verbs A unique challenge in English]] and [[Mastering English Particles From Phrasal Verbs to Negation]].

The second major type of word-order choice in the sample involves **separable phrasal verbs**. These contain a verb and a particle that can sometimes be separated by the direct object:

_Daniel **took out the slide**._

_Daniel **took the slide out**._

Both possibilities can be grammatical when the direct object is a lexical noun phrase such as _the slide_. Yet Celce-Murcia and Olshtain (1999) argue that these alternatives can perform different discourse functions. Drawing on earlier discourse research, they describe a three-way distinction involving a new or emphasized object, an intermediate case, and an already established object represented by a pronoun.

Their model can be illustrated with _take out_:

**take out the slide** → the object can receive relatively strong informational prominence

**take the slide out** → the lexical object is positioned between the verb and particle

**take it out** → the pronoun signals that the referent is already highly accessible

The sample deliberately moves through this pattern. First we read:

_Daniel realized that **one slide contained outdated information**. He quickly **took the slide out** of the presentation._

Because _the slide_ has just been established, the definite noun phrase refers back to information already available. Later the text says:

_His partner noticed another unnecessary example and told him to **take it out** as well._

At this point, _it_ can be used because the relevant object has just been mentioned and is easily recoverable. The pronoun is not simply a shorter substitute for a noun phrase. Its use signals **givenness and topicality**: the writer assumes that the reader already knows what _it_ refers to. Celce-Murcia and Olshtain similarly show that pronominalization and particle placement work together in signaling information status.

The same principle appears near the end of the text:

_“Next time, **send me the final version** before class.”_

_Daniel agreed and immediately **sent it to her**._

The first sentence introduces _the final version_ as the important object of the instruction. In the second, the pronoun _it_ shows that this object is now established. Meanwhile, _her_ identifies the recipient without unnecessarily repeating _Professor Reyes_. Word order, reference, and information status therefore work together.

## From Grammar Rules to Discourse Choices

For discourse analysis, the central lesson is that grammatical alternatives should not always be treated as freely interchangeable structures. A sentence-level exercise might ask students to transform _The professor gave Daniel the drive_ into _The professor gave the drive to Daniel_. A discourse-level activity asks something more useful: **Which version fits this context better, and what information does the speaker assume is already known?**

The same shift can be applied to phrasal verbs. Instead of merely practicing _take out the slide / take the slide out / take it out_, learners can examine the preceding sentences and determine why one form is appropriate at a particular moment.

Word-order analysis therefore connects grammar with **information management**. Speakers and writers continually make judgments about what their audience already knows, what needs to be introduced, what deserves emphasis, and which referents are currently accessible. English syntax limits the available choices, but where alternatives exist, discourse helps explain why one pattern may be preferred over another. As Celce-Murcia and Olshtain demonstrate, studying these choices in connected language reveals something that isolated grammar exercises often cannot: **grammar is not only a system for constructing correct sentences; it is also a resource for organizing meaning across discourse.**
