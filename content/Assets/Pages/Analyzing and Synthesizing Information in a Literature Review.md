# Analyzing and Synthesizing Information in a Literature Review

A literature review is more than a collection of summaries of previous studies. Its purpose is to examine existing knowledge critically and then combine evidence from multiple sources to develop a coherent understanding of a topic. Two central processes make this possible: **analysis** and **synthesis**. Although the terms are sometimes used interchangeably, they represent different intellectual activities. Analysis focuses primarily on understanding and evaluating individual sources, whereas synthesis focuses on identifying relationships, patterns, tensions, and implications across multiple sources (Grant & Booth, 2009; Snyder, 2019).

## Analyzing Information

Analysis can be understood as a **within-source process**. The researcher examines a source by breaking it into meaningful components and determining how those components contribute to the source's overall argument. Critical literature reviewing therefore involves more than identifying a study's topic or recording its conclusions. Researchers should examine the purpose of the study, theoretical framework, research questions, methodology, evidence, findings, assumptions, limitations, and relevance to the research problem being investigated (Hart, 2025).

A useful analysis begins by identifying the central claim of a source and determining how the author supports that claim. Researchers should distinguish between what a study **claims**, what evidence it **provides**, and how convincingly the evidence supports the conclusions. This distinction is important because published conclusions should not automatically be treated as established facts. Research quality depends partly on how appropriately the research design, data, and interpretation correspond to the question being investigated (Fink, 2014).

For example, a study may conclude that a particular instructional strategy improves student learning. Analysis requires the reviewer to look beyond that conclusion. The reviewer might examine who participated in the study, how learning was measured, how long the intervention lasted, whether a comparison group existed, and whether alternative explanations were addressed. These details help determine how much weight the study should receive in a literature review.

Analysis should also consider the broader purpose of the review. Literature reviews can differ according to their focus, goals, perspective, coverage, organization, and intended audience (Cooper, 1988). Consequently, researchers do not necessarily extract the same information from every source. The information selected for analysis should be connected to the research question guiding the literature review.

Structured note-taking can make this process more systematic. Researchers can record comparable information for every source, including research purpose, context, participants, methodology, theoretical framework, findings, limitations, and relevance. Using consistent categories makes it easier to compare studies later and reduces the tendency to rely on vague impressions or isolated quotations (Fink, 2014; Okoli, 2015).

## Synthesizing Information

Synthesis shifts attention from individual sources to **relationships across sources**. Instead of asking, "What does this article say?" the researcher asks, "What becomes visible when these sources are considered together?"

A strong literature review is generally organized around concepts, themes, questions, or debates rather than around individual authors. Organizing a review source by source often produces a sequence of summaries rather than an integrated argument. A concept-centered structure makes it easier to compare evidence, identify patterns, and reveal gaps in existing research (Webster & Watson, 2002).

Synthesis may reveal several types of relationships. Multiple studies may **converge** on a similar conclusion. One study may **extend** another by examining a different population or context. Studies may **contradict** one another, suggesting that findings depend on methodological or contextual differences. Research may also reveal **boundary conditions**, meaning that a claim appears valid only under certain circumstances.

Differences among studies should not simply be reported; they should be interpreted. For example, conflicting findings might result from differences in participants, research methods, definitions, measurement instruments, settings, or time periods. Identifying these relationships transforms a review from a descriptive report into an analytical account of what is currently known and what remains uncertain (Booth et al., 2016).

Integrative literature reviewing goes even further. Synthesis can result in new conceptual frameworks, classifications, models, or perspectives that are not contained completely within any single source. In this sense, synthesis is productive rather than merely descriptive because it can generate new ways of understanding an existing body of research (Torraco, 2005).

The appropriate form of synthesis also depends on the purpose and design of the review. Systematic, semi-systematic, and integrative reviews may require different approaches to collecting, evaluating, and combining evidence (Snyder, 2019). Researchers should therefore select synthesis procedures that correspond to their research questions and the kinds of evidence available.

## How Analysis and Synthesis Work Together

Analysis and synthesis are distinct, but they are interdependent. Reliable synthesis requires careful analysis first. Researchers cannot reasonably conclude that studies agree, contradict one another, or reveal a research gap without first understanding how each study was conducted and what its evidence actually demonstrates.

For instance, two studies may initially appear contradictory. Closer analysis may reveal that one investigated novice teachers while the other examined experienced teachers. Alternatively, one study may have measured short-term performance while another examined long-term development. What initially appears to be disagreement may therefore represent different conditions rather than genuinely conflicting evidence.

Systematic review procedures help researchers make such comparisons visible. Literature-review methodologies commonly separate activities such as searching, appraisal, synthesis, and analysis because each serves a different purpose in building an evidence-based review (Grant & Booth, 2009). Maintaining structured records of individual sources helps researchers move more reliably from analysis to cross-source comparison (Okoli, 2015).

## Moving From Sources to a Synthesis Matrix

A synthesis matrix can provide a practical bridge between reading sources and writing the literature review. Instead of organizing notes only by article, researchers can create columns representing themes, concepts, variables, methods, populations, or controversies. Individual studies can then be compared across these categories.

The matrix allows researchers to identify which sources support similar claims, which extend previous findings, which contradict others, and which address areas neglected in the existing literature. This approach encourages concept-centered rather than author-centered writing (Webster & Watson, 2002).

The final paragraph should therefore begin with a **synthesis claim**, not with the name of a researcher. Evidence from several sources can then be used to support, qualify, or complicate that claim. The writer's responsibility is to explain how the sources relate to one another and why those relationships matter.

A useful distinction can therefore guide the entire literature-review process:

**Analysis asks:** _What does this source contribute, how was that contribution produced, and how credible and relevant is it?_

**Synthesis asks:** _What can be concluded from the relationship among several sources that could not be concluded from any single source alone?_

￼## What It Means to Synthesize Information

Synthesis is primarily an **across-source activity**. Instead of asking what one source says, the reviewer asks what becomes visible when several sources are compared. Webster and Watson argue for concept-centric rather than author-centric literature reviews. This distinction is crucial. An author-centric paragraph moves from Smith to García to Chen, giving each researcher a turn. A concept-centric paragraph begins with an idea, pattern, controversy, or relationship and uses multiple sources as evidence.

Torraco pushes synthesis further in the integrative review: the reviewer should not merely combine findings but use critique and integration to generate new perspectives, frameworks, or conceptualizations. Snyder similarly emphasizes that review methods should be chosen according to the review’s purpose; systematic, semi-systematic, and integrative reviews synthesize evidence differently. Booth, Sutton, and Papaioannou likewise distinguish methods for synthesizing qualitative and quantitative evidence and stress that synthesis procedures should match the nature of the evidence base. Synthesis is therefore not one technique but a family of strategies for constructing higher-level understanding.

A synthesis might reveal **convergence**: several studies using different methods reach similar conclusions. It might reveal **divergence**: quantitative studies report a positive association while qualitative studies show important contextual exceptions. It might identify a **boundary condition**: an intervention appears effective for experienced teachers but not novices. It might expose a **methodological pattern**: contradictory findings align with different measures or time frames. Or it might reveal a **gap**: researchers have repeatedly studied outcomes while neglecting the process that produces those outcomes.

￼## Why Analysis Must Precede Synthesis

Good synthesis depends on good analysis. A reviewer cannot responsibly claim that studies agree or disagree without first establishing what each study actually examined, how concepts were operationalized, and how strong the evidence is. Two papers may appear contradictory yet investigate different populations, use different definitions, or measure outcomes at different times. Analysis discovers these differences; synthesis explains their significance.

This is why structured extraction is valuable. Fink recommends systematic procedures for evaluating quality, extracting information, and recording results, while Okoli emphasizes transparency and rigor across the full review process. Grant and Booth’s **SALSA framework—Search, Appraisal, Synthesis, and Analysis—**also highlights that a review contains distinct but connected methodological functions. The practical lesson is that notes should capture comparable information across studies. Consistent fields make patterns easier to see.

￼## Moving from a Matrix to a Literature-Review Paragraph

A synthesis matrix provides a bridge from reading notes to prose. Begin by assigning theme codes to sources. Then compare sources within each theme using questions such as: Which sources support the same claim? Which extend it? Which contradict it? Are differences associated with method, population, setting, theory, or measurement? Which evidence is strongest? What remains unexplained?

The written paragraph should then begin with the reviewer’s synthesis claim, not with an author. For instance, a paragraph might argue that “research generally links formative feedback with improved revision, but the effect depends on learners’ ability to interpret and act on feedback.” Several studies can then provide converging evidence, followed by studies that qualify the pattern. The paragraph closes by explaining the implication: perhaps feedback quality alone is insufficient unless learners possess feedback literacy.

This structure changes the reviewer’s role. The literature is no longer a collection of reports to summarize; it becomes **evidence to reason with**.
