# L - Module 2: Following the Rhythm of Everyday Life


⚡️ Daily Routines, Time Expressions, and Listening for Gist and Detail

> Daily routines are familiar, but routine descriptions can still be difficult to understand because they contain times, sequences, frequency expressions, and many similar actions. A listener may recognize individual words such as breakfast, class, work, or exercise yet lose the order of events. This chapter develops a two-stage approach: listen first for the general pattern of a person’s day and then listen selectively for exact times, activities, and frequency. You will also use titles, images, and context to predict likely vocabulary before listening. By the end of the week, your notes should show not only isolated details but also how those details fit together into a meaningful daily schedule.


## Essential understanding(s)


- How can listeners move from understanding isolated words to understanding a complete routine?

- When is an exact detail necessary, and when is the general pattern enough?

## Guiding Questions


- Which words and images help predict the stages of a daily routine?

- How do time expressions and frequency adverbs organize what a speaker says?

- What note format best represents a sequence of activities?

- How can repeated listening resolve confusion about times and actions?

## Learning outcomes

- Predict likely content and vocabulary from a title, image, and listening context.

- Identify the general pattern of a speaker’s daily routine.

- Locate specific times, dates, activities, and frequency expressions.

- Organize information in a timeline or schedule and verify it after repeated listening.

- Document any authorized pre-listening GenAI use and complete CAADI Worksheet 2.

## Key concepts and vocabulary

**daily routine** • **gist** • **timeline** • **sequence** • **frequency** • **usually** • **sometimes** • **before** • **after** • **then** • **at** • **around** • **schedule** • **prediction check**

## Main chapter content

### From familiar topic to organized meaning

A familiar topic does not automatically produce easy listening. In a routine, many events sound predictable, so listeners may hear what they expect instead of what the speaker actually says. The goal is to combine prediction with careful verification. You may predict that a university student gets up, travels to campus, attends classes, studies, eats, and relaxes. These expectations prepare useful vocabulary, but the audio may include a different order, an unusual schedule, or a contrast such as “I usually study in the morning, but on Fridays I study at night.” Effective listening keeps predictions flexible.

### First listening: capture the shape of the day

During the first listening, avoid writing every time you hear. Ask what kind of day the speaker describes. Is it a weekday or weekend? Is the person busy, relaxed, or changing a routine? Which parts of the day receive the most attention? A general statement might be: “The speaker describes a busy weekday that begins early and ends with homework.” This gist creates a structure for later details.

Listen for organizing expressions. Words such as first, then, after that, before class, in the afternoon, and finally signal movement through time. Frequency expressions—always, usually, often, sometimes, hardly ever, and never—show whether an activity is typical or exceptional. These words can be more important than one exact clock time because they explain the pattern of behavior.

### Second listening: select details with a purpose

Before listening again, decide what information is required. If the task asks for a schedule, create three columns: Time, Activity, and Frequency or Comment. Write short entries rather than sentences. For example: “6:30—gets up—weekdays” or “after lunch—library—usually.” This format prevents notes from becoming a transcript and allows you to see gaps.

Times require attention to pronunciation and context. “Seven fifteen” and “seven fifty” are easy to confuse. “A quarter past seven” and “seven fifteen” express the same time. The speaker may also say around eight, just before nine, or at about noon. These expressions communicate approximate time. Do not force an exact answer when the speaker is approximate. Dates and days can also guide interpretation: “on Mondays,” “every other day,” or “twice a week” tells you how a routine changes.

### Checking contradictions and revisions

After the second listening, inspect your notes for contradictions. Perhaps you wrote that the speaker exercises at 6:00, but later information suggests that this happens after class. Mark the detail and listen again with a narrow purpose. Repeated listening should become more focused each time. The third listening is not simply another attempt to understand everything; it is a chance to answer one or two unresolved questions.

Compare your timeline with a partner. Differences are useful because they reveal where the audio was unclear or where one listener made an assumption. Use evidence language: “I wrote 7:15 because I heard ‘a quarter past seven’” or “I changed my prediction because the speaker said ‘on weekends’.” Then summarize the routine orally or in writing without reading every note. A strong summary selects the most important stages and expresses the overall pattern.

### Preparation tools and independence

With teacher authorization, GenAI may be used before listening to generate a short list of routine vocabulary, prediction questions, or a blank schedule organizer. It should not provide a transcript, translation, summary, or answers to the assigned audio. Record the tool, date, prompt, and the specific way the preparation helped. During the listening task, work independently.

CAADI Worksheet 2 extends this practice. Use the same cycle: predict, listen for gist, listen selectively for details, organize a timeline, and verify uncertain information.

    ## The Three Sounds of **-ed**

    Regular past-tense verbs have three possible **-ed** pronunciations:

    - **/id/** — after verbs ending in a **/t/** or **/d/** sound
    *wanted, needed*

    - **/d/** — after a **voiced sound**, except /d/
    *played, lived*

    - **/t/** — after a **voiceless sound**, except /t/
    *worked, watched*

    ## Quick Rule...

    ### **/id/**

    The verb already ends in a **/t/** or **/d/** sound.

    **want → wanted**
    **need → needed**

    The **-ed adds an extra syllable**.

    ### **/d/**

    The verb ends in a **voiced sound** other than /d/.

    **play → played**
    **live → lived**
    **call → called**

    Your vocal cords vibrate when you make the final sound of the base verb.

    ### **/t/**

    The verb ends in a **voiceless sound** other than /t/.

    **work → worked**
    **wash → washed**
    **help → helped**

    Your vocal cords do not vibrate when you make the final sound of the base verb.

    ### Important Reminder

    Students should listen to the **final sound of the base verb**, not simply look at the final letter.

    For example:

    - **laugh** ends with the sound /f/ → **laughed /t/**

    - **change** ends with the voiced sound /dʒ/ → **changed /d/**

    - **use** as a verb ends with /z/ → **used /d/**
