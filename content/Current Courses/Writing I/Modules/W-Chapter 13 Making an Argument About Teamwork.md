# W-Chapter 13: Making an Argument About Teamwork


⚡️ Supporting an opinion with reasons and examples

> Argumentative writing begins with a clear position, but an opinion alone is not enough. Readers need reasons that explain why the position makes sense and examples that show what those reasons mean in practice. Teamwork and collaboration are useful topics because students experience both benefits and challenges when working with others. This week, you will analyze simple argumentative models, formulate a focused opinion, and plan a paragraph with reasons and examples. If authorized, GenAI may help you explore different perspectives, but you must evaluate whether its ideas are relevant, balanced, and appropriate to your context. The paragraph you eventually submit must reflect your own position and decisions. At A2 level, a clear, well-supported opinion is more effective than a complex argument expressed with language you cannot control.


## Essential understanding(s)

- What makes an opinion convincing in a short A2-level paragraph?

- How can writers consider different perspectives while maintaining their own position?

## Guiding Questions

- How can an opinion be stated clearly and specifically?

- What is the difference between a reason and an example?

- How can teamwork experiences provide relevant evidence?

- How should AI-generated perspectives be evaluated before use?

## Learning outcomes

- Identify opinion, reasons, and examples in simple argumentative models.

- Write a focused opinion statement about teamwork or collaboration.

- Generate relevant reasons and specific examples from experience or familiar contexts.

- Organize an argumentative paragraph plan.

- Evaluate authorized GenAI perspectives for relevance, balance, and context.

## Main chapter content

### Stating a focused opinion

A strong opinion sentence names the topic and takes a clear position. “Teamwork is good” is too general. “University projects are more effective when team members have clear responsibilities” gives a specific direction. The paragraph can explain why roles matter. Avoid trying to argue several positions at once. One short paragraph needs one central opinion.

### Developing reasons

A reason answers the question why. For the opinion about clear responsibilities, one reason might be that roles prevent duplicated work. Another might be that each member knows what to prepare. Reasons should be different from each other but connected to the same position. “Because teamwork is important” only repeats the opinion; it does not explain it. Use because, one reason is, and another reason is when they help make the relationship clear.

### Adding specific examples

An example shows how a reason works in a real or realistic situation. After the reason about duplicated work, the writer might explain: “In one class project, two students prepared the same slides because the group did not assign tasks.” The example is specific and directly connected to the reason. It does not need to be dramatic. Familiar classroom experiences can provide strong support when they are relevant and explained clearly.

### Considering other perspectives

Good argumentation does not require pretending that only one view exists. A writer may acknowledge a limitation: “Some students prefer to work alone, but teamwork can develop communication skills.” If GenAI exploration is authorized, it may list benefits, challenges, or alternative positions. Evaluate each idea. Is it connected to university teamwork? Is it culturally and contextually appropriate? Is it too general? Do not use an idea simply because it sounds persuasive. Choose only ideas you understand and can support.

### Distinguishing reasons from examples

Writers sometimes present two examples and believe they have two reasons. Test each supporting sentence by asking whether it is general or specific. A reason is general enough to apply in more than one situation: “Clear roles save time.” An example is a particular illustration: “In our presentation project, each person prepared one section, so we finished before the deadline.” The example supports the reason but does not replace it. Another test is to place because before the reason and for example before the example. If the sentence works naturally, its role is probably clear. In your organizer, use separate boxes for reasons and examples. This visual separation helps you create an argument rather than a list of personal experiences. Your experiences remain valuable because they make each general reason understandable and credible. Keep the reason before the example so the reader meets the general point first.

### Planning the paragraph

Use an organizer with four parts: opinion, reason one with example, reason two with example, and closing sentence. You may decide that one well-developed reason is enough if the paragraph is short, but the support must be specific. Check that each example proves or illustrates its reason. End by restating the position or explaining the result of effective collaboration. This week’s main product is the plan, not the final paragraph. Preserve any AI-use record and identify which ideas you accepted, adapted, or rejected.
