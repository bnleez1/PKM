# Writing I Module 08: Revising with Evidence


⚡️ Consolidating narrative and descriptive writing in Unit II

> Revision becomes more meaningful when you can show what changed and explain why. In this chapter, you will consolidate the skills from Units I and II by comparing your original draft with a revised narrative or descriptive paragraph. You will examine organization, sequence, lexical cohesion, punctuation, and sentence completeness. The purpose is not to accept every comment from a classmate, teacher, or AI tool. The purpose is to make deliberate changes that improve communication while preserving your own ideas and language level. You will submit the original draft, revised version, and revision trail, then complete a monthly reflection and self-evaluation. The first written examination will also assess your ability to recognize and apply the concepts from the first half of the course. Your evidence of process is as important as the polished final paragraph.


## Essential understanding(s)


- What evidence shows that revision has improved a paragraph?

- How can writers consolidate learning across several writing tasks?

## Guiding Questions

- Which changes affect meaning and organization, and which affect correctness?

- How can a writer compare two versions systematically?

- What should a revision trail reveal about accepted and rejected feedback?

- How can reflection prepare a writer for the next unit?

## Learning outcomes

- Compare an original and revised draft using course criteria.

- Revise narrative or descriptive writing for sequence, cohesion, clarity, and punctuation.

- Produce a complete revision trail with reasons for decisions.

- Complete a reflection and self-evaluation based on specific evidence.

- Demonstrate Unit I-II concepts in the first written examination.

## Main chapter content

### Comparing versions systematically

Place the original and revised paragraphs side by side. First, compare the topic sentences. Is the revised focus clearer or more specific? Next, compare the order of supporting details or events. Mark moved, added, and removed sentences. Then examine lexical cohesion and connectors. Finally, compare punctuation and sentence completeness. This sequence helps you see whether the revision improved communication, not only surface correctness.

### Distinguishing major and minor changes

A major revision changes the reader’s understanding. Examples include narrowing the topic, adding an important event, removing an unrelated sentence, clarifying a pronoun, or changing the order of ideas. A minor edit corrects a capital letter, spelling error, comma, or verb form. Both matter, but a final paragraph with only minor edits may still have an unclear focus. Your revision trail should include at least one meaningful change and explain its effect.

### Evaluating feedback rather than following it

Review every suggestion in relation to your purpose and the A2 criteria. A peer may identify a confusing sentence without knowing how to fix it. An AI tool may recommend a fluent sentence that changes your meaning or sounds unlike you. The teacher may ask a question that requires you to add explanation. Your decision can be accept, adapt, or reject. A strong justification is specific: “I adapted the suggestion because the original transition was unclear, but I used first instead of initially because first is appropriate for my level.”

### Preparing the final submission

Proofread the revised version after the major decisions are complete. Check title or heading format if required, paragraph indentation or spacing, capitalization, end punctuation, and spelling of key words. Attach the original draft and revision trail in the order requested. The collection should allow another person to follow the development of the paragraph. Do not remove evidence simply because it contains errors; the errors help demonstrate learning.

### Building an examination review routine

The first written examination will require more than memorizing definitions. Prepare by working with short examples. Identify whether a group of words is a complete sentence or fragment and explain why. Add punctuation to a short passage and justify the marks. Reorder scrambled sentences into a unified paragraph. Label a topic sentence, support, and closing sentence. Choose a connector that expresses addition, contrast, reason, or sequence. Finally, compare a narrative and descriptive paragraph and identify how their organization differs. Study in short sessions and explain answers aloud or to a classmate. When you can explain a decision, you are more likely to transfer the concept to a new text. Include one examination preparation goal in your reflection, such as checking sentence boundaries before focusing on vocabulary.

### Reflecting and preparing for the examination

In your reflection, identify one skill that improved across Units I and II and one skill that still needs practice. Use examples from your writing: “My topic sentences are more focused because I now include a controlling idea,” or “I still repeat then too frequently in narratives.” For the written examination, review paragraph parts, sentence completeness, punctuation, basic connectors, sequence, narrative and descriptive organization, and lexical cohesion. Practice explaining why a sentence belongs in a paragraph, not only identifying a rule. This prepares you to transfer the concepts to new examples.
