# W-Chapter 16: Publishing and Reflecting on Your Writing Process


⚡️ Final editing, process evidence, authorship, and course closure

> The final week brings together the complete writing process: planning, drafting, feedback, revision, editing, reflection, and responsible documentation. You will prepare the final argumentative paragraph, assemble a portfolio or process trail, and include an AI-use declaration when applicable. The final version must remain student-authored, show authentic voice and critical thinking, and meet the course expectation of 90-100 percent originality. Originality does not mean refusing all feedback; it means that you make and understand the final decisions and do not submit generated language as your own. You will also complete a final reflection, self-evaluation, and the second written examination covering Units III and IV. The aim is to recognize not only what you produced, but how your writing process has changed and which strategies you can carry into Intermediate Writing.


## Essential understanding(s)


- What evidence demonstrates authentic student authorship and growth?

- How can reflection turn a completed course into a plan for future writing?

## Guiding Questions

- What final editing steps make an argumentative paragraph ready for submission?

- Which documents best show the development of the text?

- How should authorized GenAI use be declared transparently?

- Which writing strategies should transfer to the next course?

## Learning outcomes

- Edit and proofread a final argumentative paragraph.

- Assemble process evidence showing planning, drafting, feedback, and revision.

- Write a transparent declaration of authorized GenAI use when applicable.

- Complete a final reflection and self-evaluation using evidence.

- Demonstrate Unit III-IV concepts in the second written examination.

## Main chapter content

### Completing final editing

Begin with one last meaning check. Does the opinion remain clear? Does each reason support it? Does each example illustrate a reason? Is the solution connected to the problem? Then edit sentence-level features in focused passes: complete sentences and verb forms; capitalization and end punctuation; connectors and commas; spelling and vocabulary. Read the paragraph aloud and compare it with the rubric. Stop adding new content unless a genuine gap remains, because late additions may introduce new errors.

### Assembling the process trail

Organize the materials in a clear sequence: graphic organizer, independent first draft, peer or teacher feedback, authorized AI feedback and use record if applicable, revision trail, revised draft, and final version. You may add brief labels or dates. The process trail should allow another person to see how the paragraph developed and which decisions you made. Do not remove early drafts because they contain mistakes; growth is visible through contrast.

### Declaring GenAI use transparently

If you did not use GenAI, state that no GenAI was used. If use was authorized, name the tool, date, purpose, and type of prompt. Explain which suggestions you accepted, adapted, or rejected and how you verified them. A declaration might state that the tool identified possible unclear connectors after the first draft, while all sentences and revisions were written and selected by the student. Do not claim that a tool was used only for grammar if it also generated language. Transparency protects trust.

### Understanding originality and authentic voice

The course requires submitted work to maintain 90-100 percent originality and reflect authentic voice and critical thinking. This means the final paragraph should be based on your own ideas, experiences, and decisions. Feedback may help you notice problems, but it must not replace your writing. You should understand every word and sentence and be able to explain why it appears. A simple sentence that accurately represents your meaning is more valuable than a fluent generated sentence that you cannot reproduce or discuss.

### Creating a final growth statement

End the portfolio with a short growth statement that uses evidence from the semester. Organize it around three claims: what you can now do, which process strategy helps you most, and what you will improve next. Support each claim with a document or change. For example, you may compare the Week 1 diagnostic paragraph with the final argument to show clearer topic sentences and stronger support. You may cite the revision trail as evidence that you now evaluate feedback instead of accepting it automatically. Your next-step goal should be specific and observable, such as developing longer support with examples or checking cohesion across multiple paragraphs. A growth statement does not need to claim perfection. Honest recognition of continuing needs demonstrates mature self-assessment and prepares you to enter the next course with a plan.

### Reflecting and preparing for the next course

Review work from Week 1 and compare it with the final paragraph. Identify changes in sentence control, paragraph organization, cohesion, detail, revision, and feedback use. Support each claim with evidence. Then set two next-step goals for Intermediate Writing: one product goal, such as developing longer paragraphs, and one process goal, such as planning before drafting. For the second examination, review descriptive, expository, and argumentative organization; precise vocabulary; addition, contrast, sequence, reason, example, and result connectors; coherence; cohesion; revision; verification; and authorship. The course closes with a clearer understanding of writing as a process you can continue to manage independently.
