# W-Chapter 9: Describing Sports and Physical Activities


⚡️ Selecting precise details for a coherent paragraph

> Unit III asks you to write with greater lexical precision, coherence, and cohesion. Sports and physical activities provide a useful context because they involve actions, places, equipment, routines, abilities, and feelings. A strong description does not simply list adjectives. It selects details that create a clear central impression or explain what an activity is like. This week, you will analyze models, distinguish relevant from unnecessary details, and plan a descriptive paragraph about a sport or physical activity. You will write your own draft and use a checklist to review coherence and punctuation. The challenge is to choose vocabulary that is specific enough to communicate clearly but still appropriate to your A2 level. Accuracy and meaning are more important than using advanced words.


## Essential understanding(s)

- How do precise details create a clear description?

- What makes a descriptive paragraph coherent rather than a list?

## Guiding Questions

- What central impression or purpose should guide the description?

- Which details about actions, place, equipment, or feelings are most relevant?

- How can vocabulary become more precise without becoming unnecessarily difficult?

- How can a checklist reveal problems with coherence and punctuation?

## Learning outcomes

- Identify the central impression and supporting details in descriptive models.

- Plan a descriptive paragraph about a sport or physical activity.

- Use precise A2 vocabulary for actions, equipment, place, and feelings.

- Organize details coherently around one focus.

- Review an independent draft for coherence and punctuation.

## Key concepts and vocabulary

| **Term**               | **Working definition**                                        |
| :--------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **central impression** | the main feeling or understanding a description creates       |
| **precise vocabulary** | words that communicate an exact or specific meaning           |
| **general word**       | a broad word that gives limited information                   |
| **action verb**        | a verb that expresses a physical or mental action             |
| **equipment**          | objects used to perform an activity or sport                  |
| **setting**            | the place and conditions in which an activity happens         |
| **coherence**          | the logical and understandable relationship among ideas       |
| **detail selection**   | the decision to include information that supports the purpose |
| **sensory detail**     | information connected to sight, sound, touch, taste, or smell |
| **checklist review**   | a systematic examination of a draft using stated criteria     |

## Main chapter content

### Choosing a central impression

Before listing details, decide what you want the reader to understand. You might describe swimming as a calm individual activity, football practice as energetic teamwork, or walking as an accessible way to reduce stress. The central impression guides vocabulary. If the focus is teamwork, details about communication, positions, and shared goals are more relevant than the color of the field. If the focus is relaxation, breathing, rhythm, and environment may be more useful.

### Moving from general to precise words

General words such as good, nice, do, and thing communicate little by themselves. Replace them when a familiar, more exact word is available. Instead of “I do exercise,” write “I jog for twenty minutes.” Instead of “We use things,” name the equipment: ball, racket, helmet, mat, or shoes. Precision does not require rare vocabulary. A correct common word is better than an advanced word used incorrectly. Use a dictionary to confirm meaning, word class, and example patterns.

### Organizing descriptive details

A descriptive paragraph needs a logical path. You can move from general to specific, from the setting to the action, or from the beginning to the end of a typical activity. A paragraph about a gym class might begin with the setting, describe the warm-up and main exercises, and finish with the writer’s feeling afterward. Use connectors such as first, during, also, but, and finally when they match the organization. Avoid a series of unrelated sentences that each introduce a new aspect without connection.

### Using models and checklists

When reading a model, underline words that create the central impression and group details by category. Ask why the writer included each detail. In your own draft, write the central impression in the margin. Then place a check beside each sentence that supports it. Mark general words that could become more precise and verify possible replacements. Review punctuation after the ideas are organized. Commas and periods should help separate details into readable sentences.

### Developing detail through observation

Precise description begins with careful observation. Before drafting, spend a few minutes recalling or observing the activity. List what participants do, what equipment they use, where the activity occurs, and how the body or mind feels. Then rank the details according to the central impression. If the paragraph presents walking as relaxing, steady movement, fresh air, and quieter thoughts are more useful than a complete list of clothing. Turn selected notes into specific sentences. Compare “Basketball is fast” with “Players move quickly from one end of the court to the other, and they must make decisions in seconds.” The second sentence explains the general adjective. At A2 level, one precise explanation can be more effective than several decorative adjectives. Verify action verbs and prepositions in a dictionary or model before using them.

### Weekly writing task

Plan and draft a descriptive paragraph about a sport or physical activity that you know directly. Include a focused topic sentence, two or three groups of relevant details, and a closing sentence that returns to the central impression. Use at least three precise action or equipment words. Complete a checklist for coherence and punctuation, and identify one detail you removed because it did not support the focus. Keep the draft for revision in later weeks.
