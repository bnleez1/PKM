# W-Module 3: Planning a Paragraph Before Writing


⚡️ Using prewriting and graphic organizers to create unity

> Strong paragraphs often begin before the first complete sentence is written. Prewriting helps you decide what you want to say, which ideas belong together, and what order will be easiest for a reader to follow. This chapter focuses on paragraph organization and the use of graphic organizers. You will reconstruct model paragraphs, identify their parts, and then plan a short expository paragraph about a routine or an aspect of university life. The purpose of planning is not to create a rigid formula. It is to give your ideas direction so that drafting becomes more focused. By the end of the week, you will produce your own organizer and an independent first draft. Feedback from classmates and the teacher will help you notice whether the paragraph has one main focus and enough relevant support.

## Main chapter content

### Why planning matters

Beginning with the first sentence can feel productive, but it may lead to repetition, missing information, or unrelated ideas. Prewriting creates a space to think. You can list what you know, ask questions, group related details, and decide what to leave out. A plan also reduces the pressure to make every sentence perfect immediately. When the ideas have a logical order, you can concentrate on sentence construction during drafting.

### Recognizing paragraph structure

A topic sentence names the topic and gives it a direction. “My morning routine helps me arrive at university prepared” is more focused than “I have a morning routine.” The first sentence suggests that the paragraph will explain preparation. Supporting sentences can describe waking up, checking materials, eating breakfast, and leaving on time. A closing sentence can restate the result: “Because I follow these steps, I can begin class calmly.” Each sentence supports the controlling idea. A sentence about a favorite movie would not belong unless it clearly connects to preparation.

### Reconstructing a model paragraph

One way to study organization is to place scrambled sentences in a logical order. First, identify the sentence that introduces the general focus. Next, look for time markers, repeated key words, and pronouns that connect details. Finally, find the sentence that sounds like a conclusion rather than a new supporting point. More than one order may be grammatically possible, but the best order gives the reader a clear path. Explain your reasoning instead of guessing.

### Choosing a graphic organizer

Different organizers support different purposes. A timeline works well when actions happen in time order. A cluster helps you explore categories connected to a topic. A simple outline works well when you already know the main idea and want to arrange two or three supports. For an expository paragraph about campus life, you might place the topic sentence at the top, list three supporting points, add one specific detail under each point, and write a possible closing idea. The organizer is a thinking tool, not the final paragraph.

### Turning an organizer into sentences

An organizer does not automatically become a coherent paragraph. When drafting, treat each major point as a sentence purpose. The first sentence establishes the focus. The next sentence develops the first support, and a following sentence may add an example. Continue in the planned order, but allow yourself to adjust the plan if a clearer relationship appears. Use a repeated key word or simple reference to connect the new sentence to the previous one. After drafting, draw a line from every supporting sentence back to the topic sentence. If you cannot explain the connection, revise or remove the sentence. Also look for an unsupported general statement such as “University life is difficult.” Add a brief reason or example that matches your experience. Planning reduces confusion, but drafting is the stage where you test whether the planned ideas can actually communicate a complete message to a reader.

### Drafting and responding to feedback

Use your organizer to write a complete first draft. Do not stop after every sentence to make small corrections. First, communicate the full idea. Then ask a peer to identify the topic sentence, underline supporting details, and mark any sentence that seems unrelated or unclear. The peer should not rewrite the paragraph. Useful comments include “I need an example here,” “This sentence seems to change the topic,” or “I understand the order.” Use teacher feedback to decide what to revise next. Submit your organizer and first draft together so the development of your thinking is visible.
