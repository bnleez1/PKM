# W-Chapter 15: Strengthening Argumentative Organization


⚡️ Connecting opinions, reasons, examples, and feedback

> An argumentative paragraph is convincing when every part has a clear relationship to the central position. The opinion gives direction, reasons explain the position, and examples make the reasons concrete. If one part is weak or disconnected, the reader may understand the sentences but not the argument. This week, you will test the relationships in your draft through peer and teacher feedback. After your own paragraph exists, authorized GenAI may provide limited feedback on clarity, organization, or cohesion. You will decide which suggestions to accept, adapt, or reject. The objective is not to make the paragraph sound more advanced. It is to make the logic more visible and the support more relevant while preserving your own language and decisions. Each change should be linked to a specific reader need or course criterion.


## Essential understanding(s)


- How can a writer make the logic of an argument easy to follow?

- How should different sources of feedback influence—but not control—revision?

## Guiding Questions

- Does each reason directly support the opinion?

- Does each example clearly illustrate the reason before it?

- Which connectors accurately show cause, contrast, addition, or result?

- How can feedback be translated into an independent revision decision?

## Learning outcomes

- Evaluate the relationship among opinion, reasons, examples, and solution.

- Reorganize supporting ideas for clearer argumentative logic.

- Use connectors to express reason, example, contrast, addition, and result.

- Give and receive focused peer and teacher feedback.

- Use authorized GenAI feedback only after drafting and document decisions.

## Key concepts and vocabulary

| **Term**                  | **Working definition**                                                  |
| :------------------------ | :---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **argument structure**    | the organized relationship among position and support                   |
| **direct support**        | information that clearly strengthens the stated position                |
| **reason-example link**   | the connection between a general reason and a specific illustration     |
| **cause**                 | a reason something happens                                              |
| **result**                | what happens because of an action or condition                          |
| **counterpoint**          | a relevant different view or limitation                                 |
| **relevance test**        | a question used to decide whether a sentence supports the main position |
| **organization feedback** | comments about order and relationships among ideas                      |
| **adapt**                 | change a suggestion so that it fits the writer’s purpose and language   |
| **decision log**          | a record of feedback and the writer’s response                          |

## Main chapter content

### Testing the opinion-reason relationship

Write the opinion at the top of a page. Under it, list each reason and complete the sentence “This supports my opinion because….” If you cannot complete the explanation, the reason may be too general or unrelated. For example, the opinion “Campus teams should assign roles” is not supported by “Students have many classes” unless the writer explains how roles save time or reduce confusion. Make the relationship explicit.

### Testing the reason-example relationship

Place each example directly after the reason it illustrates. Ask whether a reader can see the connection without guessing. A reason may say that clear roles prevent duplicated work. The example should show duplicated work or its prevention. An example about a friendly classmate may be positive but irrelevant. Add phrases such as for example or in one project when they help signal the illustration, but ensure the example itself is specific.

### Improving connectors and flow

Use because to introduce a reason, for example to introduce an illustration, but or however to show contrast, also or another reason to add support, and so or as a result to show an outcome. Check punctuation patterns from class. Avoid beginning every sentence with a connector. Logical order, repeated key words, and pronoun reference also create cohesion. A connector cannot repair a sentence that does not belong.

### Using feedback as evidence

Ask peers to label O for opinion, R for reason, E for example, and S for solution. Any sentence that cannot be labeled may need clarification or removal. Ask the teacher about places where the reasoning is incomplete. An authorized AI prompt may ask it to identify where a reason lacks an example or where the paragraph changes focus, without rewriting. Compare all feedback. Agreement among readers may indicate a genuine clarity problem, but you still decide how to revise.

### Reading the paragraph as a chain of support

Reduce the paragraph to a chain: opinion → reason → example → result or closing. Write each part on a separate line using only key words. The logic should remain understandable even without the full sentences. If the chain breaks, locate the missing relationship. Perhaps an example does not illustrate the reason, or the closing sentence introduces a different issue. Return to the full draft and revise the connection. Then ask a reader to repeat the chain after one reading. If the reader can state the opinion and main support accurately, the organization is working. This activity also reveals when too many reasons are competing for limited space. One or two developed reasons are usually more convincing than four reasons with no examples. Depth of support matters more than the number of points, especially in one short paragraph.

### Recording revision decisions

For each important comment, record the source, the issue, your action, and your reason. You may accept a suggestion, adapt it to your language, or reject it. For example: “Peer said example two was unrelated; accepted and replaced it with a campus example that shows the effect of unclear roles.” Or: “AI suggested nevertheless; rejected because but expresses the contrast clearly at my level.” The decision log demonstrates critical use of feedback and prepares the paragraph for final editing.
