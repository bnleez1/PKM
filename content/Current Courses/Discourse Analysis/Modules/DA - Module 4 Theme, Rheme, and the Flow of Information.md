# DA - Module 4: Theme, Rheme, and the Flow of Information


⚡️ Tracing given and new information across clauses, paragraphs, and extended texts

> Writers and speakers do more than choose what information to include; they decide where to place it. Clause openings orient readers and listeners, while later positions often carry information that advances the message. The concepts of theme and rheme offer a practical way to examine this information packaging. Across a paragraph, themes may remain constant, take up information from a previous rheme, or derive from a larger organizing topic. These patterns influence processing, emphasis, and perceived coherence. This week you will annotate themes and rhemes in several genres, diagram thematic progression, and revise a short text whose information flow is difficult to follow. The goal is not to enforce one preferred pattern, but to understand how different progressions serve different communicative purposes and how teachers can make these choices visible to learners.


## Essential understanding(s)


How does the placement of information guide a reader or listener through discourse?

How can thematic progression be analyzed and taught without reducing writing to a fixed formula?

## Guiding Questions


What counts as theme and rheme in a working clause-level analysis?

How do given and new information interact with thematic choices?

What are constant, linear, and derived thematic progression patterns?

How can a revision improve information flow while preserving the writer’s intended emphasis?

## Learning outcomes

Identify topical themes and rhemes in clauses from varied genres.

Distinguish theme–rheme structure from the related but non-identical distinction between given and new information.

Diagram constant, linear, and derived thematic progression across a paragraph or short text.

Explain how thematic choices influence emphasis, continuity, and processing.

Revise a short text to improve information flow and justify the changes pedagogically.

## Key concepts and vocabulary

| **Term**                 | **Working definition**                                                                                            |
|:------------------------ |:----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Theme**                | The point of departure that orients a clause within its discourse.                                                |
| **Rheme**                | The portion of the clause in which the theme is developed.                                                        |
| **Topical theme**        | The first experiential element in the clause, often the participant, process, or circumstance being foregrounded. |
| **Marked theme**         | A less typical thematic choice that foregrounds an element such as time, place, condition, or object.             |
| **Unmarked theme**       | A thematically typical choice, often the grammatical subject in a declarative clause.                             |
| **Given information**    | Information treated as accessible or recoverable for the audience.                                                |
| **New information**      | Information presented as not yet active or as the main update.                                                    |
| **Thematic progression** | The patterned movement of themes and rhemes across clauses.                                                       |
| **Constant progression** | A pattern in which successive clauses maintain the same or closely related theme.                                 |
| **Linear progression**   | A pattern in which information introduced in one rheme becomes the theme of a later clause.                       |
| **Derived progression**  | A pattern in which several themes develop aspects of a larger hypertheme or topic.                                |

## Main chapter content

1. The clause as a message
A clause can be analyzed simultaneously as grammar, representation, interaction, and message. Theme–rheme analysis focuses on the clause as message. The theme is the point from which the speaker or writer launches the clause; the rheme develops that point. In a simple declarative clause, the grammatical subject frequently functions as theme: “The students analyzed the transcript.” The students is the theme, and analyzed the transcript is the rheme.
Theme is not identical to “topic” in the everyday sense. A whole text may be about classroom interaction, while individual clauses begin with the teacher, a question, after several seconds, or however. Each choice creates a local orientation. A marked theme places an element in initial position for a particular effect: “During the second exchange, the learner initiated repair.” The time circumstance frames the interpretation before the participant and action are introduced.
The analysis should remain workable. Depending on the theoretical model, theme can include textual and interpersonal elements before the topical theme. In this course, begin by identifying the clause’s topical point of departure and then note connectors, modal adjuncts, or other initial elements when they contribute to organization or stance. Consistency matters more than multiplying labels.

2. Given and new are related, not identical
Theme often contains given information, while rheme often introduces new information, but the relationship is not automatic. Given information is treated as accessible through prior mention, shared knowledge, the situation, or inference. New information is presented as the main update or as not currently active. A writer may deliberately place new information in theme position to create contrast, shift perspective, or signal a new stage.
Consider: “The interview produced three recurring themes. The first theme concerned feedback. A second pattern emerged in the peer discussions.” The phrase the first theme is given because themes have already been introduced. A second pattern is new, even though it appears in thematic position. The choice announces a transition.
Prosody helps spoken discourse mark information status. Stress, intonation, and rhythm can highlight new or contrastive elements regardless of clause position. In writing, punctuation, formatting, cleft constructions, and lexical signals may perform related work. Analysts should therefore avoid equating first position with old information and final position with new information. Instead, ask what the producer assumes is accessible and where attention is directed.

3. Major patterns of thematic progression
In constant thematic progression, several clauses return to the same theme or participant. This pattern can create clarity when describing a single concept: “Discourse analysis examines language in context. It considers connected texts. It also investigates social action.” The risk is monotony or insufficient development if every sentence simply repeats the topic.
In linear progression, an element introduced in one rheme becomes the next theme: “The class analyzed a service encounter. The encounter contained an indirect complaint. That complaint prompted a repair sequence.” This chain creates a sense of forward movement and can help readers follow complex explanations.
In derived progression, several themes develop aspects of a larger organizing topic or hypertheme. A paragraph about spoken discourse may proceed through planning, turn-taking, prosody, and contextual dependence. Each theme is different, but all derive from the announced category. This pattern is common in classification and overview passages.
Real texts combine patterns. A paragraph may maintain a constant topic, use a linear chain to explain a process, and then shift to a derived subtopic. Diagramming makes the pattern visible, but the purpose is interpretive: explain how the progression supports the genre and where a break creates or resolves difficulty.

4. Diagnosing information-flow problems
A text can feel “jumpy” when each sentence introduces a new theme without connecting it to active information. It can feel repetitive when a constant theme is maintained too mechanically. It can also overload the reader when long themes contain too much new information before the main clause develops. Theme–rheme analysis provides a diagnostic lens, not a universal scoring system.
Begin revision by identifying the intended progression. If a paragraph explains a sequence, linear progression may help. If it describes dimensions of a concept, derived themes may be more appropriate. If it argues a position, constant reference to the central claim may be necessary, while evidence and interpretation develop in the rhemes. Then inspect whether pronouns and lexical choices make the links recoverable.
Revision may involve changing clause order, repeating a key noun, replacing a vague pronoun, combining sentences, or adding a framing sentence. It may also involve leaving an unexpected theme in place because the writer intends contrast or emphasis. Every change should be justified in relation to audience processing and communicative purpose, not merely because a diagram appears more symmetrical.

5. Pedagogical applications
Theme–rheme analysis can support reading and writing when introduced through concrete tasks. Learners can underline clause openings in a model paragraph and discuss what each opening asks them to keep in mind. They can reorder scrambled sentences using information flow rather than connector matching alone. They can compare two versions of a paragraph and explain which is easier to process and why.
For drafting, students can write a theme-only outline: the sequence of planned clause or sentence openings. This reveals whether a paragraph sustains a topic, develops a chain, or shifts unpredictably. During peer review, readers can mark where they lose the thread and identify the active referent they expected to see next.
Teachers should not require every paragraph to follow a single progression. Genres differ, and multilingual writers may draw on rhetorical resources that do not match one institutional expectation. The objective is expanded choice. When learners understand how thematic organization affects processing, they can preserve meaningful stylistic differences while adapting strategically to a target audience and genre.

6. A progression-based revision protocol
Use a paragraph of six to eight clauses and create a three-column table: clause number, theme, and rheme. Then add a fourth column identifying whether the theme is given, new, or contrastive in that local context. Draw lines showing when a rheme element becomes a later theme. The resulting pattern should make the paragraph’s movement visible.
Read the paragraph aloud while looking only at the theme column. Does the sequence provide a recognizable route, or does every clause begin from an unrelated point? Then read only the rhemes. Do they develop the themes or repeat information without advancing the message? Select no more than three revisions. Possible changes include reordering clauses, replacing a vague pronoun, repeating a key noun, moving a time circumstance into initial position, or dividing an overloaded clause.
After revision, compare the two diagrams and write a rationale for each change. Explain what the reader is now expected to carry forward and what information becomes prominent. Retain any marked theme that serves a clear purpose. The objective is not perfect regularity but intentional information flow.

            ### Answer Key

            | Type | Examples | Relationship |
            |--- | ---|---|
            | **Repetition** | *library → library → library*; *students → students → students* | The same lexical item is repeated to maintain the topic. | **Synonymy / near-synonymy** | *students → learners* | Different words refer to approximately the same group. |
            | **Antonymy** | *quiet ↔ noisy*  | The words express contrasting meanings. |
            | **Hypernymy** | *resources*; *furniture*                                                                                         | These are broader categories.                                         |
            | **Hyponymy**                 | *books, journals, dictionaries, databases* → **resources**; *desks, tables, chairs, bookshelves* → **furniture** | These are specific members of broader categories.                     |
            | **Collocation**              | *borrow books, take notes, conduct research, study for exams*                                                    | These words commonly occur together in the context of academic study. |

            This paragraph also works well for having students **underline the lexical items first and classify them themselves before seeing the answer key**.

    The main difference between lexical cohesion and grammatical cohesion depends on where the connection comes from. Grammatical cohesion relies primarily on grammatical resources and structural relationships, whereas lexical cohesion relies on relationships among words and meanings.

    Here are three clear examples of each type of lexical cohesion:

    | Type                            | Example 1                                                                                                                     | Example 2                                                                                | Example 3                                                                                        |
    |:------------------------------- |:----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |:---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |:------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
    | **Repetition**                  | The **student** asked a question. The **student** wanted clarification.                                                       | The **book** was expensive, but the **book** was useful.                                 | **Technology** is changing education. **Technology** also affects communication.                 |
    | **Synonymy**                    | The **teacher** entered the room. The **instructor** began the lesson.                                                        | The task was **difficult**. The exercise was quite **challenging**.                      | She was **happy** with the result. Her parents were also **pleased**.                            |
    | **Antonymy**                    | Some students found the test **easy**, while others found it **difficult**.                                                   | Attendance **increased** in September but **decreased** in October.                      | The first text is very **formal**, while the second is more **informal**.                        |
    | **Hyponymy / Subordination**    | **Rose** is a type of **flower**.                                                                                             | **Laptop** is a type of **computer**.                                                    | **Spanish** is a type of **language**.                                                           |
    | **Hypernymy / Superordination** | **Animal** is the broader category for **dog**, **cat**, and **horse**.                                                       | **Vehicle** is the broader category for **car**, **bus**, and **motorcycle**.            | **Fruit** is the broader category for **apple**, **banana**, and **orange**.                     |
    | **Collocation**                 | The **professor** entered the **classroom**, opened the **textbook**, and asked the **students** to begin the **assignment**. | The **teacher** gave the **students** a **worksheet** to complete during the **lesson**. | The **doctor** examined the **patient** and recommended a new **treatment** at the **hospital**. |

    The key distinction between **hyponymy** and **hypernymy** is direction:

    - **Hyponym → more specific:** *dog → animal*

    - **Hypernym → more general:** *animal → dog*

    So in the sentence **“The dog ran across the yard. The animal was chasing a ball,”** *dog* is the hyponym and *animal* is the hypernym.

            Here is the paragraph divided into **Theme** and **Rheme** at the clause level:

            | #   | Theme                      | Rheme                                                                         |
            |:--- |:-------------------------- |:----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
            | 1   | **The university library** | *is one of the busiest places on campus.*                                     |
            | 2   | **The library**            | *provides quiet study areas, computers, and group rooms for students.*        |
            | 3   | **During exam week**       | *the library stays open until midnight so students have more time to study.*  |
            | 4   | **A new tutoring center**  | *has also opened beside the main entrance.*                                   |
            | 5   | **In this center**         | *students can receive help with writing, mathematics, and study skills.*      |
            | 6   | **These services**         | *make the library an important place for students who need academic support.* |

    - Basic Theme–Rheme Structure

        Text

        The students analyzed the interview transcript.
        The transcript contained several examples of repair.
        These examples revealed how the speakers solved communication problems.

        Analysis

        Clause	Theme	Rheme
        1	The students	analyzed the interview transcript
        2	The transcript	contained several examples of repair
        3	These examples	revealed how the speakers solved communication problems

        What it illustrates: In each clause, the theme gives the reader a starting point, while the rheme provides information about that starting point. This is a useful introductory example because the grammatical subjects also function as the topical themes.


        Key takeaway: there are two patterns to theme and rheme: 1) Same theme but new rheme from one sentence to the next and 2) Change rheme of one sentence to the theme in the next sentence.

## Class Discussion

Unmarked vs. Marked Themes plus Rheme

The university library is one of the busiest places on campus. The library provides quiet study areas, computers, and group rooms for students. During exam week, the library stays open until midnight so students have more time to study. A new tutoring center has also opened beside the main entrance. In this center, students can receive help with writing, mathematics, and study skills. These services make the library an important place for students who need academic support.

- Unmarked vs. Marked Theme - Review explanation on your own.

    **Text**: The researchers interviewed twenty university students. During the second interview, several participants mentioned difficulties with academic writing. In the final question, the researchers asked students to suggest possible solutions.

    **Analysis**

    The first sentence begins with *The researchers*, an unmarked theme because the grammatical subject occupies the typical initial position.

    The next two sentences begin with circumstances:

    During the second interview → marked theme
    In the final question → marked theme

    The writer places time or location within the procedure first so that readers know how to frame the information that follows.

    What it illustrates: A marked theme is not simply unusual word order. It can deliberately foreground time, place, condition, or another circumstance before introducing the main participant or action.

- Given Information (GI) and New Information (NI) Are Not the Same as Theme and Rheme

    The university library is one of the busiest places on campus. The library provides quiet study areas, computers, and group rooms for students. During exam week, the library stays open until midnight so students have more time to study. A new tutoring center has also opened beside the main entrance. In this center, students can receive help with writing, mathematics, and study skills. These services make the library an important place for students who need academic support.

    - Explanation - Review on your own.

        Text:

        The class identified three problems with the textbook dialogue.
        The first problem was the absence of natural hesitation.
        Another important issue was the lack of overlapping speech.
        A completely different problem appeared in the final exchange.

        Here, *the first problem* refers to something already made accessible by three problems, so it represents relatively given information appearing in theme position.

        However, *a completely different problem* introduces something new while also functioning as the theme of its clause.

        What it illustrates: Theme does not automatically mean “old information,” and rheme does not automatically mean “new information.” Writers can deliberately introduce new information as a theme when they want to announce a shift or redirect attention. This distinction is explicitly emphasized in the module. Celce-Murcia and Olshtain similarly connect information structure with the management of old and new information across discourse.
