# DA - Chapter 15: Teaching Readers to Navigate Discourse


⚡️ Culturally responsive pre-reading and while-reading design with UDL-informed scaffolds

> Discourse analysis becomes pedagogically valuable when it helps learners notice how a text works and choose effective ways to read it. Pre-reading activities can activate relevant knowledge, establish purpose, and preview a structure without giving away the text. While-reading activities can direct attention to lexical chains, reference, paragraph functions, rhetorical stages, and evidence. Poorly designed support can oversimplify the text, impose stereotypes, or turn reading into a sequence of disconnected questions. This week you will design feasible pre-reading and while-reading activities based on an authentic text and its macrostructure. The plan must include clear outcomes, culturally responsive framing, UDL options, instructions, scaffolds, and an evidence-based rationale. Peer review will test whether each activity aligns with the discourse objective and remains practical for a real ELT classroom.


## Essential understanding(s)


How can instruction make discourse structure visible while preserving meaningful reading and learner agency?

What makes a reading activity aligned, culturally responsive, accessible, and feasible?

## Guiding Questions


Which barriers arise from language, background knowledge, structure, task design, or cultural assumptions?

What should learners notice before and during reading?

How can multiple means of engagement, representation, and action/expression support the same outcome?

How will the teacher know whether learners used discourse structure to comprehend the text?

## Learning outcomes

Diagnose discourse-related barriers and resources in an authentic written text.

Design aligned pre-reading and while-reading activities that leverage macrostructure.

Integrate culturally responsive framing and UDL options without lowering the analytical objective.

Specify instructions, timing, grouping, materials, scaffolds, and evidence of learning.

Use peer review to improve alignment, accessibility, and classroom feasibility.

## Key concepts and vocabulary

| **Term**                                    | **Working definition**                                                                                                   |
| :------------------------------------------ | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Pre-reading**                             | Activity completed before close reading to establish purpose, activate relevant knowledge, or preview useful structure.  |
| **While-reading**                           | Activity completed during reading to guide attention, monitoring, integration, and evidence use.                         |
| **Scaffolding**                             | Temporary, responsive support that enables learners to perform beyond what they could do independently.                  |
| **Alignment**                               | The fit among learning outcome, activity, text, support, and assessment evidence.                                        |
| **Cultural responsiveness**                 | Intentional use of learners’ knowledge and identities while examining assumptions and providing access to new practices. |
| **Barrier analysis**                        | Identification of features that may obstruct access for particular learners or under particular conditions.              |
| **Multiple means of engagement**            | Options that support relevance, choice, persistence, and purposeful participation.                                       |
| **Multiple means of representation**        | Options for accessing information through varied formats and supports.                                                   |
| **Multiple means of action and expression** | Options for navigating tasks and demonstrating learning.                                                                 |
| **Gradual release**                         | A progression from modeled and guided performance toward independent application.                                        |

## Main chapter content

1. Begin with the discourse objective
Activity design should start with what learners will understand or do through discourse. “Read the article” is a procedure, not an outcome. A discourse objective might be: identify the problem–solution stages and use them to summarize the author’s argument; trace lexical chains that develop two positions; or compare how paragraph openings guide the reader.
The objective determines the evidence. If learners must identify macrostructure, they should produce a map, sequence labels, or justify stage boundaries. A vocabulary matching exercise may support the task but cannot demonstrate the global outcome by itself.
Alignment also prevents activity overload. An authentic text offers many possible features, but a single lesson should prioritize a manageable lens. Select supports that make the target feature accessible and defer unrelated analysis.

2. Culturally responsive pre-reading
Pre-reading can activate knowledge, but teachers should not assume all learners share the same experiences. An open prompt, image set, short scenario, or concept map can invite multiple points of connection. When a text includes culturally specific institutions or practices, provide necessary context without presenting a community as uniform.
Prediction should be evidence-based. Learners can inspect title, source, headings, and opening paragraph to predict purpose and structure, then revise predictions during reading. This links top-down expectations with bottom-up verification.
Avoid pre-teaching every unfamiliar word. Select vocabulary that is essential to the macrostructure or cannot be inferred. Too much frontloading delays engagement and suggests that comprehension requires complete lexical mastery. Offer optional supports for learners who need them.

3. While-reading through discourse structure
While-reading tasks should direct attention to relationships. Learners can label paragraph functions, highlight cause and effect, trace reference, complete a comparison matrix, or identify the evidence used to evaluate a solution. Tasks should require returning to the text.
Chunking can reduce processing load, but chunks should preserve meaningful stages. After each section, learners can update a global organizer and state how the new information changes their model. This supports integration rather than isolated detail collection.
Teacher prompts can move from noticing to explanation: What signal marks the shift? What does this paragraph contribute to the whole? Which earlier idea does this expression refer to? How does the evidence support the writer’s evaluation? Such questions make discourse analysis part of comprehension rather than an abstract labeling exercise.

4. UDL options and equivalent evidence
UDL planning anticipates variability. Learners might access the text in print or a screen-readable format, use a glossary or audio support, and choose among organizer formats. Engagement can be supported through relevant text choice, role-based reading purposes, and collaborative or independent pathways.
Options must remain aligned. If the outcome is to identify problem–solution structure, a learner might draw a map, complete a structured table, or orally explain stages with textual references. These are different forms of equivalent evidence. An option that merely asks for a personal reaction would not demonstrate the same outcome.
Supports should be adjustable. Provide sentence frames or partially completed organizers for those who need them, while allowing others to work from a blank template. Plan how assistance will fade so that learners prepare for independent analysis.

5. Feasibility and peer review
A complete plan includes text source, learner profile, objective, duration, grouping, materials, instructions, anticipated responses, monitoring, and assessment. Consider whether the text length fits the time, whether technology is available, and whether all learners can participate. Include a contingency if devices or internet access fail.
Peer review should use alignment questions rather than general praise. Does each activity contribute to the outcome? Are instructions clear? Which barrier does each scaffold address? Does any prompt rely on a stereotype or inaccessible background knowledge? What evidence will be collected?
Revise the rationale after peer review. Cite the discourse feature that motivates each activity and explain why the support is appropriate for the learners. The final design should be teachable, not merely theoretically attractive.

6. Design review through alignment cases
Test the lesson plan against three learner cases. Learner A has strong topic knowledge but limited vocabulary. Learner B decodes accurately but struggles to identify the main organization. Learner C understands the structure but is reluctant to speak during group work. For each learner, identify a barrier, an available strength, and one support that preserves the same outcome.
Then audit every activity with a simple chain: objective → learner action → evidence → feedback. If the objective is to use problem–solution structure to comprehend an article, the action must involve identifying or applying that structure, the evidence must show the relationship among stages, and feedback must address the map rather than only grammar.
Finally, prepare a one-page teacher script that includes instructions, anticipated questions, time cues, and decisions for extending or shortening the task. This practical layer often reveals hidden assumptions in a theoretical plan. A feasible design allows another teacher to understand what learners will do, why they will do it, and how the resulting evidence will guide the next instructional decision.

7. Planning feedback and follow-up
A reading lesson is incomplete without a plan for using learner evidence. Decide in advance what different responses will mean. If many learners identify the correct stages but cannot cite signals, the next step may focus on local evidence. If they decode details but produce incompatible maps, the teacher may model how to prioritize main propositions. If a cultural assumption blocks interpretation, further contextual framing may be needed.
Design one immediate feedback move and one follow-up task. Immediate feedback might compare two learner maps and ask the class to justify boundaries. The follow-up might require an independent map of a shorter text with the same macrostructure. This gradual release shows whether the scaffold transfers.
Also plan how learners will reflect on strategy. A brief exit prompt can ask which signal changed their prediction or where they revised their map. Such metacognitive evidence helps the teacher evaluate not only the final answer but the reading process. The plan therefore becomes responsive: analysis informs instruction, learner performance informs feedback, and feedback informs the next design decision.

8. Analytical checkpoint
Before submitting, confirm that every scaffold addresses an identified barrier and every option produces equivalent evidence of the same objective. Remove activities that are attractive but unaligned. The final plan should clearly show what learners notice, how they use the discourse structure, and how the teacher responds to the evidence.
