# Discourse Analysis Module 2: Modes of Discourse, Audience, and Communicative Purpose

> [!abstract]- ⚡ Module Overview
> **Speaking, writing, hybrid discourse, and their implications for ELT**
>
> Spoken and written discourse are not simply the same language delivered through different channels.
>
> Each mode creates different possibilities and constraints.
>
> Conversation commonly:
>
> - unfolds in real time;
> - allows immediate feedback;
> - depends strongly on shared context.
>
> Writing is commonly:
>
> - more permanent;
> - spatially organized;
> - available for revision;
> - produced for readers who may not be physically present.
>
> However, these differences are **tendencies rather than absolute rules**.
>
> Digital communication complicates the distinction:
>
> - a text message is written but may behave like conversation;
> - a scripted lecture is spoken but may be highly planned;
> - a podcast may be spontaneous, scripted, edited, or all three.
>
> This module develops a flexible framework for comparing discourse through:
>
> - mode;
> - spontaneity;
> - planning;
> - permanence;
> - interactivity;
> - lexical density;
> - deixis;
> - paralinguistic features;
> - genre;
> - communicative purpose;
> - audience;
> - hybrid discourse.
>
> You will apply these dimensions to authentic samples and consider how discourse awareness can inform **ELT materials, teaching, and assessment**.

> [!note]- 🧠 Key Concepts and Vocabulary
>
> > [!note]- Mode
> > The form or channel through which communication is produced and received.
> >
> > Examples:
> >
> > - spoken language
> > - written language
> > - video
> > - digital messaging
>
> > [!note]- Spontaneity
> > The degree to which language is produced immediately with little or no advance preparation.
> >
> > Spontaneous discourse may contain:
> >
> > - pauses;
> > - fillers;
> > - repetition;
> > - self-correction.
>
> > [!note]- Planning
> > The degree to which a speaker or writer:
> >
> > - prepares;
> > - organizes;
> > - rehearses;
> > - edits;
> > - revises
> >
> > a message before producing it.
>
> > [!note]- Permanence
> > The extent to which discourse remains available after production and can be:
> >
> > - reviewed;
> > - reread;
> > - replayed;
> > - referred to later.
>
> > [!note]- Interactivity
> > The degree to which participants can respond to one another and jointly influence how discourse develops.
>
> > [!note]- Lexical Density
> > The amount of information carried by content words such as:
> >
> > - nouns;
> > - main verbs;
> > - adjectives;
> > - adverbs
> >
> > in relation to the total amount of language used.
>
> > [!note]- Deixis
> > Words whose meaning depends on the communicative situation or context.
> >
> > Examples:
> >
> > - I
> > - you
> > - here
> > - there
> > - now
> > - tomorrow
>
> > [!note]- Paralinguistic Features
> > Vocal and nonverbal features that contribute meaning beyond the words themselves.
> >
> > Examples:
> >
> > - intonation;
> > - stress;
> > - volume;
> > - pauses;
> > - facial expressions;
> > - gestures;
> > - gaze;
> > - body movement.
>
> > [!note]- Genre
> > A recognizable type of communication with a typical:
> >
> > - purpose;
> > - audience;
> > - structure;
> > - style;
> > - set of conventions.
> >
> > Examples include:
> >
> > - job applications;
> > - news reports;
> > - lectures;
> > - text messages.
>
> > [!note]- Communicative Purpose
> > What a speaker or writer is trying to accomplish through a text.
> >
> > Examples:
> >
> > - informing;
> > - persuading;
> > - requesting;
> > - explaining;
> > - entertaining;
> > - warning;
> > - maintaining a social relationship.
>
> > [!note]- Audience
> > The person or group for whom a message is intended.
> >
> > Audience can influence:
> >
> > - vocabulary;
> > - formality;
> > - explanation;
> > - tone;
> > - other language choices.
>
> > [!note]- Hybrid Discourse
> > Communication combining characteristics of more than one mode, medium, or traditional discourse type.
> >
> > Examples:
> >
> > - a written group chat that behaves like conversation;
> > - a scripted video combining written planning with spoken delivery.

> [!example]- 1️⃣ Moving Beyond a Strict Spoken–Written Binary
> Introductory descriptions sometimes characterize:
>
> **Speech as**
>
> - informal;
> - temporary;
> - interactive;
> - context-dependent.
>
> **Writing as**
>
> - formal;
> - permanent;
> - monologic;
> - explicit.
>
> These tendencies can be useful, but they are **not definitions**.
>
> Consider:
>
> - A legal judgment may be read aloud but retain the density and planning associated with formal writing.
> - A group chat is written but may include rapid turns, ellipsis, emojis, repair, and immediate feedback.
> - A podcast may be spontaneous, scripted, edited, or all three.
>
> > [!important]- Analytical Principle
> > Compare discourse according to **dimensions**, not absolute categories.
> >
> > Ask:
> >
> > - How is it produced?
> > - How much planning occurred?
> > - Can participants interact?
> > - Can the discourse be revisited?
> > - How dependent is interpretation on the immediate situation?
> > - What linguistic patterns appear?
> > - What social purpose is being performed?

---

> [!note]- 🔍 Dimensions for Comparing Discourse
> **Mode**
>
> How is the discourse materially produced and received?
>
> **Planning**
>
> How much preparation, editing, or revision is available?
>
> **Interaction**
>
> Can participants respond and shape the discourse as it unfolds?
>
> **Permanence**
>
> Can the language be revisited?
>
> **Context dependence**
>
> How much does interpretation rely on the immediate situation?
>
> **Lexical and grammatical patterning**
>
> Consider:
>
> - lexical density;
> - clause structure;
> - repetition;
> - reference;
> - explicitness.
>
> **Purpose and genre**
>
> What social activity is being performed?

> [!example]- 2️⃣ Why Spontaneous Speech Looks Different
> Spontaneous conversation commonly contains:
>
> - false starts;
> - fillers;
> - vague reference;
> - repetition.
>
> These should not automatically be treated as examples of poor language.
>
> They can help speakers:
>
> - hold the floor;
> - plan while speaking;
> - monitor understanding;
> - maintain interpersonal connection.
>
> By contrast, academic writing may package more information into dense noun phrases and use explicit connectors because readers cannot normally interrupt the writer and ask for clarification.
>
> > [!important]- Key Principle
> > The difference is **functional**.
> >
> > Spoken and written modes distribute the work of meaning-making differently.

---

> [!example]- 3️⃣ Features of Spoken Discourse
> Spoken interaction is organized through time.
>
> Participants produce and interpret language **moment by moment**.
>
> Meaning may be supported by:
>
> - intonation;
> - rhythm;
> - stress;
> - volume;
> - gesture;
> - gaze;
> - the visible environment.
>
> Speakers may:
>
> - check understanding;
> - rephrase;
> - repair problems;
> - adapt to listener reactions.
>
> Turn-taking allows discourse to be **collaboratively constructed** rather than completely planned by a single producer.
>
> > [!note]- Common Linguistic Features
> > Spoken discourse may include:
> >
> > - contractions;
> > - pronouns;
> > - deictic expressions;
> > - discourse markers;
> > - ellipsis;
> > - coordination;
> > - repetition;
> > - relatively short information units.
>
> > [!example]- Discourse Markers
> > Expressions such as:
> >
> > - well
> > - so
> > - you know
> > - I mean
> >
> > may signal:
> >
> > - topic movement;
> > - hesitation;
> > - reformulation;
> > - interpersonal alignment.
>
> > [!example]- Backchannels
> > Listeners may produce:
> >
> > - mm-hm
> > - right
> > - okay
> >
> > These can signal attention without necessarily claiming the speaking turn.
>
> > [!important]- Interpret Features in Context
> > Repetition might:
> >
> > - emphasize a point;
> > - provide planning time;
> > - create cohesion;
> > - establish shared wording.
> >
> > An incomplete clause may be completely understandable when participants share the relevant context.
> >
> > A direct imperative may be appropriate in an emergency but inappropriate in another interpersonal context.
> >
> > Therefore, analyze **what participants are doing together**, not simply which sentence forms appear.

---

> [!example]- 4️⃣ Features of Written Discourse
> Writing is spatially organized and is commonly separated from the immediate production context.
>
> Readers can:
>
> - move backward;
> - move forward;
> - reread.
>
> Writers can:
>
> - draft;
> - revise;
> - reorganize;
> - use visual structure.
>
> Visual resources include:
>
> - paragraphs;
> - headings;
> - lists;
> - punctuation;
> - typography.
>
> Because immediate feedback may be unavailable, writers may make relationships explicit through:
>
> - cohesive devices;
> - metadiscourse;
> - definitions;
> - examples;
> - carefully managed reference.
>
> > [!important]- Written Discourse Is Highly Variable
> > Compare:
> >
> > - a research article;
> > - a personal note;
> > - an online comment;
> > - an instructional handout;
> > - a novel.
> >
> > These differ substantially in:
> >
> > - purpose;
> > - audience;
> > - stance;
> > - degree of planning.
>
> > [!note]- Lexical Density
> > Written discourse may display greater lexical density and syntactic embedding.
> >
> > However, this tendency is especially associated with **formal expository writing**, not all written language.
>
> > [!important]- Writing Can Also Be Interactive
> > Writers can construct interaction through:
> >
> > - questions;
> > - directives;
> > - evaluation;
> > - hedging;
> > - references to the reader.
> >
> > Written language should therefore not automatically be treated as socially isolated or purely monologic.

---

> [!example]- 🧱 Visual Organization as Meaning
> The visual organization of writing contributes to discourse meaning.
>
> **Paragraph boundaries**
>
> → propose units of thought.
>
> **Headings**
>
> → forecast macrostructure.
>
> **Citation practices**
>
> → position the writer in relation to a disciplinary community.
>
> **Punctuation and formatting**
>
> → can signal emphasis or hierarchy.
>
> An analysis of written discourse should therefore consider the **whole communicative design**, including multimodal elements when relevant.

> [!example]- 5️⃣ Purpose, Audience, and Genre
> The same topic can be realized through very different genres.
>
> A teacher might explain the same grammar point through:
>
> - a lecture;
> - a textbook page;
> - a feedback comment;
> - a video caption;
> - a private message.
>
> The language changes because:
>
> - the purpose changes;
> - the audience relationship changes;
> - time constraints change;
> - expectations change.
>
> **Genre therefore provides a bridge between textual form and social context.**

---

> [!warning]- ⚠️ Do Not Classify Purpose from Content Alone
> Classification should be **evidence-based**.
>
> A text containing information is not automatically primarily informative.
>
> For example:
>
> **An advertisement**
>
> may provide considerable information while primarily attempting to **persuade**.
>
> A conversation may shift from:
>
> **interactional small talk**
>
> to:
>
> **transactional problem-solving**.
>
> A classroom explanation may simultaneously:
>
> - transmit content;
> - establish teacher authority;
> - encourage participation;
> - manage time.
>
> > [!important]- Multiple Purposes Can Coexist
> > A discourse event may serve several purposes at once.
> >
> > However, one purpose may become dominant at a particular moment.

---

> [!example]- 6️⃣ Audience Design
> Producers make assumptions about what recipients:
>
> - know;
> - value;
> - need.
>
> These assumptions influence:
>
> - pronoun choice;
> - explanation;
> - terminology;
> - politeness;
> - explicitness.
>
> When assumptions about the audience are inaccurate, communication may fail.
>
> > [!tip]- ELT Application
> > Instead of having learners memorize decontextualized lists of "formal" and "informal" expressions, compare how **similar content is adapted for different audiences**.

---

> [!example]- 7️⃣ From Discourse Analysis to ELT Practice
> A discourse-informed teacher can use authentic samples to help learners notice patterns without turning those patterns into simplistic rules.
>
> > [!example]- Listening
> > Ask learners to identify how speakers:
> >
> > - signal topic changes;
> > - repair misunderstandings;
> > - check understanding.
>
> > [!example]- Writing
> > Compare how two different genres introduce the same topic.
>
> > [!example]- Speaking
> > Practice ways to:
> >
> > - enter a conversation;
> > - clarify meaning;
> > - close an exchange.
>
> > [!example]- Assessment
> > Assessment criteria can include:
> >
> > - coherence;
> > - audience awareness;
> > - pragmatic appropriateness;
> > - genre fulfillment;
> > - grammatical accuracy.

---

> [!warning]- 📚 Evaluate Teaching Materials Critically
> Textbook dialogues may be unnaturally complete.
>
> For example:
>
> - every turn may be grammatically polished;
> - nobody overlaps;
> - every answer directly responds to the previous question;
> - hesitation and repair may be absent.
>
> These dialogues can support controlled practice.
>
> However, they should not automatically be presented as neutral examples of natural conversation.
>
> Similarly, model essays may represent one culturally or institutionally valued organizational pattern without acknowledging possible alternatives.

> - paralinguistic features;
> - genre;

> - audience;

>

> Support each claim with specific textual or contextual evidence.

> Do not assume that one sample represents all speech or all writing.

> Ask:
>
> **What might a language learner need to notice, practice, or understand?**

> [!note]- 📋 The 12-Feature Analytical Framework
>
> > [!note]- 1. Mode
> > How is the message produced and received?
> >
> > Example:
> >
> > Spoken:  
> > “Hey, are you coming to class today?”
> >
> > Written:  
> > “Dear Professor, I am writing to confirm whether class will meet today.”
>
> > [!note]- 2. Spontaneity
> > How immediately is the language produced?
> >
> > Example:
> >
> > “Well, um, I think… maybe we should meet tomorrow? Or actually, Friday might be better.”
>
> > [!note]- 3. Planning
> > How much preparation is evident?
> >
> > Example:
> >
> > “Today I will discuss three factors affecting second-language learning: motivation, exposure, and interaction.”
>
> > [!note]- 4. Permanence
> > Can the discourse be revisited?
> >
> > Compare:
> >
> > - a printed university policy;
> > - a casual unrecorded conversation.
>
> > [!note]- 5. Interactivity
> > Can participants respond and influence the discourse?
> >
> > **A:** Did you finish the assignment?  
> > **B:** Almost. What about you?  
> > **A:** Not yet.
>
> > [!note]- 6. Lexical Density
> > Compare:
> >
> > **More lexically dense**
> >
> > “Globalization has significantly transformed patterns of international communication and intercultural interaction.”
> >
> > **Less lexically dense**
> >
> > “People around the world talk to each other much more now.”
>
> > [!note]- 7. Deixis
> > Example:
> >
> > “I'll meet you **here tomorrow** after **she** arrives.”
>
> > [!note]- 8. Paralinguistic Features
> > Example:
> >
> > “That was really helpful.”
> >
> > The meaning changes when delivered:
> >
> > - enthusiastically with a smile;
> > - sarcastically with an eye roll.
>
> > [!note]- 9. Genre
> > Example:
> >
> > “Dear Hiring Committee, I am writing to apply for the position of English instructor…”
> >
> > Genre: **job application letter**
>
> > [!note]- 10. Communicative Purpose
> > Example:
> >
> > “Please submit your research proposal by Friday at 5:00 p.m.”
> >
> > Dominant purpose: **requesting/instructing**
>
> > [!note]- 11. Audience
> > **To a friend**
> >
> > “Hey! Wanna grab lunch?”
> >
> > **To a dean**
> >
> > “Would you be available to meet for lunch next week?”
>
> > [!note]- 12. Hybrid Discourse
> > Example:
> >
> > “Hi everyone! 👋 Just a reminder that the assignment is due tonight. Let me know if you have questions!”
> >
> > The Teams message is written but displays conversational and interactive characteristics.

> [!exercise]- 🧪 Comparative Analysis Task
> Select **two discourse samples**.
>
> Compare them using the 12-feature framework.
>
> You do **not** need to claim that every feature is equally important.
>
> Focus on the dimensions that best explain the communicative differences between the samples.
>

> [!success]- 🧭 Final Takeaway
> **Speech and writing are not two fixed packages of linguistic features.**
>
> Instead, discourse varies along multiple dimensions.
>
> When analyzing a text or interaction, ask:
>
> **How is it produced?**
>
> **How planned is it?**
>
> **How permanent is it?**
>
> **How interactive is it?**
>
> **How much does it rely on shared context?**
>
> **Who is the audience?**
>
> **What is the producer trying to accomplish?**
>
> **What genre expectations shape the language?**
>
> **Does it combine features of several modes?**
>
> The goal is not simply to label discourse as **spoken** or **written**.
>
> The goal is to explain **how linguistic and contextual choices work together to accomplish a communicative purpose**.
