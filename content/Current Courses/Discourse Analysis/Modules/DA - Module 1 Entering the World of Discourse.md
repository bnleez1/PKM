# Discourse Analysis Module 1: Entering the World of Discourse

**⚡ Language, context, and the foundations of discourse analysis**

> [!abstract]- Module Overview
> Discourse analysis begins with a deceptively simple observation: people do not communicate through isolated sentences.
>
> They use language to:
>
> - act,
> - relate,
> - organize knowledge,
> - position themselves, and
> - respond to particular social situations.
>
> During this opening week, you will become familiar with the course expectations while beginning to see language as **situated action** rather than merely a collection of grammatical forms.
>
> The week moves from a diagnostic comparison of spoken and written texts to a historical overview of the field.
>
> It also introduces the course’s transparent approach to generative artificial intelligence. AI may help locate starting points or stimulate questions, but responsibility for:
>
> - interpretation,
> - verification, and
> - analytical judgment
>
> remains with you.
>
> By the end of the week, you should be able to explain why discourse analysis matters to future English teachers and how different traditions have shaped the questions analysts ask.
>

> > 2. How do structural and functional perspectives differ in what they treat as evidence?

> [!book]- Key Concepts and Vocabulary
>
> | Term | Working definition |
> |---|---|
> | **Discourse analysis** | The systematic study of language in use, including how meaning depends on textual, interactional, social, and cultural context. |
> | **Text** | A stretch of spoken, written, or multimodal language treated as an analyzable product. |
> | **Discourse** | Language understood as situated meaning-making, social action, and interaction. |
> | **Context** | The linguistic, situational, social, cultural, and historical conditions that shape interpretation. |
> | **Co-text** | The language immediately surrounding an expression within a text or interaction. |
> | **Structural perspective** | An orientation that examines patterns, units, and formal organization beyond the sentence. |
> | **Functional perspective** | An orientation that asks what language choices accomplish in communicative situations. |
> | **Communicative event** | A socially recognizable occasion in which participants use language for particular purposes. |
> | **ELT** | English language teaching, the professional context in which course concepts will be applied. |
> | **AI planning appendix** | A transparent record of approved AI-assisted brainstorming or resource discovery completed before the student’s independent analysis. |

> [!book]- Main Chapter Content
>
> > [!note]- 1. Language Beyond the Sentence
> > Traditional grammar provides essential tools for describing:
> >
> > - clauses,
> > - phrases,
> > - word classes, and
> > - sentence patterns.
> >
> > Yet successful communication depends on more than grammatical well-formedness.
> >
> > A sequence of correct sentences can still feel:
> >
> > - confusing,
> > - inappropriate,
> > - evasive,
> > - rude, or
> > - disconnected.
> >
> > Conversely, speakers regularly produce fragments, repetitions, pauses, and incomplete clauses that listeners understand without difficulty.
> >
> > Discourse analysis addresses this wider level of meaning. It asks how stretches of language become interpretable as:
> >
> > - explanations,
> > - stories,
> > - requests,
> > - arguments,
> > - lessons,
> > - interviews,
> > - jokes, or
> > - classroom exchanges.
> >
> > The unit of analysis therefore expands.
> >
> > Instead of asking only whether a sentence is correct, an analyst may ask:
> >
> > - Who produced it?
> > - For whom?
> > - In what setting?
> > - After what previous turn?
> > - Through which medium?
> > - With what likely purpose?
> >
> > Consider:
> >
> > > **“That window is still open.”**
> >
> > In a grammar exercise, it is a declarative sentence in the present tense.
> >
> > In different contexts, however, it may function as:
> >
> > - an indirect request in a cold classroom,
> > - a report of procedural failure during a security inspection, or
> > - a device for creating suspense in a story.
> >
> > Its communicative force emerges from the relationship between **linguistic form and context**.
> >
> > This broader view is especially important for future English teachers. Learners need grammatical resources, but they also need to understand how those resources work across connected discourse.
> >
> > They must recognize:
> >
> > - how speakers open and close conversations,
> > - how writers guide readers through arguments,
> > - how politeness is negotiated, and
> > - how genre conventions shape expectations.
> >
> > Discourse analysis makes these normally invisible patterns available for teaching.
>
> > [!note]- 2. Text, Discourse, and Context
> > The terms **text** and **discourse** are sometimes used interchangeably, but a productive working distinction can guide analysis.
> >
> > **Text** can be treated as the observable record:
> >
> > - words,
> > - clauses,
> > - turns,
> > - paragraphs,
> > - images, and
> > - other semiotic elements available for examination.
> >
> > **Discourse** emphasizes the interpretive and social process through which that record becomes meaningful.
> >
> > For example:
> >
> > - A transcript is a **text**.
> > - The negotiation of authority, identity, politeness, or shared knowledge within it is part of its **discourse**.
> >
> > **Context is not decorative background.**
> >
> > It actively constrains interpretation.
> >
> > Different dimensions include:
> >
> > - **Linguistic context / co-text:** what comes before and after an expression.
> > - **Situational context:** participants, location, medium, activity, and immediate purpose.
> > - **Social context:** roles and relationships such as teacher–student, friend–friend, customer–employee, or expert–novice.
> > - **Cultural and historical context:** broader conventions and assumptions that participants may share unevenly.
> >
> > Analysts must avoid treating context as unlimited.
> >
> > An analysis becomes weak when every feature is explained by invoking vague ideas such as “culture” or unknowable intentions.
> >
> > Strong analysis identifies the contextual evidence needed for a specific claim.
> >
> > For example, a change from:
> >
> > > “Could you possibly…?”
> >
> > to:
> >
> > > “Do this now.”
> >
> > may be related to:
> >
> > - power,
> > - urgency,
> > - genre, or
> > - interpersonal distance.
> >
> > However, the analyst must show how the text and situation support that interpretation.
>
> > [!note]- 3. A Brief Map of the Field
> > Discourse analysis developed through several overlapping traditions rather than a single unified theory.
> >
> > **Structural approaches**
> >
> > - Extend linguistic description beyond the sentence.
> > - Examine how larger units are organized.
> >
> > **Functional approaches**
> >
> > Examine what language does, including:
> >
> > - requesting,
> > - informing,
> > - persuading,
> > - evaluating,
> > - maintaining relationships, and
> > - organizing experience.
> >
> > **Pragmatics**
> >
> > Examines meaning in context, including:
> >
> > - implied meanings,
> > - speech acts, and
> > - contextual interpretation.
> >
> > **Conversation analysis**
> >
> > Focuses closely on the sequential organization of naturally occurring talk.
> >
> > **Systemic-functional approaches**
> >
> > Connect grammatical choices to meanings and social functions.
> >
> > **Critical traditions**
> >
> > Examine how discourse participates in:
> >
> > - ideology,
> > - identity,
> > - inequality, and
> > - institutional power.
> >
> > These traditions ask different questions and often work with different kinds of data.
> >
> > For example:
> >
> > - A **structural analysis** might identify cohesive links across a paragraph.
> > - A **functional analysis** might explain how those links support an argument.
> > - A **pragmatic analysis** might examine an indirect request.
> > - A **conversation analyst** might examine how the next speaker responds.
> > - A **critical analysis** might ask whose interests are served by institutional interactional norms.
> >
> > The purpose of the historical timeline is **not** to memorize a parade of names.
> >
> > The purpose is to notice how definitions of language and evidence have broadened.
> >
> > Each approach highlights some features while backgrounding others.
> >
> > Future teachers benefit from methodological flexibility because different classroom problems require different analytical lenses.
>
> > [!note]- 4. Discourse Analysis as Professional Knowledge for ELT
> > Discourse analysis contributes to English language teaching in at least four ways.
> >
> > **1. Material selection**
> >
> > Teachers can evaluate:
> >
> > - whether dialogues sound interactionally plausible,
> > - whether reading passages display expected organizational patterns, and
> > - whether model texts represent realistic genres.
> >
> > **2. Instruction**
> >
> > Teachers can make visible:
> >
> > - cohesive devices,
> > - thematic progression,
> > - speech acts,
> > - conversational repair, and
> > - rhetorical patterns.
> >
> > **3. Classroom interaction**
> >
> > Teachers can examine:
> >
> > - wait time,
> > - questioning patterns,
> > - turn allocation,
> > - feedback moves, and
> > - the balance between transactional instruction and relationship-building talk.
> >
> > **4. Assessment**
> >
> > Instead of evaluating only sentence-level accuracy, teachers can examine:
> >
> > - coherence,
> > - register,
> > - stance,
> > - audience awareness,
> > - pragmatic appropriateness, and
> > - management of extended turns.
> >
> > Professional application requires caution.
> >
> > An analyst should not:
> >
> > - present one sample as representative of an entire community,
> > - confuse personal preference with a discourse norm, or
> > - prescribe a single “correct” style when multiple conventions are legitimate.
> >
> > The goal is to use analysis to **expand learners’ choices and interpretive awareness**, not to erase identities or enforce unexamined native-speaker norms.
>
> > [!note]- 5. Transparent AI-Supported Planning
> > This week introduces **Level 2 AI use: planning support**.
> >
> > An approved GenAI tool may help:
> >
> > - brainstorm historical theories,
> > - propose search terms, or
> > - locate possible authentic samples.
> >
> > It may **not** replace:
> >
> > - student reading,
> > - source evaluation, or
> > - analytical conclusions.
> >
> > A plausible AI response is not automatically an accurate response.
> >
> > Models may:
> >
> > - merge traditions,
> > - invent references,
> > - oversimplify debates, or
> > - describe non-existent examples.
> >
> > The **AI planning appendix** makes the process visible.
> >
> > Record:
> >
> > 1. the exact prompt,
> > 2. useful ideas returned,
> > 3. sources independently checked, and
> > 4. decisions you made.
> >
> > Distinguish **discovery from evidence**.
> >
> > An AI suggestion may point toward a scholar or concept, but an assigned text or verified publication must support the claim.
> >
> > Also record rejected suggestions and explain why they were unsuitable.
> >
> > Your shared knowledge base in **Capacities.io** should similarly show relationships rather than a copied list.
> >
> > Connect each historical orientation to:
> >
> > - typical questions,
> > - preferred data,
> > - analytical concepts, and
> > - potential ELT applications.
> >
> > The final map should represent **your understanding**.
> >
> > Technology supports organization; it does not supply ownership of the ideas.
