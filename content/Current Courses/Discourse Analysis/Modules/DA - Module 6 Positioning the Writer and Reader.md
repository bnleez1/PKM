# Discourse Analysis Module 6: Positioning the Writer and Reader

> [!abstract]- ⚡ Module Overview
> **Modality, hedging, register, and an integrated AI-supported revision trail**
>
> Every extended text positions its producer in relation to the message and the audience.
> Writers and speakers express certainty, possibility, obligation, willingness, evaluation,
> and distance through modal verbs, adverbs, reporting structures, lexical choices, and
> patterns of attribution.
>
> These resources construct **stance** and **interpersonal meaning**. They also interact
> with cohesion, thematic progression, tense, aspect, and register.
>
> This week brings Unit II together through an integrated analysis. You will first produce
> a fully human draft, then use an approved GenAI tool as a **Level 3 critical friend**
> to review academic clarity, hedging, and register.
>
> The graded evidence is not an AI-polished product alone; it is the **annotated revision
> trail** showing:
>
> - what changed,
> - what you accepted,
> - what you rejected, and
> - why.
>
> Your rationale should demonstrate ownership, disciplinary judgment, and attention to
> meaning rather than automatic compliance.
>
> > [!question]- Essential Understandings
> > 1. How do modality and related stance resources construct authority, caution,
> >    obligation, and interpersonal relationships?
> > 2. How can AI feedback support revision without replacing the analyst’s judgment
> >    or voice?
>
> > [!question]- Guiding Questions
> > - What degrees and types of modality are expressed in a text, and through which
> >   linguistic resources?
> > - How does stance vary across academic, institutional, and everyday genres?
> > - How do cohesion, thematic progression, tense–aspect, modality, and register interact?
> > - What evidence justifies accepting, adapting, or rejecting an AI-generated suggestion?
>
> > [!success]- Learning Outcomes
> > By the end of this module, you should be able to:
> >
> > - Identify modal verbs and other stance resources in extended discourse.
> > - Interpret degrees of certainty, obligation, permission, willingness, evaluation,
> >   and interpersonal distance.
> > - Produce an integrated analysis of cohesion, information flow, tense–aspect,
> >   modality, and register.
> > - Use GenAI as a critical friend only after completing an independent draft.
> > - Create an annotated revision trail that explains pedagogical and analytical
> >   reasons for each substantive decision.

> [!book]- Key Concepts and Vocabulary
>
> | Term | Working definition |
> |---|---|
> | **Modality** | Linguistic resources that express judgments about possibility, necessity, obligation, permission, willingness, or related meanings. |
> | **Epistemic modality** | Judgments about likelihood, certainty, evidence, or knowledge. |
> | **Deontic modality** | Judgments about obligation, permission, or necessity in relation to action. |
> | **Stance** | The producer’s positioning toward the proposition, audience, evidence, or social situation. |
> | **Hedging** | Reducing categorical force to express caution, limitation, politeness, or appropriate uncertainty. |
> | **Boosting** | Increasing commitment or emphasis through expressions of certainty or importance. |
> | **Register** | A configuration of linguistic choices associated with a situation, purpose, participant relationship, and mode. |
> | **Interpersonal meaning** | How language enacts roles, relationships, attitudes, and degrees of alignment. |
> | **Critical friend** | A reviewer who offers questions and feedback while leaving responsibility and decision-making with the author. |
> | **Revision trail** | A documented comparison of drafts, changes, feedback, and rationales that makes the revision process visible. |

> [!book]- Main Chapter Content
>
> > [!note]- 1. Modality as a System of Choices
> > Modal verbs such as **may, might, can, could, must, should,** and **will** are
> > prominent resources, but modality extends beyond them.
> >
> > Adverbs such as **probably** and **certainly**, adjectives such as **possible**
> > and **essential**, reporting verbs such as **suggest** and **demonstrate**, and
> > phrases such as **it appears that** all position a claim.
> >
> > The analyst should therefore examine a **network of choices** rather than
> > underline modal auxiliaries only.
> >
> > **Epistemic modality** concerns commitment to truth or likelihood.
> >
> > - *The pattern may indicate...* presents a possibility.
> > - *The pattern clearly demonstrates...* presents strong commitment.
> >
> > **Deontic modality** concerns action, obligation, and permission.
> >
> > - *Students must submit...* constructs an institutional requirement.
> > - *Students may consult...* grants permission or possibility depending on context.
> >
> > Meanings are not fixed to forms. **Must** can express obligation or a strong
> > inference. **Can** can express ability, possibility, or permission.
> >
> > Intonation and context further influence interpretation in speech.
> >
> > A discourse analysis should:
> >
> > 1. identify the form,
> > 2. propose the likely meaning, and
> > 3. explain how the choice positions producer and audience within the genre.
>
> > [!note]- 2. Hedging, Boosting, and Responsible Claims
> > Academic hedging is sometimes misunderstood as weak writing.
> >
> > Appropriate hedging matches the strength of a claim to the available evidence.
> > A small classroom sample may support:
> >
> > *The findings suggest...*
> >
> > but not necessarily:
> >
> > *The findings prove....*
> >
> > Expressions of limitation, probability, attribution, and scope help writers avoid
> > overgeneralization. They also create space for disciplinary dialogue.
> >
> > **Boosters** such as *clearly*, *undoubtedly*, and *demonstrate* strengthen
> > commitment. They can emphasize well-supported conclusions, but overuse creates
> > an inflated or defensive tone.
> >
> > Under-commitment can also be a problem. A writer who qualifies every sentence
> > with *maybe* and *perhaps* may obscure a conclusion that the evidence reasonably
> > supports.
> >
> > Stance is relational:
> >
> > - *You must revise this section.*
> > - *This section could be clarified by...*
> >
> > These position the producer differently in relation to the reader.
> >
> > The appropriate choice depends on:
> >
> > - authority,
> > - purpose,
> > - urgency, and
> > - institutional norms.
> >
> > An instructor’s rubric, a peer comment, a research article, and a personal
> > reflection draw on different stance resources.
> >
> > The analyst evaluates **fit** rather than labeling one form universally polite
> > or formal.
>
> > [!note]- 3. Register and the Integrated Text
> > Register connects linguistic choices to the situation.
> >
> > - **Field** concerns what activity or content is taking place.
> > - **Tenor** concerns participant roles and relationships.
> > - **Mode** concerns how language functions through spoken, written, or
> >   multimodal channels.
> >
> > These dimensions help explain why a text uses particular:
> >
> > - vocabulary,
> > - grammatical density,
> > - pronouns,
> > - directives,
> > - hedges, or
> > - interactional markers.
> >
> > Unit II concepts interact:
> >
> > - Reference chains and lexical cohesion maintain topics.
> > - Theme–rheme choices manage the flow of information.
> > - Tense and aspect construct temporal perspective.
> > - Modality and evaluation position claims and relationships.
> > - Register provides a contextual explanation for why the configuration is
> >   appropriate—or why it clashes with audience expectations.
> >
> > An integrated analysis should **not become a checklist of unrelated labels**.
> > Select the patterns most relevant to the text’s communicative work.
> >
> > For example, an academic paragraph may use:
> >
> > - a constant theme to maintain the study as topic,
> > - present tense to state interpretations,
> > - lexical chains to develop *feedback*, and
> > - epistemic hedges to limit claims.
> >
> > The goal is to explain how these patterns work together to construct a cautious
> > analytical voice.
>
> > [!note]- 4. Using AI as a Level 3 Critical Friend
> > The workflow begins with a **complete human-written analysis**.
> >
> > This order protects the assessment’s purpose: the student must first demonstrate
> > noticing, interpretation, and explanation.
> >
> > Afterward, an approved GenAI tool may review:
> >
> > - clarity,
> > - register,
> > - hedging,
> > - organization, or
> > - sentence-level expression.
> >
> > A useful prompt defines the role narrowly. Ask the tool to identify:
> >
> > - passages that sound categorical without sufficient evidence,
> > - unclear references, or
> > - shifts in register.
> >
> > Require the tool to **explain rather than rewrite everything**.
> >
> > AI feedback must be treated as a proposal.
> >
> > Models may:
> >
> > - flatten the writer’s voice,
> > - add unnecessary hedges,
> > - replace precise course terminology,
> > - make a claim sound more authoritative without improving evidence, or
> > - misunderstand the analyzed text because they lack the original context.
> >
> > Verify every suggestion against:
> >
> > 1. the source text,
> > 2. course concepts,
> > 3. assignment criteria, and
> > 4. intended meaning.
> >
> > A critical-friend process is dialogic. You can ask the model why it suggested a
> > change, request alternatives, or challenge its interpretation.
> >
> > The goal is **not** to produce the most polished-sounding prose. The goal is to
> > improve the accuracy and communicative effectiveness of your own analysis.
>
> > [!note]- 5. Building an Annotated Revision Trail
> > The revision trail should include:
> >
> > - the original draft,
> > - the AI-reviewed version or feedback,
> > - tracked changes, and
> > - commentary.
> >
> > For each substantive change, state whether you:
> >
> > - **accepted**,
> > - **adapted**, or
> > - **rejected**
> >
> > the suggestion.
> >
> > Then explain the reason.
> >
> > **Example rationale**
> >
> > *I rejected the replacement of may suggest with proves because the sample includes
> > only one interaction.*
> >
> > **Another example**
> >
> > *I accepted the clearer antecedent because the original pronoun could refer to
> > either participant.*
> >
> > Commentary should address **meaning**, not merely preference.
> >
> > *This sounds better* is insufficient.
> >
> > Explain effects on:
> >
> > - clarity,
> > - register,
> > - stance,
> > - coherence,
> > - accuracy, or
> > - audience.
> >
> > Also identify places where the AI:
> >
> > - failed to notice a problem, or
> > - introduced a new one.
> >
> > Before submission:
> >
> > 1. Read the final analysis without the tracked changes.
> > 2. Confirm that it still represents your voice.
> > 3. Confirm that every analytical claim is supported.
> > 4. Audit the revision trail for transparency.
> > 5. Make sure prompts are included.
> > 6. Make AI contributions identifiable.
> > 7. Verify sources.
> > 8. Confirm that the final decisions are yours.
> >
> > This documented process is the central evidence of responsible **Level 3 use**.
>
> > [!note]- 6. An Integrated Analytical Sequence
> > For the Unit II task, work from **global purpose toward local form**.
> >
> > 1. State the genre, audience, and communicative purpose.
> > 2. Identify the dominant topic and map the text’s paragraph progression.
> > 3. Trace one reference or lexical chain that sustains the topic.
> > 4. Diagram the themes of a representative paragraph.
> > 5. Identify a tense–aspect pattern and a cluster of stance resources.
> > 6. Explain how these choices fit—or fail to fit—the register.
> >
> > The written analysis should group features by **interpretive claim** rather than
> > textbook category.
> >
> > One paragraph might argue that the writer creates **cautious authority**.
> > Evidence could include:
> >
> > - epistemic modals,
> > - reporting verbs,
> > - attribution, and
> > - thematic placement of source-based claims.
> >
> > Another paragraph might argue that the text guides the reader poorly.
> > Evidence could include:
> >
> > - unstable reference,
> > - abrupt thematic shifts, and
> > - a connector that signals an unsupported relation.
> >
> > Before consulting AI, write a **self-review memo** identifying two uncertainties.
> >
> > After receiving critical-friend feedback, compare the model’s attention with
> > your own:
> >
> > - Did it notice discourse-level issues?
> > - Did it focus mainly on sentence polish?
> >
> > Include that comparison in the revision trail.
> >
> > This turns AI use into an **object of analysis** as well as a source of feedback.

> > [!example]- Tuesday–Thursday: Text 1 — The Missing Presentation File
> > On Monday morning, Professor Reyes arrived early for her research seminar.
> > Before class began, one of the students approached her with a problem.
> >
> > Daniel had prepared the group's presentation the night before, but he could
> > not find the final version on his laptop.
> >
> > Professor Reyes gave Daniel her USB drive and asked him to check whether he
> > had saved a copy there during the previous class. A few minutes later, another
> > student found the file in the shared course folder and sent the file to Daniel.
> >
> > Relieved, Daniel opened the presentation and began checking the slides.
> >
> > Before the seminar started, Professor Reyes handed the students a short
> > evaluation form. She also handed a copy of the presentation guidelines to each
> > group leader because several groups had asked about the assessment criteria.
> >
> > During the presentation, Daniel realized that one slide contained outdated
> > information. He quickly took the slide out of the presentation. Later, his
> > partner noticed another unnecessary example and told him to take it out as well.
> >
> > After class, Professor Reyes asked what had happened. Daniel explained the
> > situation and promised to organize his files more carefully. She smiled and said, “Next time, send me the final version before class.” Daniel agreed and immediately sent it to her.

> > [!example]- Tuesday–Thursday: Text 3 — Rethinking the University Library
> > For many students, the university library is primarily a place to find books
> > and study quietly.
> >
> > Recently, however, its role has begun to change. There are now several
> > collaborative areas on the first floor, and students can use them for group
> > projects, tutoring sessions, and informal discussions.
> >
> > Last year, university administrators asked students what improvements they
> > wanted.
> >
> > **What many students requested was more flexible study space.**
> >
> > They explained that traditional silent areas were useful for individual work
> > but less appropriate for collaborative assignments.
> >
> > **What the university decided to create was a combination of quiet and
> > interactive spaces.**
> >
> > The changes have not satisfied everyone. There have been several complaints
> > about noise, particularly during examination periods.
> >
> > Some students argue that collaboration belongs elsewhere on campus. Others
> > believe the library should support different ways of learning.
> >
> > **What seems to matter most is how the different spaces are managed.**
> >
> > On the upper floors, silence remains the expectation. On the first floor,
> > conversation is permitted within designated areas.
> >
> > There is also a new reservation system for group rooms, which has reduced
> > conflicts between students.
> >
> > The library is therefore no longer organized around a single idea of studying.
> >
> > **What the redesign demonstrates is that one building can support several kinds
> > of academic activity.**
> >
> > > [!tip]- Analysis Focus
> > > This text deliberately emphasizes two marked constructions:
> > >
> > > - **existential _there_** for introducing information into the discourse;
> > > - **wh-clefts** for focusing or contrasting particular information.
> > >
> > > Review **Marked Constructions as Discourse Resources**.
