# Discourse Analysis Module 6: Positioning the Writer and Reader

⚡️ Modality, hedging, register, and an integrated AI-supported revision trail

> Every extended text positions its producer in relation to the message and the audience. Writers and speakers express certainty, possibility, obligation, willingness, evaluation, and distance through modal verbs, adverbs, reporting structures, lexical choices, and patterns of attribution. These resources construct stance and interpersonal meaning. They also interact with cohesion, thematic progression, tense, aspect, and register. This week brings Unit II together through an integrated analysis. You will first produce a fully human draft, then use an approved GenAI tool as a Level 3 critical friend to review academic clarity, hedging, and register. The graded evidence is not an AI-polished product alone; it is the annotated revision trail showing what changed, what you accepted or rejected, and why. Your rationale should demonstrate ownership, disciplinary judgment, and attention to meaning rather than automatic compliance.
## Essential understanding(s)

1. How do modality and related stance resources construct authority, caution, obligation, and interpersonal relationships?
2. How can AI feedback support revision without replacing the analyst’s judgment or voice?
## Guiding Questions

What degrees and types of modality are expressed in a text, and through which linguistic resources?

How does stance vary across academic, institutional, and everyday genres?

How do cohesion, thematic progression, tense–aspect, modality, and register interact?

What evidence justifies accepting, adapting, or rejecting an AI-generated suggestion?

## Learning outcomes

Identify modal verbs and other stance resources in extended discourse.

Interpret degrees of certainty, obligation, permission, willingness, evaluation, and interpersonal distance.

Produce an integrated analysis of cohesion, information flow, tense–aspect, modality, and register.

Use GenAI as a critical friend only after completing an independent draft.

Create an annotated revision trail that explains pedagogical and analytical reasons for each substantive decision.

## Key concepts and vocabulary

| **Term**                  | **Working definition**                                                                                                              |
| :------------------------ | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Modality**              | Linguistic resources that express judgments about possibility, necessity, obligation, permission, willingness, or related meanings. |
| **Epistemic modality**    | Judgments about likelihood, certainty, evidence, or knowledge.                                                                      |
| **Deontic modality**      | Judgments about obligation, permission, or necessity in relation to action.                                                         |
| **Stance**                | The producer’s positioning toward the proposition, audience, evidence, or social situation.                                         |
| **Hedging**               | Reducing categorical force to express caution, limitation, politeness, or appropriate uncertainty.                                  |
| **Boosting**              | Increasing commitment or emphasis through expressions of certainty or importance.                                                   |
| **Register**              | A configuration of linguistic choices associated with a situation, purpose, participant relationship, and mode.                     |
| **Interpersonal meaning** | How language enacts roles, relationships, attitudes, and degrees of alignment.                                                      |
| **Critical friend**       | A reviewer who offers questions and feedback while leaving responsibility and decision-making with the author.                      |
| **Revision trail**        | A documented comparison of drafts, changes, feedback, and rationales that makes the revision process visible.                       |

## Main chapter content

1. Modality as a system of choices
Modal verbs such as may, might, can, could, must, should, and will are prominent resources, but modality extends beyond them. Adverbs such as probably and certainly, adjectives such as possible and essential, reporting verbs such as suggest and demonstrate, and phrases such as it appears that all position a claim. The analyst should therefore examine a network of choices rather than underline modal auxiliaries only.
Epistemic modality concerns commitment to truth or likelihood. “The pattern may indicate…” presents a possibility; “The pattern clearly demonstrates…” presents strong commitment. Deontic modality concerns action, obligation, and permission. “Students must submit…” constructs an institutional requirement; “Students may consult…” grants permission or possibility depending on context.
Meanings are not fixed to forms. Must can express obligation or a strong inference. Can can express ability, possibility, or permission. Intonation and context further influence interpretation in speech. A discourse analysis identifies the form, proposes the likely meaning, and explains how the choice positions producer and audience within the genre.

2. Hedging, boosting, and responsible claims
Academic hedging is sometimes misunderstood as weak writing. Appropriate hedging matches the strength of a claim to the available evidence. A small classroom sample may support “The findings suggest…” but not “The findings prove….” Expressions of limitation, probability, attribution, and scope help writers avoid overgeneralization. They also create space for disciplinary dialogue.
Boosters such as clearly, undoubtedly, and demonstrate strengthen commitment. They can emphasize well-supported conclusions, but overuse creates an inflated or defensive tone. Under-commitment can also be a problem. A writer who qualifies every sentence with maybe and perhaps may obscure a conclusion that the evidence reasonably supports.
Stance is relational. “You must revise this section” positions the producer differently from “This section could be clarified by…” The appropriate choice depends on authority, purpose, urgency, and institutional norms. An instructor’s rubric, a peer comment, a research article, and a personal reflection draw on different stance resources. The analyst evaluates fit rather than labeling one form universally polite or formal.

3. Register and the integrated text
Register connects linguistic choices to the situation. Field concerns what activity or content is taking place; tenor concerns participant roles and relationships; mode concerns how language functions through spoken, written, or multimodal channels. These dimensions help explain why a text uses particular vocabulary, grammatical density, pronouns, directives, hedges, or interactional markers.
Unit II concepts interact. Reference chains and lexical cohesion maintain topics. Theme–rheme choices manage the flow of information. Tense and aspect construct temporal perspective. Modality and evaluation position claims and relationships. Register provides a contextual explanation for why the configuration is appropriate—or why it clashes with audience expectations.
An integrated analysis should not become a checklist of unrelated labels. Select the patterns most relevant to the text’s communicative work. For example, an academic paragraph may use a constant theme to maintain the study as topic, present tense to state interpretations, lexical chains to develop “feedback,” and epistemic hedges to limit claims. Explain how the pattern works together to construct a cautious analytical voice.

4. Using AI as a Level 3 critical friend
The workflow begins with a complete human-written analysis. This order protects the assessment’s purpose: the student must first demonstrate noticing, interpretation, and explanation. Afterward, an approved GenAI tool may review clarity, register, hedging, organization, or sentence-level expression. A useful prompt defines the role narrowly: ask the tool to identify passages that sound categorical without sufficient evidence, unclear references, or shifts in register, and require it to explain rather than rewrite everything.
AI feedback must be treated as a proposal. Models may flatten the writer’s voice, add unnecessary hedges, replace precise course terminology, or make a claim sound more authoritative without improving evidence. They may also misunderstand the analyzed text because they lack the original context. The student should verify every suggestion against the source text, course concepts, assignment criteria, and intended meaning.
A critical-friend process is dialogic. You can ask the model why it suggested a change, request alternatives, or challenge its interpretation. The goal is not to produce the most polished-sounding prose; it is to improve the accuracy and communicative effectiveness of your own analysis.

5. Building an annotated revision trail
The revision trail should include the original draft, the AI-reviewed version or feedback, tracked changes, and commentary. For each substantive change, state whether you accepted, adapted, or rejected the suggestion. Then explain the reason. Strong rationales refer to evidence: “I rejected the replacement of may suggest with proves because the sample includes only one interaction,” or “I accepted the clearer antecedent because the original pronoun could refer to either participant.”
Commentary should address meaning, not merely preference. “This sounds better” is insufficient. Explain effects on clarity, register, stance, coherence, accuracy, or audience. Also identify places where the AI failed to notice a problem or introduced a new one. Such moments demonstrate critical evaluation.
Before submission, read the final analysis without the tracked changes. Confirm that it still represents your voice and that every analytical claim is supported. Then audit the trail for transparency: prompts are included, AI contributions are identifiable, sources are verified, and the final decisions are yours. This documented process is the central evidence of responsible Level 3 use.

6. An integrated analytical sequence
For the Unit II task, work from global purpose toward local form. First state the genre, audience, and communicative purpose. Second, identify the dominant topic and map the text’s paragraph progression. Third, trace one reference or lexical chain that sustains the topic. Fourth, diagram the themes of a representative paragraph. Fifth, identify a tense–aspect pattern and a cluster of stance resources. Finally, explain how these choices fit—or fail to fit—the register.
The written analysis should group features by interpretive claim rather than by textbook category. One paragraph might argue that the writer creates cautious authority; evidence could include epistemic modals, reporting verbs, attribution, and thematic placement of source-based claims. Another paragraph might argue that the text guides the reader poorly; evidence could include unstable reference, abrupt thematic shifts, and a connector that signals an unsupported relation.
Before consulting AI, write a self-review memo identifying two uncertainties. After receiving critical-friend feedback, compare the model’s attention with your own. Did it notice discourse-level issues or focus mainly on sentence polish? Include that comparison in the revision trail. This turns AI use into an object of analysis as well as a source of feedback.

### Text 1: the Missing Presentation File

On Monday morning, Professor Reyes arrived early for her research seminar. Before class began, one of the students approached her with a problem. Daniel had prepared the group's presentation the night before, but he could not find the final version on his laptop.

Professor Reyes **<mark style="background:#d3f8b6">gave Daniel her USB drive</mark>** and asked him to check whether he had saved a copy there during the previous class. A few minutes later, another student found the file in the shared course folder and <mark style="background:#d3f8b6">**sent the file to Daniel**</mark>. Relieved, Daniel opened the presentation and began checking the slides.

Before the seminar started, Professor Reyes <mark style="background:#d3f8b6">**handed the students a short evaluation form**</mark>. She also <mark style="background:#d3f8b6">**handed a copy of the presentation guidelines to each group leader**</mark> because several groups had asked about the assessment criteria.

During the presentation, Daniel realized that one slide contained outdated information. He quickly <mark style="background:#d3f8b6">**took the slide out**</mark> of the presentation. Later, his partner noticed another unnecessary example and told him to **take it out** as well.

After class, Professor Reyes asked what had happened. Daniel explained the situation and promised to organize his files more carefully. She smiled and said, “Next time, <mark style="background:#d3f8b6">**send me the final version** before class</mark>.” Daniel agreed and immediately **sent it to her**.

### Text 3: Rethinking the University Library

> [!note] Note
> Review [[Marked Constructions as Discourse Resources]].

For many students, the university library is primarily a place to find books and study quietly. Recently, however, its role has begun to change. <mark style="background:#d3f8b6">**There are now several collaborative areas on the first floor**</mark>, and students can use them for group projects, tutoring sessions, and informal discussions.

Last year, university administrators asked students what improvements they wanted. <mark style="background:#d3f8b6">**What many students requested was more flexible study space</mark>.** They explained that traditional silent areas were useful for individual work but less appropriate for collaborative assignments. <mark style="background:#d3f8b6">**What the university decided to create was a combination of quiet and interactive spaces.**</mark>

The changes have not satisfied everyone. <mark style="background:#d3f8b6">**There have been several complaints about noise**</mark>, particularly during examination periods. Some students argue that collaboration belongs elsewhere on campus. Others believe the library should support different ways of learning.

<mark style="background:#d3f8b6">**What seems to matter most is how the different spaces are managed.** </mark>On the upper floors, silence remains the expectation. On the first floor, conversation is permitted within designated areas. <mark style="background:#d3f8b6">**There is also a new reservation system for group rooms**,</mark> which has reduced conflicts between students.

The library is therefore no longer organized around a single idea of studying. <mark style="background:#d3f8b6">What the redesign demonstrates is that one building can support several kinds of academic activity.</mark>

The third text deliberately emphasizes the two marked constructions highlighted in the reading: **existential _there_** for introducing information into the discourse and **wh-clefts** for focusing or contrasting particular information.
