# DA - Chapter 10: From Sound to Evidence


⚡️ Transcribing natural interaction and building a defensible conversational coding scheme

> A transcript is not a neutral copy of speech. It is an analytical representation produced through selective decisions. The transcriber chooses where turns begin, which pauses matter, how overlap is displayed, whether intonation or emphasis is marked, and what contextual information is included. Those choices shape what later analysts can notice. This week you will create a short transcript from a publicly available audio or video clip and then code speech acts, adjacency pairs, turn-taking signals, repair, and conversational breakdowns. The aim is not maximum notation. It is an accurate, transparent, and fit-for-purpose record that preserves evidence relevant to the research question. You will also peer-check another transcript, compare interpretations, and refine a coding scheme that distinguishes observable features from analytical claims.


## Essential understanding(s)


How does transcription transform an interaction into analyzable evidence?

What makes a coding decision transparent, reliable, and adequately supported?

## Guiding Questions


Which features of timing, overlap, emphasis, and context are necessary for the analytical purpose?

How can a transcriber balance readability and detail?

What is the difference between describing a feature and interpreting its function?

How should disagreements between coders be documented and resolved?

## Learning outcomes

Select a short, ethically and publicly available spoken-interaction sample.

Produce a readable transcript marking analytically relevant turns, pauses, overlap, emphasis, and contextual information.

Apply a defined coding scheme to speech acts, adjacency pairs, turn-taking, and repair.

Identify conversational breakdowns and propose evidence-based explanations.

Use peer checking to revise transcription and coding decisions transparently.

## Key concepts and vocabulary

| **Term**                     | **Working definition**                                                                                      |
| :--------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Transcription**            | The selective representation of recorded interaction in written or symbolic form.                           |
| **Transcription convention** | An agreed notation for representing features such as pauses, overlap, stress, or cut-offs.                  |
| **Turn**                     | A participant’s contribution to an interaction, bounded analytically by speaker change or action structure. |
| **Pause**                    | A period of silence whose placement and duration may be interactionally relevant.                           |
| **Prosody**                  | Rhythm, stress, pitch, intonation, and related vocal features.                                              |
| **Coding scheme**            | A defined set of categories and decision rules for labeling data.                                           |
| **Unit of analysis**         | The segment or phenomenon treated as the primary object of coding.                                          |
| **Inter-coder agreement**    | The extent to which analysts independently apply categories in the same way.                                |
| **Analytic memo**            | A note documenting reasoning, uncertainty, patterns, and decisions during analysis.                         |
| **Conversational breakdown** | A disruption in mutual understanding, action progression, or task completion.                               |

## Main chapter content

1. Transcription is an interpretive practice
Recorded interaction contains more information than any transcript can capture. Speech includes timing, pitch, volume, voice quality, gesture, gaze, posture, movement, and material surroundings. A transcript selects among these features according to an analytical purpose. A study of turn-taking needs overlap and timing; a study of reference may need gesture and visual context; a speech-act analysis may prioritize sequence and prosody.
This selectivity should be explicit. State the source, clip length, participants as far as ethically appropriate, setting, and transcription conventions. Do not imply that the transcript is the interaction itself. It is one representation created for a particular inquiry.
Repeated listening is essential. The first pass establishes the broad sequence. Later passes check wording, boundaries, timing, and ambiguous moments. Headphones and slowed playback may help, but the transcriber should not overclaim inaudible material. Mark uncertainty rather than inventing certainty.

2. Choosing fit-for-purpose conventions
A practical course transcript can use a limited set of conventions: numbered turns; speaker labels; short and timed pauses; overlap brackets; cut-offs; lengthening; emphasis; uncertain hearing; and relevant nonverbal actions. The exact symbols matter less than consistent use and a clear key.
Too little detail can erase evidence. Writing every contribution as a polished sentence removes hesitation, self-repair, and timing. Too much detail can make the transcript inaccessible and shift effort away from the course concepts. Include a feature when it contributes to the analytical question or prevents a misleading interpretation.
Punctuation is especially influential. A period, comma, or question mark can imply grammatical and intonational boundaries. Listen for delivery rather than punctuating only according to written norms. If the speaker’s intonation makes a declarative form function as a question, represent that feature in a way your key explains.

3. Designing a coding scheme
A coding scheme translates course concepts into operational categories. Each code should have a name, definition, inclusion criteria, exclusion criteria, and one or more examples. “Request” should not simply mean any sentence with please. The definition should address the action of seeking another participant’s behavior, and the decision rule should require contextual or sequential evidence.
Codes may operate at different levels. A turn can receive a speech-act code, a sequence code, and a turn-taking code. Keep these layers distinct so the analysis does not confuse action with form. For example, a pause is an observable timing feature; treating it as hesitation or resistance is an interpretation requiring additional evidence.
Begin with syllabus concepts, test them on a small section, and revise the scheme when categories overlap or fail to capture relevant cases. Record changes in an analytic memo. A good scheme supports consistency without pretending that all interaction is unambiguous.

4. Peer checking and analytical reliability
Peer checking is not a vote that produces an automatically correct answer. It exposes where the transcript or coding rules allow multiple interpretations. Two analysts may hear a word differently, locate a turn boundary differently, or classify an utterance as a request versus a suggestion. These disagreements are valuable data about the clarity of the evidence and definitions.
Compare work independently before discussion. Then identify the exact source of disagreement: hearing, segmentation, category definition, missing context, or theoretical interpretation. Replay the clip and apply the decision rules. If uncertainty remains, preserve it in the analysis instead of forcing agreement.
Reliability is strengthened through transparency. Keep the original version, the revised version, and notes explaining major changes. The objective is not a perfect agreement percentage for a small course task, but disciplined reasoning that another reader can follow.

5. Explaining breakdowns
A conversational breakdown may involve hearing, reference, vocabulary, speech-act recognition, turn timing, or conflicting assumptions about the activity. Locate the moment where progress becomes problematic and examine the turns before and after it. Repair attempts often reveal what participants treated as the source of trouble.
Avoid attributing a breakdown to “poor English” without analysis. A grammatically nonstandard utterance may be fully understood, while a polished utterance may fail pragmatically. Consider noise, institutional roles, unequal knowledge, ambiguous wording, politeness, and listener expectations.
Your final explanation should move through evidence: transcript feature, code, sequential position, participant response, and interpretation. Then propose an ELT implication. Learners might need practice with clarification, confirmation, turn entry, reference, or speech-act variation. The pedagogical recommendation should follow from the documented interaction rather than from a generic speaking lesson.

6. Data-quality checklist and memo
Before coding, complete a data-quality check. Confirm that the clip is short enough for repeated listening, the interaction is publicly available or ethically usable, the audio is intelligible, and the context is sufficient for the analytical question. Record the source and access information without including unnecessary personal data about participants.
After the first transcript, write an analytic memo describing uncertain words, possible turn boundaries, and features you chose not to represent. During peer checking, ask the reviewer to mark only three kinds of issues: hearing differences, missing interactional detail, and unclear coding. This focus keeps feedback manageable.
Revise the transcript and calculate a simple agreement summary for a subset of codes, but interpret it cautiously. A disagreement may reveal an unclear definition rather than poor work. Add a final memo explaining which categories were revised and which ambiguities remain. The complete record should allow another analyst to understand not only the final coding but also how the evidence and decision rules developed.

7. Turning coded data into an argument
After coding, resist the temptation to report frequencies without context. In a short transcript, stating that there are five questions and three repairs says little unless the distribution supports an interpretive claim. Ask what the coded pattern reveals about the activity, roles, or source of difficulty.
Build the analysis around two or three claims. One claim might explain how a participant controls the transaction through question–answer sequences. Another might show how repeated other-initiated repair reveals insufficient reference. For each claim, present a short transcript excerpt, explain the code, and connect the feature to the interactional outcome.
Use deviant cases productively. If most questions receive immediate answers but one receives silence and reformulation, that exception may reveal a sensitive topic or unclear wording. A small qualitative analysis becomes stronger when it explains both the recurrent pattern and the moment that does not fit. End by distinguishing what the transcript demonstrates from what it merely suggests about wider communication.
