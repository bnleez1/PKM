# DA - Module 2: Speaking, Writing, and Communicative Purpose


⚡️ Comparing modes of discourse and translating foundational concepts into ELT practice

> Spoken and written discourse are not simply the same language delivered through different channels. Each mode creates distinct possibilities and constraints. Conversation normally unfolds in real time, allows immediate feedback, and depends heavily on shared context. Writing is usually more permanent, spatially organized, and available for revision, but writers must often anticipate readers who are absent. Digital communication complicates the contrast: a text message is written yet conversational, while a scripted lecture is spoken yet carefully planned. This week develops a flexible framework for comparing discourse by mode, purpose, audience, planning, interaction, and context. You will apply the framework to authentic samples, connect foundational theories to ELT methodology, and complete the first unit’s knowledge graph, AI planning appendix, assignment, and quiz.


## Essential understanding(s)


How do mode, purpose, audience, and context shape the organization of spoken and written discourse?

How can teachers use comparisons of speech and writing without turning tendencies into rigid rules?

## Key concepts and vocabulary

| Feature                     | Definition                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| :-------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Mode**                    | The form or channel through which communication is produced and received, such as spoken language, written language, video, or digital messaging.                                                                                              |
| **Spontaneity**             | The degree to which language is produced immediately, with little or no advance preparation. Spontaneous discourse often includes pauses, fillers, repetition, and self-correction.                                                            |
| **Planning**                | The degree to which a speaker or writer prepares, organizes, rehearses, edits, or revises a message before producing it.                                                                                                                       |
| **Permanence**              | The extent to which a discourse remains available after it is produced and can be reviewed, reread, replayed, or referred to later.                                                                                                            |
| **Interactivity**           | The degree to which participants can respond to one another and jointly influence how the discourse develops.                                                                                                                                  |
| **Lexical density**         | The amount of information carried by content words such as nouns, main verbs, adjectives, and adverbs in relation to the total amount of language used.                                                                                        |
| **Deixis**                  | The use of words whose meaning depends on the situation or context, especially expressions referring to people, place, and time, such as *I, you, here, there, now,* and *tomorrow*.                                                           |
| **Paralinguistic features** | Vocal and nonverbal features that contribute meaning in addition to the words themselves, such as intonation, stress, volume, pauses, facial expressions, gestures, gaze, and body movement.                                                   |
| **Genre**                   | A recognizable type of communication that has a typical purpose, audience, structure, style, and set of conventions, such as a job application, news report, lecture, or text message.                                                         |
| **Communicative purpose**   | What a speaker or writer is trying to accomplish through a text, such as informing, persuading, requesting, explaining, entertaining, warning, or maintaining a social relationship.                                                           |
| **Audience**                | The person or group for whom a message is intended. The audience can influence vocabulary, formality, explanation, tone, and other language choices.                                                                                           |
| **Hybrid discourse**        | Communication that combines characteristics of more than one mode, medium, or traditional discourse type, such as a written group chat that behaves like conversation or a scripted video that combines written planning with spoken delivery. |

## Main chapter content

1. **Moving beyond a strict binary**
Introductory comparisons often describe speech as informal, temporary, interactive, and context-dependent, while writing is described as formal, permanent, monologic, and explicit. These tendencies can be useful, but they are not definitions. A legal judgment may be read aloud yet retain the density and planning of writing. A group chat is written but contains rapid turns, ellipsis, emojis, repair, and immediate feedback. A podcast may be spontaneous, scripted, edited, or all three. The analyst should therefore compare dimensions rather than assign absolute properties.
Mode concerns how discourse is materially produced and received. Planning concerns how much time and revision are available. Interaction concerns whether participants can respond and shape the unfolding discourse. Permanence concerns whether the language can be revisited. Context dependence concerns how much interpretation relies on the immediate situation. Lexical and grammatical patterning concerns density, clause structure, repetition, reference, and explicitness. Purpose and genre concern the social activity being performed.
A strong comparison identifies both typical patterns and local exceptions. For example, spontaneous conversation often contains false starts, fillers, vague reference, and repetition. These are not signs of deficient language; they help speakers hold the floor, plan online, monitor understanding, and maintain interpersonal connection. Academic writing often packages information into dense noun phrases and uses explicit connectors because the reader cannot interrupt to request clarification. The difference is functional: each mode distributes the work of meaning-making differently.

2. **Features of spoken discourse**
Spoken interaction is temporally organized. Participants produce and interpret language moment by moment. Meaning is supported by intonation, rhythm, stress, volume, gesture, gaze, and the visible environment. Speakers can check understanding, rephrase, repair trouble, and adapt to the listener’s reactions. Turn-taking allows discourse to be collaboratively built rather than fully planned by one producer.
Common features include contractions, pronouns, deictic expressions, discourse markers, ellipsis, coordination, repetition, and relatively short information units. Expressions such as “well,” “so,” “you know,” and “I mean” can signal topic movement, hesitation, reformulation, or interpersonal alignment. Listeners produce backchannels such as “mm-hm,” “right,” and “okay,” which demonstrate attention without necessarily claiming the floor. Pauses and overlaps also carry interactional meaning.
These features must be interpreted in context. Repetition may emphasize a point, buy planning time, create cohesion, or establish shared wording. An incomplete clause may be entirely interpretable because the participants share the referent. A direct imperative may be appropriate in an emergency but face-threatening in a routine request. When analyzing a clip, record what participants are doing together, not merely which sentence types occur.

3. **Features of written discourse**
Writing is spatially organized and typically separated from the immediate production context. Readers can move backward and forward, while writers can draft, revise, and arrange information visually through paragraphs, headings, lists, punctuation, and typography. Because immediate feedback is often unavailable, writers may need to make relationships explicit through cohesive devices, metadiscourse, definitions, examples, and carefully managed reference.
Written genres vary dramatically. A research article, personal note, online comment, instructional handout, and novel differ in purpose, audience, stance, and degree of planning. Written discourse may display greater lexical density and syntactic embedding, but this is especially true of formal expository genres, not all writing. Writers also create interaction through questions, directives, evaluation, hedging, and references to the reader. “Monologic” does not mean socially isolated.
The visual organization of writing contributes to meaning. Paragraph boundaries propose units of thought. Headings forecast macrostructure. Citation practices position the writer in relation to a disciplinary community. Punctuation and formatting can signal emphasis or hierarchy. An analysis of written discourse should therefore consider the whole communicative design, including layout and multimodal elements when relevant.

4. **Purpose, audience, and genre**
The same topic can be realized through multiple genres. A teacher may explain a grammar point in a lecture, a textbook page, a feedback comment, a video caption, or a private message. The language changes because the purpose, audience relationship, time constraints, and expectations change. Genre provides a useful bridge between textual form and social context. Members of a community recognize recurring communicative situations and develop conventional ways of responding to them.
Classification should be evidence-based. A text that contains information is not automatically “informative”; an advertisement provides information while primarily attempting to persuade. A conversation may shift from interactional small talk to transactional problem-solving. A classroom explanation may simultaneously transmit content, establish teacher authority, encourage participation, and manage time. Multiple purposes can coexist, although one may be dominant at a particular moment.
Audience design is equally important. Producers make assumptions about what recipients know, value, and need. Pronoun choice, explanation, terminology, politeness, and explicitness reflect those assumptions. When assumptions are inaccurate, communication may fail. ELT learners benefit from comparing how similar content is adapted for different audiences rather than memorizing decontextualized “formal” and “informal” phrases.

5. **From analysis to teaching practice**
A discourse-informed teacher can use authentic samples to help learners notice patterns while avoiding simplistic prescriptions. A listening task can ask students to identify how speakers signal topic changes or repair misunderstandings. A writing task can compare how two genres introduce the same topic. A speaking lesson can practice ways to enter a conversation, clarify meaning, and close an exchange. Assessment criteria can include coherence, audience awareness, pragmatic appropriateness, and genre fulfillment alongside grammatical accuracy.
Materials should also be evaluated critically. Textbook dialogues are often unnaturally complete: each turn is grammatically polished, no one overlaps, and every response directly answers the preceding question. Such dialogues may support controlled practice but should not be presented as neutral representations of natural conversation. Likewise, model essays may display one culturally and institutionally valued organization without acknowledging alternatives.
For the Unit I assignment, use the shared analytic framework to compare samples and connect findings to ELT. Describe observable features before interpreting them. Support claims with specific evidence. State limitations: a single sample cannot prove how all speech or writing works. Your AI appendix should document planning support, but your classification, explanation, and pedagogical implications must be independently produced.
