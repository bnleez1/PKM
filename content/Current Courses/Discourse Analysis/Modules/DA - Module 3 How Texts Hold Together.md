# DA - Module 3: How Texts Hold Together

⚡️ Grammatical cohesion, lexical chains, and the construction of continuity

> Readers and listeners usually experience a text as connected rather than as a random series of sentences. That connectedness is partly created through visible linguistic ties: pronouns refer back to participants, conjunctions signal relations, repeated or related words sustain a topic, and omitted material can be recovered from the surrounding discourse. Yet formal links do not guarantee that a text makes sense. Cohesion concerns the linguistic resources that connect parts of a text; coherence concerns the meaningful whole constructed by producers and interpreters. This week introduces major grammatical and lexical cohesive resources and asks how they function across genres. You will annotate authentic texts, build lexical-chain maps, and evaluate when cohesion supports clarity, when it creates ambiguity, and when a coherent interpretation depends more on shared knowledge than on explicit wording.

## Essential understanding(s)

How do grammatical and lexical choices create continuity across extended discourse?

Why can a text be cohesive without being coherent—or coherent with relatively few explicit ties?

## Guiding Questions


How do reference, substitution, ellipsis, and conjunction link clauses and sentences?

How do repetition, synonymy, superordination, and collocation form lexical chains?

When does a cohesive device clarify relationships, and when does it create ambiguity or unnecessary repetition?

How can teachers help learners use cohesion as a meaning-making resource rather than a connector checklist?
## Learning outcomes

Identify and classify grammatical cohesive ties in authentic written and spoken texts.

Construct and interpret lexical-chain maps across paragraphs or turns.

Distinguish cohesion from coherence and explain how contextual knowledge contributes to interpretation.

Evaluate the effectiveness and appropriateness of cohesive choices across genres.

Design a brief noticing or revision activity for ELT learners focused on connected discourse.
## Key concepts and vocabulary

| **Term**             | **Working definition**                                                                         |
| :------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Cohesion**         | Observable linguistic ties that connect elements within a text.                                |
| **Coherence**        | The meaningful unity interpreters construct from textual organization, context, and knowledge. |
| **Reference**        | The use of an expression whose interpretation depends on another element or the context.       |
| **Anaphora**         | Reference backward to a previously mentioned element.                                          |
| **Cataphora**        | Reference forward to an element introduced later.                                              |
| **Substitution**     | Replacement of a linguistic item with a form such as one, do, or so.                           |
| **Ellipsis**         | Omission of recoverable material.                                                              |
| **Conjunction**      | A resource for signaling additive, adversative, causal, temporal, or other logical relations.  |
| **Lexical cohesion** | Continuity created through semantic relationships among lexical items.                         |
| **Lexical chain**    | A sequence of semantically related words that develops or sustains a discourse topic.          |

## Main chapter content

1. Cohesion is a resource, not a guarantee
Cohesion refers to explicit or recoverable relationships among elements of a text. Pronouns, repeated words, conjunctions, and omitted material can signal that a clause should be interpreted in relation to another clause. These resources reduce unnecessary repetition and help participants track people, objects, events, and logical relations. They are part of the linguistic evidence an analyst can identify directly.
Coherence is broader. It concerns whether a discourse is interpretable as a meaningful whole. A highly cohesive text can still be incoherent if its claims contradict one another or its topic shifts without a plausible purpose. Consider: “The seminar begins at nine. It has four wheels. Therefore, the reading was difficult.” Pronouns and a connector appear, but the relationships do not support a coherent interpretation. Conversely, “Doorbell. Dog barking. I’ll get it” contains few formal ties, yet a familiar situation allows the listener to infer a coherent sequence.
This distinction prevents a common teaching error: requiring students to insert more connectors whenever a text feels unclear. Coherence may depend on topic development, genre organization, shared knowledge, reference clarity, and logical consistency. Cohesive devices should express relationships that already make sense, not conceal a weak relationship beneath “however” or “therefore.”

2. Reference and participant tracking
Reference directs the interpreter to identify a person, object, event, location, or proposition. Personal pronouns, demonstratives, comparative forms, and definite noun phrases often perform this work. Anaphoric reference points backward: “María submitted the transcript. She also attached the coding key.” Cataphoric reference points forward: “Although he was nervous, Daniel began the presentation clearly.” Exophoric reference depends on the situation: “Put it over there.”
Reference chains allow texts to maintain continuity while varying expression. A participant may be introduced with a full noun phrase, maintained with a pronoun, and later reidentified when ambiguity arises. Effective reference balances economy and clarity. Too much repetition can sound mechanical; too many pronouns can overload the reader’s memory or create competing antecedents.
Analysts should trace each referring expression and ask whether its target is recoverable. Ambiguity can be strategically useful in literature or humor, but in instructions and academic writing it often causes difficulty. In spoken interaction, gesture, gaze, and shared visual context may resolve reference that appears vague in a transcript. This is one reason transcripts must include relevant contextual notes rather than treating words as the complete event.

3. Substitution, ellipsis, and conjunction
Substitution replaces an item with another linguistic form. In “I need a marker. Do you have one?” one substitutes for marker. In “She said the meeting was canceled, and I think so too,” so substitutes for a proposition. Ellipsis omits recoverable material: “Who finished the coding?” “Ana did.” The omitted phrase “finished the coding” is understood from the preceding question. These resources are common in interaction because participants share an immediately available context.
Conjunction signals how propositions are related. Additive relations extend information; adversative relations contrast expectations; causal relations connect reasons, results, or purposes; temporal relations order events. The same relation can be expressed through conjunctions, adverbials, prepositional phrases, verbs, or simply sequence. “Because the clip was noisy, we replayed it” explicitly marks cause. “The clip was noisy. We replayed it” invites a causal inference without an overt marker.
Connector analysis should therefore move beyond counting. Ask what relation the producer appears to signal, whether that relation is justified, and how the choice fits the genre. Spoken discourse may use and, so, but, then, and anyway flexibly to manage both logic and interaction. Academic writing may favor more differentiated signals, but excessive variety can obscure rather than improve meaning.

4. Lexical cohesion and topic development
Lexical cohesion arises through relationships among content words. Repetition keeps a key term visible. Synonymy and near-synonymy provide variation while maintaining reference. Superordination connects a general category with its members, such as assessment, quiz, project, and portfolio. Collocation links words that commonly occur in the same field or situation, such as classroom, teacher, learner, task, feedback, and rubric.
A lexical-chain map traces these relationships across a text. Begin by identifying recurring topic words, then group related expressions and note where chains begin, continue, intersect, or disappear. Strong chains can reveal topic continuity and paragraph focus. A sudden break may signal a purposeful shift, an undeveloped idea, or an incoherent digression. Competing chains may show how a text compares two concepts or moves between theory and application.
Lexical choices also frame a topic. Referring to an event as a protest, disturbance, demonstration, or riot activates different evaluations. In educational discourse, calling AI a tool, collaborator, assistant, threat, or shortcut similarly shapes interpretation. Lexical cohesion is therefore not merely repetition management; it contributes to stance, categorization, and the construction of social reality.

5. Teaching and revising cohesion
Learners often receive lists of connectors detached from actual discourse. A more effective approach begins with relationships. Students can sort sentences into a logical sequence, explain the relation between each pair, and then compare possible linguistic signals. They can trace reference chains in a model text, identify where a reader might lose the referent, and revise selectively. They can build lexical chains before drafting to plan topic continuity without repeating the same word mechanically.
Revision should proceed from meaning to form. First ask whether the text has a recognizable purpose and macrostructure. Next examine paragraph focus and progression. Then inspect local cohesion: reference, lexical continuity, and logical relations. This order prevents cosmetic editing from replacing conceptual revision.
In your weekly analysis, annotate an authentic sample using a consistent coding key. Describe the device, quote or identify the relevant expression, state what it links, and explain its effect. Avoid claiming that every repeated word is poor style or that every connector improves coherence. The analytical goal is to show how specific choices support—or fail to support—the text’s communicative work.

6. A worked micro-analysis
Consider a short paragraph about peer feedback. Begin by circling all expressions that refer to participants, drafts, and comments. Draw arrows from pronouns and definite noun phrases to their likely referents. If an arrow could point to more than one candidate, mark the ambiguity. Next, underline conjunctions and write the relation each one signals. Ask whether the relation is also supported by the propositions themselves.
Now build lexical chains. One chain might include feedback, comments, suggestions, revision, and response. Another might include students, peers, writers, and partners. Identify where the chains overlap and where one disappears. The pattern may reveal a shift from describing the feedback process to evaluating student participation. If the paragraph feels disconnected, decide whether the problem is a missing link, an unclear referent, an undeveloped topic, or a logical gap.
Finally, write a three-part analytical statement: name the cohesive pattern, cite the textual evidence, and explain its effect on coherence and audience processing. This structure prevents annotation from becoming an end in itself. The goal is to show how visible ties contribute to the larger communicative purpose and where they do not.

            *Typical purpose:* to explain or teach academic content.

- Cohesion

    Here are clear examples of the four main types of **grammatical cohesion**. Your Chapter 3 materials define these as reference, substitution, ellipsis, and conjunction. Celce-Murcia and Olshtain likewise identify these four as major grammatical cohesive ties.
	
    | Type             | Example                                                                            | How cohesion works                                                                                                                            |
    | :--------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
    | **Reference**    | *Maria submitted the assignment.* ***She*** *uploaded it this morning.*            | **She** refers back to **Maria**. The reader connects the two sentences through the pronoun.                                                  |
    | **Ellipsis**     | A: *Who finished the assignment?* B: *Carlos* ***did****.*                         | The words *finish the assignment* are omitted because they can be recovered from the previous question: *Carlos did [finish the assignment].* |
    | **Substitution** | *I need a blue marker. Do you have* ***one****?*                                   | **One** substitutes for the noun phrase **a blue marker**, avoiding unnecessary repetition.                                                   |
    | **Conjunction**  | *The students understood the theory,* ***but*** *they had difficulty applying it.* | **But** connects the propositions by signaling an **adversative/contrastive relationship**.                                                   |

    - Sample text (Grammatical Cohesion)

        On Tuesday afternoon, **Laura** met her classmates in the library to prepare their group presentation. **She** had already completed the introduction, but the other students still needed to finish their sections. Laura showed the group her notes and asked everyone to check **them** before they continued.

        “Who finished the slides for the results section?” Laura asked.

        “**David did**,” Ana replied.

        David opened his laptop and showed the group the slides. Ana liked the design, but she thought one of the graphs was difficult to understand. “Maybe we should replace it with a simpler **one**,” she suggested. David agreed and searched for another graph.

        The group also needed a charger for the classroom projector. Laura had forgotten hers, so she asked, “Does anyone have a charger?”

        “I have **one**,” Ana said.

        The students worked for another hour. **However**, they still needed to practice the presentation. They decided to meet again the next morning **because** everyone had an early class nearby. Before leaving, Laura uploaded the final slides to Teams, **and** she sent the group a message with the link.

        ### Cohesion to Notice

        | Type             | Example from the text                             | How cohesion works                                                                                                          |
        | :--------------- | :------------------------------------------------ | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
        | **Reference**    | **Laura → She**                                   | **She** refers back to **Laura**, allowing the writer to continue talking about the same person without repeating her name. |
        | **Reference**    | **notes → them**                                  | **Them** refers back to **notes**.                                                                                          |
        | **Ellipsis**     | “Who finished the slides?” — “**David did**.”     | The words *finish the slides* are recoverable from the question: *David did [finish the slides].*                           |
        | **Substitution** | “replace it with a simpler **one**”               | **One** substitutes for **graph**.                                                                                          |
        | **Substitution** | “Does anyone have a charger?” — “I have **one**.” | **One** substitutes for **a charger**.                                                                                      |
        | **Conjunction**  | “**but** the other students…”                     | **But** signals contrast.                                                                                                   |
        | **Conjunction**  | “**However**, they still needed…”                 | **However** signals an adversative or contrastive relationship.                                                             |
        | **Conjunction**  | “**because** everyone had…”                       | **Because** signals a causal relationship.                                                                                  |
        | **Conjunction**  | “…Teams, **and** she sent…”                       | **And** adds another related action.                                                                                        |

        Here are clear examples of the three types:

        | Type                     | Example                                                      | Explanation                                                                                    |
        | :----------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
        | **Anaphoric reference**  | Maria bought a new laptop. **She** uses **it** for class.    | **She** refers back to *Maria*, and **it** refers back to *a new laptop*.                      |

-  [Reference vs. Substitution: Two Grammatical Cohesion Resources](../Pages/Reference%20vs%20Substitution%20Two%20Grammatical%20Cohesion%20Resources.md)

    1. "The relationship [between reference and substitution] is... one of shared classification rather than shared identity**" (**Celce-Murcia and Olshtain, 2000, pp. 53-54).

    2. Reference normally involves **co-reference**, while substitution and ellipsis generally involve some difference between the first item and the second. Also, reference can reach much farther back in a text, while substitution usually depends on material in the immediately preceding discourse (Paltridge, 2022, pp. 118-129).

    - Examples of reference and substitution

        - Nominal substitution

            **Nominal substitution — one/ones**

            > *I need a blue marker. Do you have* ***one****?*

            Here, **one** means *a marker*, but not the same marker already mentioned. Compare:

            > *I bought a blue marker. I put* ***it*** *on my desk.*

            In the first pair, **one** is substitution: another member of the category *marker*. In the second pair, **it** is reference: the same marker.

        - Verbal substitution

            > A: *Did Elena finish the report?* B: *Yes, she* ***did****.*

            Here, **did** substitutes for *finished the report*. The substitute carries the grammatical structure without repeating the full verb phrase.

            > A: *Has he had dinner?* B: *He must have* ***done****.*

            Paltridge uses this type of example to show how a form of *do* can substitute for an earlier verb group (2022, p. 127).

        - Clausal substitution

            > A: *Do you think the class will be canceled?* B: *I think* ***so****.*

            **So** substitutes for the proposition *the class will be canceled*.

            > A: *Will the exam be postponed?* B: *Probably* ***not****.*

            **Not** stands for the negative version of the proposition rather than referring to an object.
