# Discourse Analysis Module 3: Cohesion and Coherence — How Texts Hold Together

> [!abstract]- ⚡ Module Overview
> **Grammatical cohesion, lexical chains, and the construction of continuity**
>
> Readers and listeners usually experience a text as connected rather than as a random series of sentences.
>
> That connectedness is partly created through visible linguistic ties:
>
> - pronouns refer to participants;
> - conjunctions signal relationships;
> - repeated or related words sustain topics;
> - omitted material can be recovered from surrounding discourse.
>
> However, formal connections do not automatically make a text meaningful.
>
> **Cohesion** concerns the linguistic resources that connect parts of a text.
>
> **Coherence** concerns the meaningful whole constructed by producers and interpreters.
>
> In this module, you will examine:
>
> - grammatical cohesion;
> - reference and participant tracking;
> - substitution;
> - ellipsis;
> - conjunction;
> - lexical cohesion;
> - lexical chains;
> - the relationship between cohesion and coherence.
>
> The goal is not simply to identify cohesive devices. The goal is to explain **how particular linguistic choices support—or fail to support—the communicative work of a text**.

---

> [!question]- 🎯 Essential Understandings
> - How do grammatical and lexical choices create continuity across extended discourse?
> - Why can a text be cohesive without being coherent—or coherent with relatively few explicit ties?

> [!question]- ❓ Guiding Questions
> - How do reference, substitution, ellipsis, and conjunction link clauses and sentences?
> - How do repetition, synonymy, superordination, and collocation form lexical chains?
> - When does a cohesive device clarify relationships?
> - When can it create ambiguity or unnecessary repetition?
> - How can teachers help learners use cohesion as a **meaning-making resource** rather than as a connector checklist?

> [!success]- ✅ Learning Outcomes
> By the end of this module, you should be able to:
>
> 1. Identify and classify grammatical cohesive ties in authentic written and spoken texts.
> 2. Construct and interpret lexical-chain maps across paragraphs or turns.
> 3. Distinguish cohesion from coherence.
> 4. Explain how contextual knowledge contributes to interpretation.
> 5. Evaluate the effectiveness and appropriateness of cohesive choices across genres.
> 6. Design a brief noticing or revision activity for ELT learners focused on connected discourse.

> [!note]- 🧠 Key Concepts and Vocabulary
>
> > [!note]- Cohesion
> > Observable linguistic ties that connect elements within a text.
>
> > [!note]- Coherence
> > The meaningful unity interpreters construct from:
> >
> > - textual organization;
> > - context;
> > - knowledge.
>
> > [!note]- Reference
> > The use of an expression whose interpretation depends on another element or on the context.
>
> > [!note]- Anaphoric Reference
> > Reference **backward** to a previously mentioned element.
> >
> > **Remember:** anaphoric = look back.
>
> > [!note]- Cataphoric Reference
> > Reference **forward** to an element introduced later.
> >
> > **Remember:** cataphoric = look forward.
>
> > [!note]- Exophoric Reference
> > Reference to something in the **situation outside the text**.
> >
> > **Remember:** exophoric = look outside the text.
>
> > [!note]- Substitution
> > Replacement of a linguistic item with another form such as:
> >
> > - *one*
> > - *do*
> > - *so*
>
> > [!note]- Ellipsis
> > The omission of material that can be recovered from the surrounding discourse.
>
> > [!note]- Conjunction
> > A resource for signaling relationships such as:
> >
> > - additive;
> > - adversative;
> > - causal;
> > - temporal.
>
> > [!note]- Lexical Cohesion
> > Continuity created through semantic relationships among lexical items.
>
> > [!note]- Lexical Chain
> > A sequence of semantically related words that develops or sustains a discourse topic.

> [!example]- 1️⃣ Cohesion Is a Resource, Not a Guarantee
> Cohesion refers to explicit or recoverable relationships among elements of a text.
>
> Resources such as:
>
> - pronouns;
> - repeated words;
> - conjunctions;
> - omitted material
>
> signal that one part of a text should be interpreted in relation to another.
>
> These resources can:
>
> - reduce unnecessary repetition;
> - help readers track participants;
> - maintain objects and events;
> - signal logical relationships.
>
> **Coherence is broader.**
>
> It concerns whether a discourse can be interpreted as a meaningful whole.
>
> > [!example]- Cohesive but Not Coherent
> > **The seminar begins at nine. It has four wheels. Therefore, the reading was difficult.**
> >
> > This example contains:
> >
> > - a pronoun;
> > - a connector.
> >
> > Yet the propositions do not form a meaningful relationship.
>
> > [!example]- Coherent with Few Explicit Cohesive Ties
> > **Doorbell. Dog barking. I'll get it.**
> >
> > There are few formal linguistic connections.
> >
> > Nevertheless, shared situational knowledge allows us to infer a coherent sequence.
>
> > [!important]- Key Principle
> > Adding more connectors does **not necessarily improve coherence**.
> >
> > Coherence may depend on:
> >
> > - topic development;
> > - genre organization;
> > - shared knowledge;
> > - reference clarity;
> > - logical consistency.
> >
> > Cohesive devices should express relationships that already make sense rather than hide a weak relationship beneath *however* or *therefore*.

> [!example]- 2️⃣ Reference and Participant Tracking
> Reference directs the interpreter toward:
>
> - a person;
> - an object;
> - an event;
> - a location;
> - a proposition.
>
> Reference can be realized through:
>
> - personal pronouns;
> - demonstratives;
> - comparative forms;
> - definite noun phrases.
>
> > [!example]- Anaphoric Reference
> > **María submitted the transcript. She also attached the coding key.**
> >
> > *She* points backward to **María**.
>
> > [!example]- Cataphoric Reference
> > **Although he was nervous, Daniel began the presentation clearly.**
> >
> > *he* points forward to **Daniel**.
>
> > [!example]- Exophoric Reference
> > **Put it over there.**
> >
> > The meaning of *it* and *there* depends on the physical or situational context.
>
> > [!important]- Reference Chains
> > A participant may be:
> >
> > 1. introduced with a full noun phrase;
> > 2. maintained through a pronoun;
> > 3. reidentified with a noun phrase when ambiguity becomes possible.
> >
> > Effective reference balances **economy and clarity**.
>
> > [!warning]- Possible Problems
> > Too much repetition may sound mechanical.
> >
> > Too many pronouns may:
> >
> > - overload the reader's memory;
> > - create competing antecedents;
> > - make participant tracking difficult.
>
> > [!tip]- Analytical Question
> > Trace each referring expression and ask:
> >
> > **Can the reader confidently recover its referent?**

---

> [!example]- 3️⃣ Reference in Spoken Discourse
> Reference that appears vague in a transcript may be perfectly understandable in the original interaction.
>
> Spoken interaction may rely on:
>
> - gesture;
> - gaze;
> - physical surroundings;
> - shared visual context.
>
> For this reason, transcripts sometimes need contextual notes.
>
> The words alone do not always represent the complete communicative event.

---

> [!example]- 4️⃣ Substitution
> Substitution replaces an item with another linguistic form rather than simply referring to the same item.
>
> > [!note]- Nominal Substitution
> > **I need a blue marker. Do you have one?**
> >
> > *one* substitutes for **a marker**.
> >
> > Importantly, it does not mean the exact marker previously mentioned.
>
> > [!example]- Compare Reference and Substitution
> > **Substitution**
> >
> > I need a blue marker. Do you have **one**?
> >
> > → another member of the category *marker*
> >
> > **Reference**
> >
> > I bought a blue marker. I put **it** on my desk.
> >
> > → the same marker
>
> > [!note]- Verbal Substitution
> > **A:** Did Elena finish the report?  
> > **B:** Yes, she **did**.
> >
> > *did* substitutes for **finished the report**.
>
> > [!note]- Clausal Substitution
> > **A:** Do you think the class will be canceled?  
> > **B:** I think **so**.
> >
> > *so* substitutes for the proposition:
> >
> > **the class will be canceled**
>
> > [!example]- Negative Clausal Substitution
> > **A:** Will the exam be postponed?  
> > **B:** Probably **not**.
> >
> > *not* represents the negative version of the proposition.

---

> [!example]- 5️⃣ Ellipsis
> Ellipsis occurs when recoverable material is omitted.
>
> **A:** Who finished the coding?  
> **B:** Ana did.
>
> The complete meaning is:
>
> **Ana finished the coding.**
>
> However, *finished the coding* does not need to be repeated because the preceding question makes it recoverable.
>
> Ellipsis is particularly common in interaction because participants share an immediately available discourse context.

---

> [!example]- 6️⃣ Conjunction and Logical Relationships
> Conjunction signals relationships among propositions.
>
> Common relationships include:
>
> **Additive**
>
> → adds information
>
> **Adversative**
>
> → contrasts expectations or ideas
>
> **Causal**
>
> → expresses reason, result, or purpose
>
> **Temporal**
>
> → orders events
>
> > [!example]- Explicit Causal Relationship
> > **Because the clip was noisy, we replayed it.**
> >
> > The causal relationship is explicitly marked.
>
> > [!example]- Implicit Causal Relationship
> > **The clip was noisy. We replayed it.**
> >
> > The reader can infer causality even without an overt connector.
>
> > [!important]- Go Beyond Counting Connectors
> > Do not simply identify or count connectors.
> >
> > Ask:
> >
> > - What relationship does the producer appear to signal?
> > - Is that relationship actually justified?
> > - How does the linguistic choice fit the genre?
>
> > [!note]- Spoken and Written Discourse
> > Spoken discourse may use:
> >
> > - and
> > - so
> > - but
> > - then
> > - anyway
> >
> > quite flexibly for both logical and interactional purposes.
> >
> > Academic writing may use more differentiated signals.
> >
> > However, excessive connector variety can obscure rather than improve meaning.

---

> [!example]- 🔗 Conjunction Examples
> **Adversative**
>
> The students completed the survey; **however**, they forgot to submit their written reflections.
>
> **Temporal**
>
> We reviewed the vocabulary, **and then** we listened to the recording.
>
> **Causal**
>
> The professor explained the concept again **because** several students were confused.
>
> **Additive**
>
> The students read the article **and** discussed the main ideas in pairs.
>
> **Concessive**
>
> **Although** the assignment looked easy, it took several hours to complete.
>
> **Additive**
>
> The class practiced identifying references. **In addition**, they analyzed examples of substitution.
>
> **Result**
>
> The internet connection failed; **therefore**, the teacher changed the activity.
>
> **Temporal**
>
> **Before** the students began writing, they created an outline.
>
> **Contrast**
>
> The first group supported the proposal, **but** the second group disagreed.
>
> **Additive**
>
> Students can use the library databases. **Moreover**, they can ask the librarian for research assistance.
>
> **Result**
>
> The students did not understand the instructions, **so** the teacher demonstrated the activity.
>
> **Temporal**
>
> **After** the discussion ended, each student wrote a short reflection.
>
> **Concession**
>
> The results were promising; **nevertheless**, additional research was necessary.
>
> **Sequence**
>
> The class finished the first activity. **Next**, they compared their answers with a partner.

> [!example]- 7️⃣ Lexical Cohesion and Topic Development
> Lexical cohesion arises through relationships among content words.
>
> > [!note]- Repetition
> > Repeating an important term keeps a concept visible throughout a text.
>
> > [!note]- Synonymy and Near-Synonymy
> > Related words provide variation while helping maintain topic continuity.
>
> > [!note]- Superordination
> > A general category can connect with its members.
> >
> > Example:
> >
> > **assessment**
> >
> > - quiz
> > - project
> > - portfolio
>
> > [!note]- Collocation
> > Words may belong to the same field or situation.
> >
> > Example:
> >
> > **classroom**
> >
> > - teacher
> > - learner
> > - task
> > - feedback
> > - rubric

---

> [!example]- 8️⃣ Building a Lexical-Chain Map
> A lexical-chain map traces semantic relationships across a text.
>
> ### Step 1
> Identify recurring topic words.
>
> ### Step 2
> Group semantically related expressions.
>
> ### Step 3
> Identify where chains:
>
> - begin;
> - continue;
> - intersect;
> - disappear.
>
> ### Step 4
> Interpret what the pattern reveals.
>
> A strong chain may indicate:
>
> - topic continuity;
> - paragraph focus.
>
> A sudden break may indicate:
>
> - a purposeful shift;
> - an undeveloped idea;
> - an incoherent digression.
>
> Competing chains may indicate:
>
> - comparison between concepts;
> - movement between theory and application.

---

> [!important]- 🧭 Lexical Choice Also Frames Reality
> Lexical cohesion is not simply a strategy for avoiding repetition.
>
> Compare describing an event as:
>
> - a **protest**;
> - a **disturbance**;
> - a **demonstration**;
> - a **riot**.
>
> Each expression activates a different evaluation.
>
> Likewise, AI might be described as:
>
> - a tool;
> - a collaborator;
> - an assistant;
> - a threat;
> - a shortcut.
>
> These lexical choices influence interpretation.
>
> Lexical cohesion therefore contributes to:
>
> - stance;
> - categorization;
> - the construction of social reality.

> [!example]- 9️⃣ Teaching and Revising Cohesion
> Learners often receive lists of connectors detached from actual discourse.
>
> A more useful approach begins with **relationships and meaning**.
>
> Students can:
>
> - sort sentences into a logical sequence;
> - explain relationships between sentences;
> - compare possible linguistic signals;
> - trace reference chains;
> - locate unclear referents;
> - revise selectively;
> - build lexical chains before drafting.
>
> > [!important]- Revision Should Move from Meaning to Form
> > Use this sequence:
> >
> > **1. Purpose and macrostructure**
> >
> > Does the text have a recognizable communicative purpose and organization?
> >
> > **2. Paragraph focus and progression**
> >
> > Does each part develop appropriately?
> >
> > **3. Local cohesion**
> >
> > Examine:
> >
> > - reference;
> > - lexical continuity;
> > - logical relations.
> >
> > This prevents cosmetic editing from replacing conceptual revision.

> [!exercise]- 🔎 A Worked Micro-Analysis
> Consider a short paragraph about peer feedback.
>
> ### Step 1: Trace Reference
> Circle expressions that refer to:
>
> - participants;
> - drafts;
> - comments.
>
> Draw arrows from:
>
> - pronouns;
> - definite noun phrases
>
> to their likely referents.
>
> If an expression could point to more than one referent, mark the ambiguity.
>
> ### Step 2: Analyze Conjunction
> Underline conjunctions.
>
> For each one, identify:
>
> - the relationship it signals;
> - whether that relationship is actually supported by the propositions.
>
> ### Step 3: Build Lexical Chains
> One possible chain:
>
> **feedback → comments → suggestions → revision → response**
>
> Another:
>
> **students → peers → writers → partners**
>
> Examine:
>
> - where the chains overlap;
> - where they continue;
> - where one disappears.
>
> ### Step 4: Diagnose Problems
> If the paragraph feels disconnected, determine whether the problem is:
>
> - a missing link;
> - an unclear referent;
> - an undeveloped topic;
> - a logical gap.
>
> ### Step 5: Write an Analytical Statement
> Use three parts:
>
> **1. Name the cohesive pattern.**
>
> **2. Cite the textual evidence.**
>
> **3. Explain its effect on coherence and audience processing.**

> [!example]- 📖 Sample Text: Grammatical Cohesion
> On Tuesday afternoon, Laura met her classmates in the library to prepare their group presentation. She had already completed the introduction, but the other students still needed to finish their sections. Laura showed the group her notes and asked everyone to check them before they continued.
>
> “Who finished the slides for the results section?” Laura asked.
>
> “David did,” Ana replied.
>
> David opened his laptop and showed the group the slides. Ana liked the design, but she thought one of the graphs was difficult to understand. “Maybe we should replace it with a simpler one,” she suggested. David agreed and searched for another graph.
>
> The group also needed a charger for the classroom projector. Laura had forgotten hers, so she asked, “Does anyone have a charger?”
>
> “I have one,” Ana said.
>
> The students worked for another hour. However, they still needed to practice the presentation. They decided to meet again the next morning because everyone had an early class nearby. Before leaving, Laura uploaded the final slides to Teams, and she sent the group a message with the link.
>
> > [!success]- Cohesion to Notice
> > **Reference**
> >
> > Laura → **She**
> >
> > notes → **them**
> >
> > **Ellipsis**
> >
> > “Who finished the slides?” → “David **did**.”
> >
> > **Substitution**
> >
> > “replace it with a simpler **one**”
> >
> > “Does anyone have a charger?” → “I have **one**.”
> >
> > **Conjunction**
> >
> > - *but the other students...*
> > - *However, they still needed...*
> > - *because everyone had...*
> > - *...Teams, and she sent...*

> Identify:

> - at least **two examples of reference**;
> - **one example of ellipsis**;
> - **two examples of substitution**;

>

>
> **1. Identify the expression.**
>
> **2. Explain what it refers to, replaces, omits, or connects.**
>
> **3. Explain how that relationship contributes to the cohesion of the text.**

> [!note]- 🔄 Review: 12 Features of Discourse
> The module also revisits the 12-feature framework from the previous module.
>
> These features should be treated as **dimensions rather than rigid categories**.
>
> > [!note]- 1. Mode
> > A student leaves a voice message instead of sending a written text.
>
> > [!note]- 2. Spontaneity
> > “Well, um, I'm not sure… maybe we could meet after class?”
>
> > [!note]- 3. Planning
> > “Today I will explain three causes of language variation: region, social identity, and context.”
>
> > [!note]- 4. Permanence
> > A university policy posted as a PDF can be saved and reread, unlike an unrecorded hallway conversation.
>
> > [!note]- 5. Interactivity
> > **A:** Did you understand the assignment?
> >
> > **B:** Not completely. What does the last question mean?
> >
> > **A:** I think it asks us to compare the two texts.
>
> > [!note]- 6. Lexical Density
> > “The implementation of multilingual educational policies requires institutional coordination and sustained professional development.”
>
> > [!note]- 7. Deixis
> > “I'll meet you here tomorrow after she finishes class.”
>
> > [!note]- 8. Paralinguistic Features
> > “That was a great presentation.”
> >
> > [smiles, nods, and speaks enthusiastically]
>
> > [!note]- 9. Genre
> > Examples include:
> >
> > - job application letter;
> > - news report;
> > - classroom lecture;
> > - recipe;
> > - group chat message.
>
> > [!note]- 10. Communicative Purpose
> > “Please upload your assignment by Friday at 8:00 p.m.”
> >
> > Purpose: instructing or reminding students to complete an action.
>
> > [!note]- 11. Audience
> > **To a friend:**  
> > “Hey, wanna meet later?”
> >
> > **To a department chair:**  
> > “Would you be available to meet this afternoon?”
>
> > [!note]- 12. Hybrid Discourse
> > “Hi everyone 👋 The room has changed to 204. See you there in ten minutes!”
> >
> > This is written discourse with conversational and immediate characteristics.
>
> > [!important]- Key Principle
> > These features operate on continua.
> >
> > Discourse may be:
> >
> > - spoken yet highly planned;
> > - written yet highly interactive;
> > - written yet spontaneous;
> > - hybrid across several dimensions.

> [!success]- 🧭 Final Takeaway
> **A connected text is more than a text with connectors.**
>
> Cohesion gives readers and listeners linguistic evidence for connecting:
>
> - participants;
> - ideas;
> - events;
> - propositions;
> - lexical topics.
>
> But coherence depends on more than visible ties.
>
> When analyzing discourse, ask:
>
> **What is connected?**
>
> **How is it connected?**
>
> **Can the reader recover the relationship?**
>
> **Does the relationship make sense?**
>
> **What does the cohesive choice contribute to the larger communicative purpose?**
>
> The analytical goal is not simply to find cohesive devices.
>
> It is to explain **how visible linguistic ties contribute to meaning—and where they do not.**
