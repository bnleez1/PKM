# Chapter 9: Designing the Literature Review


⚡️ Aligning the inquiry and organizing ten sources through a synthesis matrix and thematic outline

> A literature review is not a sequence of ten source summaries. It is an organized explanation of what a body of literature collectively shows, where it differs, and what remains unresolved. This week begins the 2,500-word Unit IV project by confirming alignment among the topic, problem, scope, and thesis or central claim. Students then code the ten sources from the annotated bibliography by theme, method, finding, and limitation. A synthesis matrix and thematic outline help move the organization away from authors and toward ideas. The result should be a plan for a review with a clear introduction, logically ordered body sections, and a conclusion that answers the purpose established at the beginning. Drafting milestones are also set so that the work remains manageable and human-authored throughout the final unit.


## Essential Questions

1. How does a literature review create an argument from relationships across sources?

2. What organizational plan will allow a 2,500-word review to remain focused and coherent?

## Guiding Questions

1. How are the topic, problem, scope, and central claim connected?

2. What information should be coded in a synthesis matrix?

3. How can sources be grouped by themes rather than discussed one at a time?

4. What word-count and drafting milestones make the project feasible?

## Learning outcomes

- Explain the purpose and major structure of a literature review.

- Confirm alignment among topic, problem, scope, and thesis or central claim.

- Code the Unit III sources by theme, method, finding, and limitation.

- Construct a synthesis matrix and thematic outline organized by ideas.

- Develop a realistic 2,500-word plan and drafting schedule.

## Key concepts and vocabulary

| **Term**                          | **Working definition**                                                                        |
| :-------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Literature review**             | A structured synthesis and critical interpretation of research relevant to a defined inquiry. |
| **Synthesis**                     | The integration of ideas from multiple sources to produce a new understanding or argument.    |
| **Central claim**                 | The main interpretive position that the literature review develops and supports.              |
| **Organizing principle**          | The logic used to arrange body sections, such as themes, debates, methods, or patterns.       |
| **Synthesis matrix**              | A table that compares sources across common analytical categories.                            |
| **Thematic outline**              | A plan that groups sources and evidence under idea-based sections.                            |
| **Scope**                         | The conceptual and contextual boundaries of the review.                                       |
| **Coding**                        | Assigning labels to source information so patterns can be compared.                           |
| **Source-by-source organization** | A structure that discusses one source at a time, often limiting synthesis.                    |
| **Drafting milestone**            | A scheduled, measurable stage in completing the manuscript.                                   |

## Main chapter content

## The literature review as an argument

A literature review answers a question about a body of knowledge. It may explain what is known about a problem, compare approaches, trace patterns, identify limitations, or establish a research gap. Although it represents sources fairly, it is not neutral in the sense of being unorganized. The writer selects an organizing principle, decides which relationships matter, and develops a central claim about the literature.

The central claim should not predict the student’s future research results. Instead, it should express an interpretation supported by the reviewed sources. For example, a review might argue that peer feedback supports revision most consistently when learners receive explicit criteria and structured opportunities to respond. Such a claim can guide thematic sections on feedback design, learner preparation, and revision behavior. The claim may be refined during drafting, but an initial version helps prevent the paper from becoming a collection of unrelated summaries.

## Rechecking alignment before drafting

The topic identifies the area of inquiry. The problem explains why the inquiry matters. The scope defines what literature will be included. The central claim provides the review’s main interpretation. These parts should share key concepts and boundaries. If the research proposal focused on undergraduate EFL writers in a local university context but the source collection concerns general digital learning, the scope may need correction before drafting continues.

Students can test alignment by writing one sentence for each element and placing the sentences side by side. Repeated key terms should be visible. Differences should be intentional and explainable. The title, research question, and planned headings should also be checked. Alignment does not require identical wording, but it requires a consistent conceptual center. Making changes now is more efficient than forcing unrelated sources into the review later.

## Building the synthesis matrix

The synthesis matrix places sources in rows and analytical categories in columns, or the reverse. Required categories from the weekly plan include theme, method, finding, and limitation. Additional columns may include context, participants, theoretical perspective, relevance, and useful quotation or page number. The matrix should contain concise notes rather than full annotations. Its value comes from comparison: the writer can scan a column to see how several sources address the same issue.

Coding requires interpretation. Two sources may use different terminology for related concepts, so the student must decide whether they belong under the same theme. A source may contribute to several sections, and that is acceptable. Color or tags can help mark agreement, contrast, methodological difference, or gap. The matrix should always remain traceable to the source, with page numbers or note locations recorded where needed.

## Moving from source order to thematic order

A source-by-source outline often produces paragraphs that begin with one author’s name and end before another source enters. This structure may be appropriate for a brief historical overview, but it usually limits synthesis. A thematic outline begins with ideas such as learner preparation, types of feedback, revision outcomes, contextual constraints, or methodological limitations. Several sources then contribute evidence to each section.

The order of themes should answer the review’s purpose. Sections may move from foundational concepts to applications, from common findings to disagreements, from causes to effects, or from broad patterns to local implications. Each heading should represent an idea, not a source title. Under each heading, the outline should identify the section claim, relevant sources, relationships among them, and the intended connection to the central thesis.

## Planning 2,500 words and drafting milestones

The syllabus specifies a 2,500-word literature review, with approximately 2,000 words devoted to the body. Word count should be treated as an allocation problem. The introduction and conclusion require enough space to orient and synthesize, but the body must carry the main analysis. Students can estimate the number of sections and paragraphs, then assign approximate ranges. A four-section body might include roughly 450 to 550 words per section, adjusted according to importance.

This week students analyze models, confirm focus and alignment, code the ten Unit III sources, and submit a synthesis matrix and thematic outline. They also establish drafting milestones for the introduction, thematic sections, conclusion, peer review, and final revision. The actual draft remains human-authored. AI may later provide permitted editorial feedback, but the matrix and outline should reflect the student’s reading and decisions. A successful plan should make the next week’s drafting concrete: the writer knows which section comes first, which sources will interact, and what claim the section will develop.

## From concepts to this week’s deliverable

The synthesis matrix is the bridge between the annotated bibliography and the thematic outline. Sources are placed in rows and possible themes, concepts, methods, or findings in columns. Students record brief notes and page numbers rather than full prose. Empty cells are informative: they may show that a source contributes to only one part of the review or that a proposed theme lacks adequate evidence. Repeated patterns across rows can support a section, while conflicting entries can become a comparative discussion. Because the matrix externalizes relationships, it helps the writer move away from one-source-at-a-time reporting and toward a structure based on the research problem.

The 2,500-word plan should distribute space according to rhetorical importance, not equally among sources. The syllabus expects roughly 2,000 words for the body, leaving the remaining space for an introduction and conclusion. Students can assign provisional word ranges to each thematic section and set drafting milestones for Weeks 10–14. The thematic outline should name the claim or function of each section, list the sources that will interact there, and indicate the relationship to the central question. Before drafting, the alignment check returns to the Unit I proposal: the research problem, purpose, and question may be refined, but they should not be silently replaced by a different project. A strong plan makes later writing flexible without making it directionless.

## Suggested weekly learning sequence

A productive learning sequence for this chapter begins with a diagnostic response to the essential question, “How does a literature review create an argument from relationships across sources?” Students should first record an individual answer and then compare it with examples, course criteria, and peer interpretations. A short concept-sorting activity using Literature review, Synthesis, Central claim, and Organizing principle can make important distinctions visible before students work on a full draft. The instructor can then model one decision aloud, showing not only the completed example but also the questions, alternatives, and evidence that led to it. Students apply the same process to a small portion of their own project and receive formative feedback before extending the work independently.

The second part of the week should move from practice to production. Students use the guiding questions—“How are the topic, problem, scope, and central claim connected?” and “What information should be coded in a synthesis matrix?”—as checkpoints while working toward the stated outcomes, especially the ability to explain the purpose and major structure of a literature review and to confirm alignment among topic, problem, scope, and thesis or central claim. A brief peer conference should focus on one high-impact feature rather than attempting to correct everything at once. Students then revise, annotate the evidence of their decision-making, and complete the week’s deliverable: Alignment check; 2,500-word plan; synthesis matrix; thematic outline; drafting milestones. The final five minutes of class can be used for an exit reflection identifying one decision the writer can now justify and one question that must be carried into the next chapter.
