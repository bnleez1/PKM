# AW-Module 4: Building Academic Flow


⚡️ Selecting vocabulary, reporting verbs, linking devices, and rhetorical patterns

> Academic writing becomes readable when vocabulary, sentence relationships, and organizational patterns work together. A writer may have accurate information but still lose the reader through repetitive wording, weak attribution, or a sequence of ideas that lacks a clear logic. This week focuses on the language resources that make an article review sound purposeful and connected: academic vocabulary, reporting verbs, standard phrases, linking devices, cause-and-effect organization, and comparison-and-contrast organization. Students will build a phrase bank, but the aim is not to produce formulaic writing. Useful phrases should support precise thinking rather than replace it. The article-review outline will organize both summary and critical evaluation, allowing students to see where sources are reported, where relationships are explained, and where the reviewer’s own judgment enters the discussion.


## Essential Questions

1. How do word choice and rhetorical organization help readers follow an academic argument?

2. How can writers use conventional academic language without losing precision or authentic voice?

## Guiding Questions

1. How do reporting verbs communicate a writer’s stance toward a source?

2. Which linking devices express addition, contrast, cause, result, and qualification?

3. When is cause-and-effect organization more useful than comparison and contrast?

4. How can a phrase bank support drafting without creating repetitive or formulaic prose?

## Learning outcomes

- Use academic vocabulary and standard phrases appropriately in an article review.

- Select reporting verbs that accurately represent source actions and claims.

- Use linking devices to make logical relationships explicit.

- Organize ideas through cause-and-effect and comparison-and-contrast patterns.

- Create a phrase bank and an article-review outline that integrate summary and evaluation.

## Key concepts and vocabulary

| :---------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Academic vocabulary** | Words and phrases frequently used to express concepts, relationships, and evaluation in scholarly contexts. |
| **Reporting verb**      | A verb used to introduce a source’s claim, method, finding, or position.                                    |
| **Attribution**         | Language that identifies whose idea or statement is being presented.                                        |

## Main chapter content

## Academic vocabulary should increase precision

Academic vocabulary is useful when it names relationships and concepts more accurately than everyday alternatives. Words such as indicates, limitation, implication, significant, contextual, and consistent can help writers express complex judgments. However, formality does not automatically improve a sentence. Replacing a clear word with a longer synonym may make the sentence less natural or change the meaning. The best vocabulary choice is the one that is accurate, understandable, and appropriate for the discipline.

Students can develop a phrase bank by rhetorical purpose rather than alphabetically. Categories might include introducing a source, stating a purpose, describing a method, reporting a finding, comparing studies, identifying a limitation, and explaining significance. Each phrase should be treated as a flexible frame. For example, “The study provides evidence that…” requires the writer to identify what evidence and how strong it is. A phrase bank supports drafting when it reduces unnecessary searching for language, but it becomes harmful when phrases are inserted without attention to meaning.

## Reporting verbs as tools of stance

Reporting verbs do more than avoid repetition of says. They classify what a source is doing and show how the reviewer positions the claim. Neutral verbs such as reports, describes, examines, and identifies are useful for accurate summary. Verbs such as argues, maintains, proposes, and contends present a position. Suggests, implies, and indicates often communicate caution. Demonstrates, establishes, and proves are much stronger and should be used only when the evidence justifies that degree of certainty.

The grammar surrounding the verb also matters. A reviewer may write “The authors argue that…” for a proposition, “The study examines…” for a topic, or “The researchers attribute the change to…” for a causal explanation. Students should check whether the subject is the authors, the article, the study, the findings, or the data. Each subject performs different actions. Data do not usually argue, and authors do not literally demonstrate a statistical relationship without the support of their methods and evidence. Precise attribution prevents sources and reviewers from being confused.

## Linking devices and logical relationships

Linking devices guide the reader by making relationships explicit. Addition markers such as furthermore and in addition extend an idea. Contrast markers such as however, in contrast, and whereas show difference. Cause-and-result markers such as because, therefore, consequently, and as a result connect conditions and outcomes. Qualification markers such as although, despite, and nevertheless show that a claim remains valid within a complication. Exemplification markers such as for example introduce specific support.

A transition cannot create logic that is not already present. Adding therefore between two unrelated sentences does not establish causation. Writers should first identify the relationship, then choose the device. Variety is useful, but forced variation can be distracting. Repeating however may be less problematic than using an inaccurate synonym. Cohesion also comes from repeated key terms, pronoun reference, parallel structure, and predictable topic development. Transitions are one part of flow, not a complete solution.

## Cause-and-effect and comparison-and-contrast organization

Cause-and-effect organization is useful when a review examines why a problem occurs, what factors contribute to it, or what consequences follow. Writers must be cautious because association does not automatically establish causation. An article may report that two variables occur together without proving that one causes the other. Language such as is associated with, may contribute to, or is linked to can represent the evidence more accurately than causes.

Comparison-and-contrast organization is central to article reviews because evaluation often depends on standards or alternatives. A reviewer may compare an article’s purpose with its method, its conclusions with its evidence, or its findings with another study. Point-by-point organization usually creates stronger analysis than discussing one source completely and then the other. In a point-by-point structure, each paragraph focuses on a criterion such as sample, method, argument, or relevance and considers both items in relation to that criterion.

## Outlining summary and evaluation

An article review needs a controlled balance between summary and critical response. The outline can begin with identification of the article and its purpose, followed by a concise account of the context, method, and main findings. The critical section should then evaluate specific dimensions such as clarity of argument, adequacy of evidence, methodological fit, relevance to English language teaching, limitations, and implications. Summary provides the object of evaluation; it should not occupy the entire paper.

This week’s work includes building a phrase bank, replacing informal or repetitive wording, practicing reporting verbs and linking devices, drafting source-summary and attribution sentences, analyzing rhetorical patterns in model reviews, and producing an outline for the assigned article or articles. Students should test the outline by asking whether each section has a clear function and whether the evaluation follows logically from the summary. A strong outline makes the later draft more coherent because it establishes relationships before sentences are polished.

## From concepts to this week’s deliverable

The academic phrase bank should be organized by rhetorical function rather than alphabetically. Useful categories include introducing a topic, reporting a source, indicating agreement, marking contrast, explaining cause or consequence, adding evidence, interpreting a pattern, and qualifying a claim. Within each category, students should record complete examples and note grammatical patterns. For instance, “X argues that…” requires a clause, while “According to X…” is followed by a noun phrase or clause without a reporting verb. Building the bank this way makes it a decision-making resource: the writer first identifies the relationship between ideas and then chooses language that expresses that relationship accurately.

The article-review outline should also make the relationship between summary and evaluation explicit. A reader needs enough summary to understand the article’s purpose, approach, and findings, but the review cannot remain a sequence of reported details. Each main point should prepare for an evaluative judgment about relevance, evidence, method, clarity, contribution, or limitation. Cause-and-effect and comparison-and-contrast structures can help organize those judgments, provided the writer does not overstate causal relationships or compare unlike features. Before drafting, students can annotate the outline with intended reporting verbs and linking devices. This small step reveals repetition, weak logic, and missing transitions before they become paragraph-level problems.

## Suggested weekly learning sequence

A productive learning sequence for this chapter begins with a diagnostic response to the essential question, “How do word choice and rhetorical organization help readers follow an academic argument?” Students should first record an individual answer and then compare it with examples, course criteria, and peer interpretations. A short concept-sorting activity using Academic vocabulary, Reporting verb, Attribution, and Linking device can make important distinctions visible before students work on a full draft. The instructor can then model one decision aloud, showing not only the completed example but also the questions, alternatives, and evidence that led to it. Students apply the same process to a small portion of their own project and receive formative feedback before extending the work independently.

The second part of the week should move from practice to production. Students use the guiding questions—“How do reporting verbs communicate a writer’s stance toward a source?” and “Which linking devices express addition, contrast, cause, result, and qualification?”—as checkpoints while working toward the stated outcomes, especially the ability to use academic vocabulary and standard phrases appropriately in an article review and to select reporting verbs that accurately represent source actions and claims. A brief peer conference should focus on one high-impact feature rather than attempting to correct everything at once. Students then revise, annotate the evidence of their decision-making, and complete the week’s deliverable: Academic phrase bank; reporting-verb and linking-device practice; source-summary and attribution sentences; article-review outline. The final five minutes of class can be used for an exit reflection identifying one decision the writer can now justify and one question that must be carried into the next chapter.

> ## 1. Topic: Feedback and Learning
> 
> ### Original direct quotation
> 
> > “Feedback is one of the most powerful influences on learning and achievement, but this impact can be either positive or negative” (Hattie & Timperley, 2007, p. 81). > ### A. Paraphrase: Vocabulary modified
> 
> Feedback is one of the **strongest factors affecting student learning and performance**, although its effects may be **beneficial or harmful** (Hattie & Timperley, 2007).
> 
> **Main change:**
> 
> - powerful influences → strongest factors
>     
> - learning and achievement → student learning and performance
>     
> - positive or negative → beneficial or harmful
>     
> 
> ### B. Paraphrase: Different emphasis
> 
> Although feedback can improve or harm student learning, its **considerable influence on academic achievement makes it particularly important** (Hattie & Timperley, 2007).
> 
> **Main change:** The original begins by emphasizing the **power of feedback**. The paraphrase foregrounds the **possible positive and negative consequences** before emphasizing its importance.
> 
> ### C. Paraphrase: Structure modified
> 
> Learning and achievement can be strongly influenced by feedback, and the effect of that feedback may be positive or negative (Hattie & Timperley, 2007).
> 
> **Main change:**  
> Original structure:
> 
> **Feedback → influences → learning**
> 
> New structure:
> 
> **Learning → is influenced by → feedback**
> 
> ### D. Paraphrase: Structure + emphasis + vocabulary modified
> 
> Because comments provided to learners can either support or interfere with their progress, the way feedback is used can substantially affect academic success (Hattie & Timperley, 2007).
> 
> **Changes:** The sentence has been reorganized, the vocabulary has been replaced, and the emphasis has shifted from the **power of feedback** to **how feedback is used**.

> ### A useful distinction for students
> 
> The examples illustrate that **good paraphrasing is more than replacing words with synonyms**. Students can think of three major tools:
> 
> **Vocabulary** → _Which words and expressions can I express differently?_  
> **Structure** → _How can I reorganize the grammatical relationships between ideas?_  
> **Emphasis** → _Which part of the author's idea will I foreground for my own discussion?_
> 
> The strongest paraphrase normally uses **all three simultaneously while preserving the author's original meaning and still citing the source**.
