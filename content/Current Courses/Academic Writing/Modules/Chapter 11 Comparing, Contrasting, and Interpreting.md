# Chapter 11: Comparing, Contrasting, and Interpreting


⚡️ Developing a second thematic section through comparative and critical synthesis

> Synthesis becomes visible when a writer explains relationships among sources. This week the literature review develops through a second thematic section organized around comparison and contrast. Students examine agreements, disagreements, methodological differences, and contextual factors across the Unit III sources. The purpose is not simply to state that studies are similar or different. The writer must explain the basis of the relationship and its significance for the central claim. Analytical sentences—the sentences that answer “so what?”—are therefore a major focus. A strong section will represent each source fairly, identify patterns without erasing differences, and show how research design or context may help explain conflicting findings. The week also includes a critical-synthesis revision assignment to ensure that evidence is consistently followed by interpretation.


## Essential Questions

**1.** How can comparison and contrast produce a deeper interpretation of a body of literature?

**2.** What makes an analytical sentence explain the significance of evidence?

## Guiding Questions

1. What exactly do the selected sources agree or disagree about?

2. How might method, population, context, or definitions explain differences?

3. Where does the draft report evidence without interpreting it?

4. How does the second thematic section advance the central claim?

## Learning outcomes

- Write a comparative-synthesis section using multiple sources.

- Explain agreements, disagreements, and partial convergence accurately.

- Analyze methodological and contextual factors that shape findings.

- Add analytical sentences that explain why evidence matters.

- Revise source-heavy paragraphs to strengthen critical synthesis.

## Key concepts and vocabulary

| **Term**                      | **Working definition**                                                                   |
| :---------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Comparative synthesis**     | Integration of sources through explicit analysis of similarities and differences.        |
| **Convergence**               | A pattern in which sources reach similar findings or interpretations.                    |
| **Divergence**                | A pattern in which sources differ in findings, explanations, or conclusions.             |
| **Methodological difference** | A difference in design, participants, instruments, procedures, or analysis.              |
| **Contextual factor**         | A feature of the setting or population that may shape results.                           |
| **Criterion**                 | A standard or dimension used for comparison.                                             |
| **Analytical sentence**       | A sentence that interprets evidence and explains its significance.                       |
| **Significance**              | The importance or implication of a finding for the review’s argument.                    |
| **Qualification**             | Language that limits a comparison to the evidence and conditions available.              |
| **Critical synthesis**        | An evaluative integration of sources that identifies patterns, limits, and implications. |

## Main chapter content

## Comparing sources by a shared criterion

Comparison is meaningful only when sources are examined according to a common criterion. Two studies can be compared by research question, theoretical perspective, participant group, method, type of intervention, outcome, or conclusion. Without a shared criterion, a paragraph may simply alternate between unrelated details. The topic sentence should name the criterion and make an interpretive claim about the relationship.

Point-by-point organization is often effective. A paragraph may compare how several studies define engagement, followed by another paragraph comparing how engagement is measured. This structure allows multiple sources to interact in each paragraph. Source-by-source blocks can still appear when a study requires explanation, but the writer should return quickly to the shared theme and explain the relationship.

## Representing agreement and disagreement accurately

Agreement is rarely complete. Sources may support a similar general conclusion while differing in strength, population, or mechanism. The writer can describe full agreement, partial agreement, extension, or qualification. Likewise, disagreement may concern definitions or methods rather than the central phenomenon. Precise language prevents oversimplification: “Both studies reported increased revision, although only one measured the quality of the changes” is more informative than “The studies agree.”

When findings conflict, the review should not immediately decide that one source is correct. The writer should investigate possible explanations and evaluate the quality of evidence. Disagreement can become one of the most productive parts of the literature review because it reveals where interpretation depends on design or context. The goal is to map the nature of the conflict and explain what can reasonably be concluded.

## Methodological and contextual explanations

Methods shape what a study can observe. A survey may identify reported perceptions across many participants, while interviews may provide detailed explanations from a smaller group. An experiment may estimate an intervention effect under controlled conditions, while classroom observation may reveal how the practice operates in a natural setting. These methods are not automatically ranked; they answer different questions and carry different limitations.

Context also matters. Findings from a well-resourced university may not transfer directly to a setting with different language proficiency, class size, technology access, or assessment expectations. A comparative section should identify these factors when they help explain variation. Contextual analysis is especially important for English language teaching because language learning practices are influenced by institutional, cultural, and linguistic conditions.

## Writing analytical sentences

An analytical sentence explains why evidence matters for the section claim and central argument. It may identify a pattern, propose a cautious explanation, evaluate the strength of evidence, or state an implication. Useful questions include: What does this comparison reveal? Why might the findings differ? Which conclusion is better supported? What remains uncertain? How does the evidence change the understanding of the problem?

Analytical sentences should follow evidence closely enough that the connection is clear. A paragraph with four citations followed by one general comment may not explain each relationship. Writers can alternate evidence and analysis or group closely related evidence before interpreting it. The analysis should remain grounded; it should not introduce claims that the sources cannot support. Appropriate hedging is especially important when proposing explanations for disagreement.

## Revising the comparative section for critical synthesis

The critical-synthesis revision assignment asks students to identify paragraphs that contain more reporting than interpretation. One strategy is to underline source information and circle analytical commentary. If the paragraph is almost entirely underlined, the writer should add explanation or reduce detail. Another strategy is to label the relationship among sources: agreement, contrast, extension, limitation, or context. If no relationship can be named, the sources may not belong in the same paragraph.

This week’s main deliverable is the second thematic section. It should explain agreements, disagreements, methodological differences, and contextual factors while advancing the literature review’s central claim. Students should verify that every comparison is fair, every citation is accurate, and every paragraph contains a clear answer to the question “Why does this relationship matter?” The section should leave the reader with a more developed understanding than any individual source could provide alone.

## From concepts to this week’s deliverable

Comparative synthesis requires a shared criterion. Two studies can be compared in relation to their definitions, populations, contexts, methods, theoretical assumptions, or findings, but a paragraph becomes confusing when it shifts criteria without warning. Students should state the point of comparison early, represent each source accurately, and then interpret the similarity or difference. Agreement does not make studies interchangeable, and disagreement does not necessarily mean that one is wrong. The writer’s task is to ask what methodological or contextual features might explain the relationship and what that relationship contributes to the literature-review question.

Analytical sentences are the point at which the student’s critical thinking becomes visible. After presenting evidence, the writer can explain significance, identify a boundary, connect a pattern to the central problem, or show how one source complicates another. Useful questions include: What follows from this comparison? Under what conditions does the pattern appear? Which explanation is best supported? What remains uncertain? During the critical-synthesis revision, students should highlight reported material and analytical commentary in different ways. A paragraph dominated by source reporting needs interpretation; a paragraph dominated by evaluation may need additional evidence. The final section should maintain fairness while making a clear, evidence-based contribution to the review’s developing argument.

## Suggested weekly learning sequence

A productive learning sequence for this chapter begins with a diagnostic response to the essential question, “How can comparison and contrast produce a deeper interpretation of a body of literature?” Students should first record an individual answer and then compare it with examples, course criteria, and peer interpretations. A short concept-sorting activity using Comparative synthesis, Convergence, Divergence, and Methodological difference can make important distinctions visible before students work on a full draft. The instructor can then model one decision aloud, showing not only the completed example but also the questions, alternatives, and evidence that led to it. Students apply the same process to a small portion of their own project and receive formative feedback before extending the work independently.

The second part of the week should move from practice to production. Students use the guiding questions—“What exactly do the selected sources agree or disagree about?” and “How might method, population, context, or definitions explain differences?”—as checkpoints while working toward the stated outcomes, especially the ability to write a comparative-synthesis section using multiple sources and to explain agreements, disagreements, and partial convergence accurately. A brief peer conference should focus on one high-impact feature rather than attempting to correct everything at once. Students then revise, annotate the evidence of their decision-making, and complete the week’s deliverable: Second thematic section organized through comparative synthesis; critical-synthesis revision assignment. The final five minutes of class can be used for an exit reflection identifying one decision the writer can now justify and one question that must be carried into the next chapter.
