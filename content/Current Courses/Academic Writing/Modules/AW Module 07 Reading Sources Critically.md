# Academic Writing Module 7: Reading Sources Critically

> [!goal]- # Understanding annotation and evaluating validity, reliability, relevance, diversity, currency, and bias
> 
> An annotated bibliography is more than a reference list with short summaries. It is a record of critical source selection and an early foundation for synthesis. This week students examine how an annotation can describe a source, evaluate its quality, and explain its relevance to a developing research focus. They also apply a source-evaluation matrix to candidate references using criteria such as validity, reliability, relevance, diversity, currency, and bias. The goal is not to create a collection of sources that all agree. A useful bibliography represents the conversation surrounding a topic, including different methods, contexts, findings, and limitations. Because the final literature review will use these same sources, the quality of selection now will shape the strength of the later argument. Each annotation must demonstrate that the student has read, understood, and independently evaluated the source.
>

# Key concepts and vocabulary

| **Term**                     | **Working definition**                                                                                           |
| :--------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Annotated bibliography**   | A list of formatted references accompanied by descriptive and evaluative commentary.                             |
| **Annotation**               | A concise entry that summarizes, evaluates, and explains the relevance of a source.                              |
| **Validity**                 | The extent to which a source’s methods and evidence support the interpretations or conclusions made.             |
| **Reliability**              | The consistency and dependability of a source’s methods, data, or procedures.                                    |
| **Relevance**                | The degree to which a source directly contributes to the research focus or argument.                             |
| **Currency**                 | The timeliness of a source in relation to the topic and field.                                                   |
| **Bias**                     | A perspective or systematic influence that shapes the selection, interpretation, or presentation of information. |
| **Diversity**                | Meaningful variation in perspectives, contexts, methods, populations, or findings.                               |
| **Peer review**              | Evaluation of scholarly work by qualified experts before publication.                                            |
| **Source-evaluation matrix** | A structured table used to compare candidate sources across common criteria.                                     |

> [!goal]- ## Main chapter content
> 
> ## The Annotated Bibliography as a Research Tool
> 
> An annotated bibliography helps a writer move from collecting documents to building a purposeful body of literature. The reference provides the information needed to locate the source. The annotation records what the source contributes, how trustworthy and useful it appears, and why it matters for the project. When annotations are written carefully, they reduce the need to reread an entire article every time the writer plans a section. They also reveal patterns across sources before the formal literature review begins.
> 
> The assignment in this course requires at least ten valid, reliable, and appropriately balanced references. The number is a minimum, not a substitute for quality. Ten nearly identical sources may provide less value than a varied set that addresses the topic through different methods or contexts. The annotated bibliography should therefore be understood as curation. The student is not simply proving that sources were found; the student is explaining why these sources belong together.
> 
> ## Three Functions Within a Strong Annotation
> 
> A complete annotation usually performs three functions: summary, evaluation, and relevance. The summary identifies the source’s purpose, context, method, argument, and principal findings or ideas. It should focus on information useful for the student’s project rather than retell every section. The evaluation considers the quality and limitations of the source. The relevance statement explains how the source may contribute to the research problem, thematic organization, or comparison with other work.
> 
> These functions should be integrated into a coherent paragraph or short series of paragraphs. A mechanical annotation that uses one sentence for each function may meet a checklist but remain shallow. Students should explain connections. For example, a qualitative study’s small sample may limit broad generalization, yet its detailed interviews may provide valuable insight into a local classroom process. Evaluation should not reduce a source to good or bad. It should identify what the source can responsibly support.
> 
> ## Evaluating validity and reliability
> 
> Validity concerns whether the evidence and methods support the claims. In empirical research, students can examine the fit among the research question, participants, instruments, procedures, analysis, and conclusions. In conceptual or theoretical work, validity may involve the quality of reasoning, use of evidence, and treatment of alternative perspectives. A source becomes questionable when its conclusions extend far beyond the data, important terms are undefined, or the methods do not address the stated question.
> 
> Reliability concerns consistency and dependability. Students may look for clear procedures, transparent analysis, appropriate instruments, and enough information to understand how conclusions were reached. Reliability does not mean that every study must be replicable in exactly the same way. Qualitative work may emphasize transparency, credibility, and consistency of interpretation rather than numerical repeatability. Source evaluation should respect the type of research instead of applying one checklist mechanically to every design.
> 
> ## Relevance, currency, diversity, and bias
> 
> A credible source may still be irrelevant to a specific project. Relevance depends on the research focus, population, context, concepts, and intended argument. A study about university writing may offer useful concepts but have limited direct application to young learners. The annotation should state the nature of the relevance rather than imply that every source addresses the exact same setting.
> 
> Currency also depends on the topic. Recent sources are important when technology, policy, or current practice changes rapidly, while older foundational works may remain essential for definitions or influential theories. Diversity strengthens a bibliography when it includes different contexts, methods, populations, and perspectives. Bias should be analyzed rather than assumed absent. All research is produced from a position, but problematic bias appears when sources ignore contradictory evidence, use loaded framing, conceal conflicts of interest, or generalize one perspective as universal.
> 
> ## Creating the evaluation matrix and practice annotation
> 
> The source-evaluation matrix allows students to compare candidate references systematically. Useful columns include full citation, source type, peer-review status, purpose, context, method, key finding, validity, reliability, relevance, currency, perspective or bias, and decision. The decision should be justified: include, exclude, or retain provisionally. A source may be excluded because it duplicates stronger work, lacks methodological transparency, or does not address the revised scope.
