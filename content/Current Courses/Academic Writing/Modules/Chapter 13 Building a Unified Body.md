# Chapter 13: Building a Unified Body


⚡️ Completing thematic sections through sequence, transitions, paragraph unity, and multiple-source synthesis

> As the literature review grows, the main challenge shifts from producing individual sections to making the body function as one argument. This week students complete the remaining thematic sections and conduct a paragraph-level audit. Sequence, transitions, coherence, and cohesion are examined within and across themes. Each paragraph should advance one controlling idea, integrate reliable evidence, and contain enough analytical commentary to explain significance. Reverse outlining becomes a central revision method because it reveals the actual logic of the draft rather than the logic the writer intended. The goal is a body in which sections build on one another, sources are applied purposefully, and no paragraph exists only to summarize an author. By the end of the week, the complete body should provide the analytical foundation for the conclusion.


## Essential Questions




1. How can multiple thematic sections function as one coherent argument?

2. What paragraph-level features distinguish synthesis from a sequence of summaries?

## Guiding Questions




1. Does the order of sections reflect a clear organizing principle?

2. How do transitions express relationships within and across themes?

3. Does each paragraph contain one controlling idea, evidence, and sufficient commentary?

4. What does a reverse outline reveal about repetition, gaps, and misplaced content?

## Learning outcomes

• Complete the remaining body sections of the literature review.

• Revise the sequence of sections and paragraphs for coherence.

• Use transitions and cohesive ties accurately.

• Audit paragraph unity, source application, and analytical commentary.

• Create and use a reverse outline to identify structural revision needs.

## Key concepts and vocabulary

| **Term**                  | **Working definition**                                                             |
| :------------------------ | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Sequence**              | The purposeful order in which sections, paragraphs, and ideas appear.              |
| **Coherence**             | The overall logical unity and intelligibility of the argument.                     |
| **Cohesion**              | The linguistic connections that help readers move between sentences and ideas.     |
| **Controlling idea**      | The single main point that governs a paragraph.                                    |
| **Paragraph audit**       | A systematic check of paragraph function, evidence, analysis, and connection.      |
| **Reverse outline**       | A map created from the draft to reveal what each paragraph actually does.          |
| **Thematic progression**  | The way topics and information develop from one sentence or paragraph to the next. |
| **Lexical chain**         | A series of related words that maintains topic continuity.                         |
| **Source application**    | The purposeful use of source evidence to develop a claim.                          |
| **Analytical commentary** | The writer’s explanation of the meaning and importance of evidence.                |

## Main chapter content

## Sequencing sections according to the organizing principle

The body should follow a logic that readers can recognize. If the organizing principle is thematic, the sequence may move from foundational concepts to classroom practices, from common findings to complications, or from individual factors to institutional conditions. The best order is not always the order in which the writer discovered the themes. It is the order that most clearly develops the central claim.

Students should write one sentence describing the function of each section and place the sentences in sequence. This section-level outline reveals whether the review builds cumulatively or jumps between unrelated issues. Transitions between sections should explain the relationship: extension, contrast, consequence, limitation, or shift in level. A heading alone marks a boundary but does not explain why the next theme follows.

## Creating coherence across paragraphs

Paragraph coherence depends on a clear controlling idea and logical development. A topic sentence should make a claim or establish a specific focus, not merely announce that several authors studied the topic. Evidence and analysis should then remain connected to that focus. When a paragraph changes from method to finding to policy implication without explanation, readers may struggle to identify its purpose.

Writers can improve coherence by using predictable patterns. A paragraph may present a common finding, compare evidence, analyze a limitation, and state an implication. Another may introduce a disagreement, explain methodological differences, and qualify the section claim. Patterns should serve the argument rather than become rigid formulas. The key is that readers can understand why each sentence appears where it does.

## Using cohesive ties without overloading transitions

Cohesion is created through more than transitional adverbs. Repetition of key terms helps maintain focus, especially in academic writing where unnecessary synonym variation can create confusion. Pronouns and demonstratives such as this finding or these differences can connect sentences, but their references must be clear. Parallel grammatical structures can show comparison, and lexical chains can maintain continuity through related terms.

Transitions should name genuine relationships. However signals contrast; therefore signals a result; similarly signals comparison; in this context limits a claim. Using transitions at the beginning of every sentence can make the prose mechanical. Students should combine explicit transitions with topic continuity and sentence structure. A cohesive paragraph often moves from known information at the beginning of a sentence to new information at the end, allowing the next sentence to build from what has just been introduced.

## Auditing unity, evidence, and commentary

The paragraph-level audit asks four questions. What is the controlling idea? Which sources provide evidence? What analytical commentary explains the evidence? How does the paragraph connect to the section claim? If one of these questions cannot be answered, the paragraph requires revision. A paragraph may need stronger evidence, more analysis, a clearer topic sentence, or relocation to another section.

Multiple-source synthesis should be purposeful. A paragraph does not need several citations merely to appear scholarly. Some claims require one detailed source; others require comparison across several. The audit should check whether each source is applied for a reason and whether citations accurately represent the evidence. Paragraphs that summarize one source at a time should be reconsidered unless the source requires focused explanation before comparison.

## Using the reverse outline to complete the body

A reverse outline is created after drafting by writing a short statement of each paragraph’s actual function. The statements can be compared with the thematic outline prepared in Week 9. Repetition becomes visible when several paragraphs make the same point. Gaps appear when an important section claim has little support. Misplaced paragraphs become obvious when their function fits another theme. The reverse outline can also reveal imbalance, such as one theme receiving half the word count while another remains underdeveloped.

This week students complete the remaining body sections, revise sequence and transitions, and conduct the paragraph-level literature-review audit. The body should now contain the majority of the required 2,500 words and use the ten Unit III sources in ways that support the central argument. The quality criterion is not whether every source appears equally often. It is whether each paragraph advances one idea, represents evidence accurately, and contributes analytical meaning to the review as a whole.

## From concepts to this week’s deliverable

Completing the body requires decisions about sequence at two levels. At the section level, the organizing principle should determine whether themes move from foundational to contested, general to specific, problem to response, or another defensible order. At the paragraph level, each claim should prepare the reader for the next. Topic sentences need not repeat the same formula, but they should signal the paragraph’s role in the section. Transitional language is useful only when the conceptual connection already exists; adding “however” cannot create a contrast that the sentences do not actually express. Writers should therefore revise the logic first and the connectors second.

The reverse outline and paragraph audit provide evidence for these decisions. In the margin or a separate table, students record the main function of every paragraph, the sources used, and the analytical contribution. Repeated functions may reveal redundancy; a paragraph with several unrelated functions may need division; a section with no counterpoint may need a broader reading of the existing evidence. The paragraph-level audit checks unity, sufficient evidence, accurate citation, analysis, and connection to the central question. By the end of the week, the literature review should have a complete body whose sections work together as one argument rather than as a collection of source summaries.

## Suggested weekly learning sequence

A productive learning sequence for this chapter begins with a diagnostic response to the essential question, “How can multiple thematic sections function as one coherent argument?” Students should first record an individual answer and then compare it with examples, course criteria, and peer interpretations. A short concept-sorting activity using Sequence, Coherence, Cohesion, and Controlling idea can make important distinctions visible before students work on a full draft. The instructor can then model one decision aloud, showing not only the completed example but also the questions, alternatives, and evidence that led to it. Students apply the same process to a small portion of their own project and receive formative feedback before extending the work independently.

The second part of the week should move from practice to production. Students use the guiding questions—“Does the order of sections reflect a clear organizing principle?” and “How do transitions express relationships within and across themes?”—as checkpoints while working toward the stated outcomes, especially the ability to complete the remaining body sections of the literature review and to revise the sequence of sections and paragraphs for coherence. A brief peer conference should focus on one high-impact feature rather than attempting to correct everything at once. Students then revise, annotate the evidence of their decision-making, and complete the week’s deliverable: Completed remaining body sections; reverse outline; paragraph-level literature-review audit. The final five minutes of class can be used for an exit reflection identifying one decision the writer can now justify and one question that must be carried into the next chapter.
