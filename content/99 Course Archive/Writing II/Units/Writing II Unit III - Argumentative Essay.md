# Writing II Unit III - Argumentative Essay

# What is an Argumentative Essay?

An argumentative essay is a genre of academic writing whose primary purpose is to **persuade** readers to accept a particular position on a debatable issue. Unlike descriptive writing, which creates sensory impressions, or expository writing, which explains a concept neutrally, argumentative writing takes a **clear stance** and defends it through reasoned claims, credible evidence, and thoughtful engagement with opposing views. For students in Writing II at the CEFR B1+ level, mastering argumentative writing develops critical skills in logical reasoning, evidence evaluation, and persuasive communication—competencies essential for academic discourse and civic participation.

### The Purpose of Argumentative Writing

Argumentative writing exists to change minds or strengthen convictions through reasoned appeal. When you write an argumentative essay, you are not merely expressing an opinion or preference. Instead, you are advancing a **defensible claim** about a debatable issue and building a logical case that respects your readers' intelligence while guiding them toward your position.

In this course, your argumentative essay will address an issue relevant to student life, education, or your local community. Your audience expects **clear reasoning, credible evidence, and fair treatment of alternative views**. Your purpose is to persuade informed but undecided readers that your position is sound, reasonable, and worthy of support.

### Key Features of Argumentative Essays

**A Clear, Debatable Claim**

Every argumentative essay centers on a **thesis statement** that presents a debatable claim. Unlike expository thesis statements that announce a topic to explain, argumentative thesis statements take a **position** that reasonable people might disagree with. Consider these examples:

- *Not debatable*: "Many students use AI grammar tools." (This is a factual observation, not a claim to argue.)

- *Debatable*: "First-year ELT students should be permitted to use AI grammar tools during drafting—with required disclosure—because doing so accelerates language acquisition while maintaining academic integrity."

A strong argumentative thesis is **specific** (naming the position clearly), **debatable** (inviting disagreement), and often **forecasting** (hinting at the main reasons that will follow). Your claim acts as the anchor for your entire essay, determining which evidence to include and which counterarguments to address.

**Reasons and Evidence**

A claim alone does not persuade. Effective argumentative essays support the claim with **reasons**—statements that explain why the claim is valid—and **evidence**—concrete support that substantiates each reason. Evidence may include:

- **Facts and statistics** from credible sources

- **Expert testimony** or scholarly consensus

- **Examples and case studies** that illustrate the claim in action

- **Logical reasoning** that connects premises to conclusions

For instance, if your claim advocates permitting AI grammar tools for first-year students, one reason might be: "AI tools provide immediate, targeted feedback that accelerates error correction." Evidence supporting this reason could include research findings on corrective feedback timing, student self-reports of learning gains, or comparisons of draft improvement rates with and without AI assistance.

Crucially, evidence must be **relevant** (directly supporting the reason), **sufficient** (enough to be convincing), and **credible** (from trustworthy, verifiable sources). Weak or tangential evidence undermines your argument, even if your claim is sound.

**Addressing Counterarguments**

One hallmark of strong argumentative writing is the **acknowledgment and refutation of counterarguments**. Counterarguments are objections or alternative positions that reasonable opponents might raise. Addressing them demonstrates intellectual honesty, strengthens your credibility, and deepens readers' understanding of the issue's complexity.

There are several strategies for engaging counterarguments:

- **Concede and refute**: Acknowledge the validity of a counterargument in certain contexts, then explain why your position remains stronger overall. ("While critics rightly note that AI tools can mask persistent grammar gaps, required disclosure and periodic unassisted assessments ensure accountability.")

- **Rebut with evidence**: Present evidence that directly contradicts the counterargument. ("Opponents claim AI dependence harms long-term learning, yet recent studies show students who use AI tools with metacognitive reflection outperform control groups on delayed post-tests.")

- **Reframe the issue**: Show that the counterargument rests on a misunderstanding or addresses a different concern. ("The objection conflates grammar assistance with content generation; permitting grammar checking does not compromise originality of ideas.")

Effectively addressing counterarguments shows readers that you have considered multiple perspectives and that your position withstands scrutiny.

**Logical Organization and Structure**

Argumentative essays follow organizational patterns that guide readers through the logic of the case. Common structures include:

- **Classical (reason-by-reason)**: Introduction with claim → Body paragraphs, each developing one reason with evidence → Counterargument section → Conclusion. This structure works well when you have distinct, equally weighted reasons.

- **Counterargument early**: Introduction with claim → Acknowledge and refute counterarguments → Present your reasons and evidence → Conclusion. This approach clears objections before building your case.

- **Counterargument integrated**: Raise and refute counterarguments within individual body paragraphs as they become relevant. This structure maintains momentum and shows responsiveness.

Regardless of structure, each body paragraph should have a **clear topic sentence** that signals the paragraph's role in the argument, followed by supporting evidence and explanation. **Transitions** are especially important in argumentative writing because they reveal logical relationships: *therefore*, *consequently*, *however*, *in contrast*, *furthermore*, *granted that*, *even though*.

**Formal, Objective Tone**

Argumentative writing at the academic level employs a **formal, objective tone** that conveys respect for the issue and the audience. This means:

- Avoiding informal language, slang, or colloquialisms

- Using precise, discipline-appropriate vocabulary

- Minimizing first-person pronouns unless discussing personal expertise or experience directly relevant to the argument

- Refraining from emotional appeals, insults, or dismissive language toward opponents

- Maintaining measured phrasing even when disagreeing strongly

Instead of "It's totally obvious that anyone who opposes this is wrong," write "The evidence strongly supports this position" or "Alternative views do not adequately account for recent findings." Formal tone strengthens your credibility and signals that your argument rests on logic rather than emotion.

**Evidence Integration and APA Citation**

When you incorporate evidence from outside sources, you must **integrate** it smoothly into your prose and **cite** it properly using APA format. Integration involves:

- **Introducing** the source ("According to recent research by García and Chen (2025)...")

- **Presenting** the evidence through quotation, paraphrase, or summary

- **Explaining** how the evidence supports your reason

Proper citation serves two purposes: it allows readers to verify your sources, and it maintains **academic integrity** by distinguishing your ideas from those of others. In this course, unauthorized use of AI-generated content without disclosure constitutes plagiarism, just as failing to cite human authors does.

**Language Control at B1+**

As a B1+ writer, you are expected to produce **connected, straightforward texts** with clear logical progression and mostly accurate grammar and vocabulary. Argumentative writing at B1+ demonstrates:

- **Complex sentence structures** that show relationships between ideas (e.g., subordinate clauses introduced by *although*, *because*, *while*)

- **Accurate use of modal verbs** to express degrees of certainty (*may*, *might*, *should*, *must*)

- **Appropriate use of hedging language** to make claims more precise (*often*, *tends to*, *in many cases*, *suggests that*)

- **Consistent verb tense** (typically simple present for general claims and evidence discussion)

- **Clarity maintained** even when occasional errors occur

### The Argumentative Writing Process

Effective argumentative essays emerge from a deliberate process:

**Pre-Writing**

Begin by **selecting a debatable issue** that matters to you and your audience. Your issue should be **narrow enough** to address thoroughly in 900–1,100 words. Broad topics ("education reform") are unmanageable; focused issues ("whether UAA should require peer review workshops in all writing courses") are arguable.

Next, **formulate your claim** and **generate reasons**. Ask yourself: Why do I hold this position? What are the strongest arguments in its favor? Then **anticipate counterarguments**: What would opponents say? What evidence challenges my view?

Finally, **locate and evaluate evidence**. Prioritize credible sources: peer-reviewed research, institutional reports, expert testimony. Create an **outline** mapping your claim, reasons, evidence, and planned counterargument engagement.

**Drafting**

Translate your outline into connected prose. Your **introduction** should establish context, introduce the issue, and present your claim clearly. Each **body paragraph** should develop one reason with evidence and explanation, using topic sentences and transitions to maintain logical flow. Your **counterargument section** should fairly represent opposing views before refuting them. Your **conclusion** should reinforce your claim's significance without merely repeating the introduction.

**Revising**

Revision is where adequate arguments become persuasive ones. Approach revision in layers:

- **Global revision**: Does every reason support my claim? Is my evidence sufficient and credible? Have I addressed the strongest counterarguments? Is my organizational logic clear?

- **Paragraph revision**: Does each paragraph have unity? Are topic sentences clear? Do I explain how evidence supports my reasons?

- **Local revision**: Are my sentences clear and concise? Is my tone appropriately formal and objective? Do I avoid logical fallacies (hasty generalizations, false dilemmas, ad hominem attacks)?

Peer feedback, instructor comments, and self-assessment using revision checklists help identify weaknesses. Strong revision often means strengthening evidence, reordering reasons for better impact, or expanding counterargument engagement.

**Editing**

After revising for content and logic, **edit** for correctness: grammar, punctuation, word choice, and APA formatting. Editing ensures that surface errors do not distract readers from your reasoning or undermine your credibility.

### Why Argumentative Writing Matters

Argumentative writing is central to academic inquiry and democratic participation. Research articles argue for the validity of findings. Policy proposals argue for specific courses of action. Grant applications argue for funding priorities. When you can construct and defend a reasoned position, you participate meaningfully in scholarly and civic conversations.

Beyond formal writing, argumentative skills transfer to oral presentations, professional reports, and everyday decision-making. The ability to weigh evidence, consider multiple perspectives, and build logical cases is valued across disciplines and professions.

As you move through Unit III, remember that effective argumentation is logical, evidence-based, and fair-minded. Your task is not to bully readers into agreement or dismiss opposing views, but to **build a case so clear and well-supported** that informed readers find your position compelling. By revising deliberately and editing carefully, you will produce a 900–1,100 word argumentative essay that demonstrates B1+ language control while advancing a reasoned position on an issue that matters to your community.
