# Writing II Module 12

## Monday

## Instruction

1. Bookmark and begin reviewing Writing II: Unit III - Argumentative Essay.

2. Review A Claim.

    1. An initial claim is the main position of the writer. The writer is for or against something or they agree or disagree with something.

    2. A counterclaim is to state what is wrong or weak about the initial claim.

    3. A rebuttal is to state what is wrong or weak about the counterclaim.

## Action Steps - For a Grade

1. Write your name and date at the top of a clean sheet of paper.

2. Below your name and date, write your assigned prompt either as an initial claim or a counterclaim (not both). Choose a position or take stance either for something or against something, etc. After you write your initial claim or counterclaim, label your position as either an “initial claim” or “counterclaim”.

3. Below your initial or counterclaim, list three to five *specific examples* - from personal experience - that support your position. Do not look up any information online.

4. Below your brainstorming list of specific examples, develop *one five-sentence paragraph, writing in the third person and avoid subjective tone like saying, “it is good/better/worse”, etc*.

    1. Create a topic sentence (main idea) of your point, your position, or one that supports the side you are taking.

    2. Describe the examples you listed in two (evidence) sentences and state what, how, why, when, where, with whom, etc. Again, examples should be specific and should be based on your experiences, so no citations and avoid looking anything up.

    3. Create one analysis sentence where you comment, explain, analyze, etc. that links the evidence to the main idea (topic sentence).

    4. Conclude your paragraph with a linking sentence that would segue into another paragraph or you may include a summarizing sentence.

5. Once you have completed your five-sentence paragraph, write below your paragraph the three parts of an introduction paragraph.

6. Hand in your work in class today - there is nothing to submit to Microsoft Teams.

- Examples

    ### Example 1 (New topic: **Year-round school calendar**)

    **Thesis statement:**

    Schools should adopt a year-round calendar because it reduces summer learning loss, supports consistent routines, and uses school facilities more efficiently.

    **Topic sentences (claim → counterclaim → rebuttal):**

    1. **Initial claim support:** (I believe that) A year-round calendar helps students retain skills by shortening the long summer break when many learners forget key content.

    2. **Counterclaim support:** (Some people say) switching to year-round schooling can disrupt family schedules and limit traditional summer childcare and vacation plans.

    3. **Rebuttal support:** (Even so/however), schools can reduce these disruptions by offering intersession programs and publishing schedules far in advance so families can plan.

    ---

    ### Example 2 (New topic: **Anonymous reporting systems for bullying and threats**)

    **Thesis statement:**

    Schools should implement anonymous reporting systems because they increase student safety, encourage earlier intervention, and reduce fear of retaliation.

    **Topic sentences (claim → counterclaim → rebuttal):**

    1. **Initial claim support:** Anonymous reporting makes it more likely that students will report bullying or threats when they are afraid of being targeted for speaking up.

    2. **Counterclaim support:** Critics argue that anonymous systems can lead to false reports that waste administrators’ time and unfairly accuse students.

    3. **Rebuttal support:** Nevertheless, clear investigation procedures and consequences for deliberate misuse can limit false claims while still protecting students who report real concerns.

    ---

    ### Example 3 (New topic: **Later start time for extracurricular activities (sports/clubs)**)

    **Thesis statement:**

    Schools should start extracurricular activities later in the afternoon because it improves academic focus, reduces student stress, and makes participation more equitable for students with transportation or family responsibilities.

    **Topic sentences (claim → counterclaim → rebuttal):**

    1. **Initial claim support:** When practices and club meetings begin later, students have more time to complete homework and attend tutoring before activities.

    2. **Counterclaim support:** Opponents claim that later activities would end too late, increasing safety risks and interfering with dinner, work, and family schedules.

    3. **Rebuttal support:** Still, schools can address timing concerns by shortening practice sessions on weekdays and coordinating shared transportation so students get home safely and predictably.

Today’s class is designed for those who either missed class on April 13, 2026 or would like to redo the graded assignment; otherwise, you do not need to attend class today. No phones may be used to complete the assignment.

1. Write your name and date at the top of a clean sheet of paper.

2. Below your name and date, write your assigned prompt either as an initial claim or a counterclaim (not both). Choose a position or take stance either for something or against something, etc. After you write your initial claim or counterclaim, label your position as either an “initial claim” or “counterclaim”.

3. Below your initial or counterclaim, list three to five *specific examples* - from personal experience - that support your position. Do not look up any information online.

4. Below your brainstorming list of specific examples, develop *one five-sentence paragraph, writing in the third person and avoid subjective tone like saying, “it is good/better/worse”, etc*.

    1. Create a topic sentence (main idea) of your point, your position, or one that supports the side you are taking.

    2. Describe the examples you listed in two (evidence) sentences and state what, how, why, when, where, with whom, etc. Again, examples should be specific and should be based on your experiences, so no citations and avoid looking anything up.

    3. Create one analysis sentence where you comment, explain, analyze, etc. that links the evidence to the main idea (topic sentence).
