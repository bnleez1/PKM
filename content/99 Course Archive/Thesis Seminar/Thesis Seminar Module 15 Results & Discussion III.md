## What constitutes a completed first draft?

1. Completed introductory pages from the template provided the first day of class: title page, approval page (no signatures), Abstract, table of contents, list of tables (if any), and list of figures (if any). You should have some tables and/or figures in your list(s), and each of the listed items above go on a separate page.

2. The title of your paper should begin the next page (with a page break) after your tables and/or figures, followed by a 250-word Introduction Paragraph and 2,250-word literature review. The last paragraph of your literature review should include a Transitional Paragraph.

3. After your literature review, include a 500-word method section (with no page break).

4. After the method section, include a 2,250-word results and discussion section (with no page break).

5. After the results and discussion section, include a 250-word Conclusion Paragraph (with no page break and no heading).

6. After the conclusion, include the references page (with a page break).

7. After the list of references, include each appendix on a separate page (with a page break for each appendix).

8. Each citation should have a reference and each reference should have at least one citation. You should have citations to support your evidence sentences in the literature review and a few citations to compare your results and/or discussion.

### 🎯 Understanding

**Instructors will understand that…**

Students can strengthen the clarity and credibility of a Results & Discussion chapter by:

- selecting the most relevant findings (not all findings) and organizing them around clear themes/patterns

- interpreting results with appropriate caution (what the data *suggests* vs. what it *proves*)

- connecting evidence (tables/quotes/codes) to claims using the M.E.A.L. structure

- relating findings back to research questions and prior literature to show contribution and meaning

### 🎯 Language Objective

**Students will be able to…**

- use results-reporting language to describe patterns, frequencies, and trends (e.g., *The data indicate…; A majority of participants…; This suggests…*)

- write discussion moves that (1) interpret findings, (2) compare with literature, and (3) acknowledge limitations

- produce one revised “Results + mini-discussion” paragraph that includes: a claim, specific evidence, interpretation, and a link back to a research question

### 🎯 Vocabulary

**Key terms (Module 15)**

- **finding / result** (a key outcome from your data)

- **evidence** (data that supports a claim: quotes, counts, excerpts, tables)

- **theme / pattern** (a repeated idea or trend in the data)

- **interpretation** (your explanation of what the results mean)

- **implication** (why the finding matters; what it suggests for practice/theory)

- **limitation** (a constraint that may affect conclusions)

- **generalizability** (how far results can be applied beyond the sample/context)

- **consistency / discrepancy** (alignment or mismatch with prior studies)

- **significance** (importance/meaning; not necessarily statistical)

- **claim** (your main point about what the evidence shows)
