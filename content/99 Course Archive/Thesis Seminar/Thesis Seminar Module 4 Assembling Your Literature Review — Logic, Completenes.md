# Thesis Seminar Module 4:  Assembling Your Literature Review — Logic, Completeness, and Analysis

[Thesis Seminar Recap #4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_XWC-DVAja0)[Thesis Seminar Recap #4 - Notes](../Weblinks/Thesis%20Seminar%20Recap%20#4%20(1).md)

### 🎯 Understanding

**Instructors will understand that…**

- **A complete literature review draft** requires all major sections (2-4) to be fully developed, with each section explicitly aligned to one thesis key point and addressing specific research questions.

- **Organizational logic drives argumentation**: sections should build a cumulative case for the research problem and proposed approach, not simply chronicle the field's historical development unless chronology directly serves the research questions.

- **Analytical language shifts the writer's stance** from reporter to interpreter—moving from "Author X found Y" to "This finding suggests..." or "These results indicate that..." demonstrates critical engagement with sources rather than passive summary.

### 🎯 Language Objective

**Students will be able to…**

- **Draft** complete versions of all 2-4 major sections, ensuring each section has multiple paragraphs that fully develop one key point from the thesis statement.

- **Organize** sections in a logical sequence that builds an argument (thematic, problem-solution, comparison-contrast) rather than defaulting to chronological order unless the research questions explicitly require historical perspective.

- **Compose** analytical sentences that interpret findings using hedging and interpretive language (e.g., "This pattern suggests," "These results indicate that," "This tension reveals") rather than merely reporting what authors found.

### 🎯 Vocabulary

- **Complete draft** — a full version of the literature review with all major sections developed through multiple paragraphs, not just outlined or partially written

- **Logical sequence** — an organizational order that serves the argument's needs (building complexity, contrasting approaches, moving from problem to solutions) rather than chronological order

- **Chronological organization** — structuring content by time periods or historical development (avoid unless temporal progression is central to research questions)

- **Analytical verbs** — verbs that signal interpretation: demonstrates, reveals, suggests, indicates, implies (vs. reporting verbs: found, stated, reported)

- **Hedging** — using cautious language to make claims appropriately (may, might, appears to, seems to suggest)

### 🎯 Comments

> Schedule time with me online or in-person immediately if you are struggling to find articles to support your literature review – do not wait until your next tutoring session.

- During weeks 1, 2, and 3: Read as many articles as necessary; complete a problem statement, thesis statement, section outline; submit first body paragraph; continue drafting body paragraphs according to key sections.

- Remember to keep revisiting your thesis statement, making sure that the key sections of your literature review directly (and explicitly) link to the key points at the end of your thesis statement and your research questions. We want to make sure that when you begin collecting your data on March 1, 2024, that your study relates to your literature review.

- Avoid organizing your literature review as a "history lesson". It's fine to include some of the old with the new if it relates to your research questions and overall objective of your study: to address a problem. You address a problem by organizing your thesis and literature review sections by proposing possible solutions to the problem.
