### Overarching Themes

Throughout these lessons, emphasis was placed on **academic integrity**, **systematic literature searching**, **precise problem framing**, and **structured writing** that maintains originality while synthesizing scholarly sources.

## 🎯 Understanding

**Instructors will understand that…**

- A researchable problem in applied linguistics/SLA must be **narrow enough** to study qualitatively, with clear boundaries around context, participants, and phenomena.

- A strong **problem statement** articulates not only what is unknown but also **why it matters**—showing the gap between current knowledge and what practitioners or researchers need to know.

- A **working thesis statement** with 2–4 key points serves as a provisional roadmap, where each key point becomes a section heading that organizes the literature review and signals the argument's structure.

- **Systematic literature search** using databases (e.g., ERIC, JSTOR, Google Scholar) and deliberate search strategies (Boolean operators, filtering by peer-review, date range, etc.) is essential to building a credible and comprehensive review.

- A **research matrix** is a living tool that tracks sources, organizes emerging themes, and allows the writer to see patterns, gaps, and connections across the literature—supporting both analysis and writing.

### 🎯 Language Objective

**Students will be able to…**

- **Describe** a researchable problem using precise academic language that specifies the context, population, and phenomenon under investigation.

- **Justify** the significance of a research problem by explaining gaps in current knowledge using phrases like "little is known about," "few studies have examined," and "there is limited research on."

- **Draft** a working thesis statement with 2–4 parallel key points using noun phrases or gerunds that signal the organizational structure of the literature review.

- **Formulate** Boolean search queries combining keywords, synonyms, and related terms (AND, OR, NOT) to retrieve relevant peer-reviewed sources.

- **Synthesize** information from multiple sources in a research matrix, using comparative language ("whereas," "similarly," "in contrast") to show relationships between studies.

### 🎯 Vocabulary

**Research design & scope**

- **Researchable problem** — a focused question or issue that can be systematically investigated

- **Qualitative study** — research that explores phenomena through non-numerical data (interviews, observations, documents) - Qualitative Systemic Literature Review

- **Unit of analysis** — the entity being studied (e.g., individual teachers, classroom interactions, curriculum materials)

- **Gap in the literature** — an area where current research is insufficient, contradictory, or absent

**Problem framing**

- **Problem Statement** — a concise description of the issue to be addressed, including context, significance, and what is unknown

- **Thesis statement** — a sentence that presents the main argument and organizational structure of the paper, typically with 2–4 key points

- **Thesis statement and skeleton outline** — a preliminary structure showing section headings derived from thesis statement key points

**Literature search & sources**

- **Boolean operators** — logical connectors (AND, OR, NOT) used to combine or exclude search terms

- **Peer-reviewed** — scholarly articles that have been evaluated by experts before publication

- **Database** — an organized collection of academic sources (e.g., ERIC, JSTOR, Google Scholar)

- **Seminal study** — a foundational or highly influential piece of research in a field

- **Recent literature** — current research, typically from within the last five years.

**Analysis & writing**

- **Research matrix** — a table or spreadsheet that tracks sources and organizes key information (author, year, methods, findings, themes)

- **Synthesis** — combining information from multiple sources to identify patterns, relationships, or new insights - For more, see How to Synthesize information.

- **Citation** — a reference to a source, following a style guide (e.g., APA)

- **Parenthetical reference** — an in-text citation that includes author and year in parentheses - See **Parenthetical Vs. Narrative Citations**

    # Open Access Journals

    - How to choose a quality Open-Access (OA) journal

        ## 1) Start with **scope & audience fit**

        - **Why it matters.** Even reputable journals reject solid work that doesn’t match their remit.

        - **How to check (2–3 minutes).** Open the journal’s *Aims & Scope* and skim 2–3 recent articles; make sure they actually publish on your topic (e.g., TESOL/TEFL, teacher education, CALL). This is judgment‑based and doesn’t require an external source.

        ---

        ## 2) Verify **OA signals** in a trusted directory (DOAJ)

        - **Why it matters.** The **Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ)** is the most widely used index of vetted OA journals; each record states whether a **fee** is charged, the **license** used, and whether content is **archived/preserved**—all essential quality markers. journalsinsights.com([https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list](https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list))

        - **How to check (1 minute).** Search the journal in DOAJ and confirm:

            - **Fee status** (No fees/Diamond vs. APC) and a transparent fee statement if APCs apply. journalsinsights.com([https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list](https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list))

            - **License** (e.g., CC BY/CC BY‑NC‑ND) so readers know how the work may be reused. journalsinsights.com([https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list](https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list))

            - **Archiving/preservation** (e.g., Portico/CLOCKSS or national libraries) to ensure long‑term access. journalsinsights.com([https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list](https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list))

        > Example (what good looks like): DOAJ records typically display “No APC,” a CC license, and named preservation services right on the journal page. [journalsinsights.com]

        ---

        ## 3) Understand the **OA model** (Diamond vs. APC‑funded)

        - **Diamond OA (💎)** = free for **both** authors and readers; typically society/university‑run and aligned with equity goals in UNESCO’s open‑science guidance. academia.edu([https://www.academia.edu/105544470/Profile_Issues_in_Teachers_and_apos_Professional_Development](https://www.academia.edu/105544470/Profile_Issues_in_Teachers_and_apos_Professional_Development))

        - **APC‑funded OA (💰)** = free to read, but authors (or their institutions/funders) pay an **Article Processing Charge**; reputable publishers post fees and waiver policies up front (e.g., SpringerOpen shows the current APC, transformative‑agreement coverage, and waiver routes **on the journal’s “How to publish” page**).

        ---

        ## 4) Confirm **peer‑review, ethics, and governance**

        - **Why it matters.** Quality journals disclose **double‑blind peer review** (or the model used), editorial policies, and governance (editorial board with verifiable affiliations). Many of these transparency points are part of the **information DOAJ requests/records** for listed journals. journalsinsights.com([https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list](https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list))

        - **How to check (2 minutes).** Look for a *Peer‑review policy* page, *Ethics* or *Publication policies*, and an *Editorial board* with institutional affiliations you can verify on university pages. (If these are missing or vague, that’s a red flag—see §8.)

        ---

        ## 5) Check **indexing/discoverability**

        - **Why it matters.** Inclusion in DOAJ is the baseline for OA quality; discipline indexes (e.g., ERIC/Scopus/ESCI) improve visibility, but the first stop is still DOAJ because it surfaces fees, license, and archiving in one place. journalsinsights.com([https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list](https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list))

        - **How to check (1 minute).** Confirm the DOAJ record; if it’s absent, you’ll need stronger evidence from the publisher about fees, licensing, and archiving. journalsinsights.com([https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list](https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list))

        ---

        ## 6) Inspect the **license & author rights**

        - **Why it matters.** A clear **Creative Commons license** (e.g., CC BY) tells you how work can be reused and whether derivatives are allowed; DOAJ records carry this field explicitly. journalsinsights.com([https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list](https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list))

        - **How to check (30 seconds).** Find the license on the article page or the DOAJ record; prefer journals where **authors retain copyright** and articles get **DOIs**. journalsinsights.com([https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list](https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list))

        ---

        ## 7) **Fees & waivers** (if APC‑funded)

        - **Why it matters.** Reputable APC journals state **the price, VAT handling, discounts/waivers**, and institutional read‑and‑publish coverage right on their site. For instance, *Language Testing in Asia* lists the exact APC and explains agreement coverage and waiver policies on its “How to publish” page.

        - **How to check (1 minute).** Find the **APC table** and **waiver policy** on the journal/publisher page; if you can’t, treat that as cautionary.

        ---

        ## 8) Common **red flags**

        - **Unclear or hidden APCs**, requests for payment **before** review, or “**publish in 1–2 weeks**” promises. These contradict the transparency and quality items DOAJ emphasizes. journalsinsights.com([https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list](https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list))

        - **No DOAJ record**, no stated license, or no archiving info. Again, DOAJ surfaces these fundamentals; their absence on the site is a warning. journalsinsights.com([https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list](https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list))

        - **Editorial board** without verifiable affiliations; generic or copy‑pasted scopes/policies. (You can often catch this by cross‑checking names on university pages—no external source needed.)

        ---

        ## 9) A 10‑minute **vetting workflow**

        1. **Find the DOAJ record** → confirm **fees**, **license**, and **archiving** in one view. journalsinsights.com([https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list](https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list))

        2. **Open two recent articles** → skim methods/ethics sections for quality and fit (TESOL/TEFL, teacher ed, CALL, etc.).

        3. **Check the peer‑review/ethics page** → look for double‑blind (or specified) review, plagiarism checks, and COI policies; journals listed in DOAJ are expected to show these elements on site. journalsinsights.com([https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list](https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list))

        4. **If APC‑funded**, verify **price + waivers** on the publisher page (e.g., Springer’s OA journals document the current APC, agreements, and waivers).

        5. **Send a 2–3 sentence “fit” query** to the editor and evaluate the professionalism and clarity of the reply.

        ---

        ## 10) Two quick **illustrations**

        - **APC‑funded example (transparent):** *Language Testing in Asia* (SpringerOpen) publishes the **current APC**, mentions institution **agreements**, and points to **waiver support**—the basics you want to see for paid OA.

        - **Diamond OA example (no fees):** DOAJ records frequently show “**No APC**,” the **CC license**, and **archiving** at a glance; that’s the pattern to prefer when students need **cost‑free** routes. journalsinsights.com([https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list](https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list))

        ---

        ## 11) Why this approach works (what the sources say)

        - **DOAJ** explicitly aims to “increase visibility and facilitate use of OA journals,” and its records expose the **fee status, license, and archiving** you need for a quick quality screen. journalsinsights.com([https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list](https://journalsinsights.com/predatory-journals-list))

        - **UNESCO** frames **Diamond OA** as the equitable, community‑driven model with no fees to authors or readers—useful context for helping students avoid cost barriers while still publishing in reputable outlets. academia.edu([https://www.academia.edu/105544470/Profile_Issues_in_Teachers_and_apos_Professional_Development](https://www.academia.edu/105544470/Profile_Issues_in_Teachers_and_apos_Professional_Development))

        ---

        ### A concise rule of thumb

        > “If a journal has a DOAJ record with clear license, archiving, and (if relevant) APC/waiver info—and the recent articles match your topic and quality bar—you’re likely in safe territory.” [journalsinsights.com]

    - Introduction Paragraph - 200 words

        - Begin with a hook (one sentence)

            - Famous quote,

            - How is it a problem?

    ## How can I increase my originality score?


    💡

    You should answer, “yes”, to the following questions:

    - Did you avoid all forms of technology when it comes to writing your text: GenAI and any grammar checkers like Grammarly, Word Editor, etc.?

    - Does each body paragraph (i.e., not the introduction paragraph, transitional paragraph, and conclusion paragraph) have five-to-eight sentences, between 140-180 words (approximately)?

    - Does each body paragraph begin with a topic sentence? See Topic sentence-heading-thesis alignment and Sentences that pack a punch: The difference between a topic sentence and thesis statement.

    - Does each body paragraph include at least one evidence sentence that follows immediately after the topic sentence? - See The M.E.A.L. Plan: A Comprehensive Approach to Paragraph Development.

    - Does each evidence sentence contain a parenthetical citation (see **Parenthetical Vs. Narrative Citations**)?

    - Is each evidence sentence specific enough? In other words, does each evidence sentence offer a specific detail, example, fact, or statistic?

    - Does each body paragraph contain analysis sentences that link the evidence (i.e., detail, example, fact, or statistic) to the topic sentence? Does the analysis sentence offer a comment, explanation, etc?

    - Are the topic sentences, analysis sentences, and linking sentences original ideas?

    - Does each body paragraph conclude with either a linking sentence or a summarizing sentence?

    - Tip 1: Sometimes, students will mistake an analysis sentence for an evidence sentence. This can negatively affect one’s originality score as well.

    - Tip 2: Do not worry about grammatical mistakes. Rely on human feedback from your tutor and others to help correct errors in grammar.
