# L-Chapter 10: Connecting the Stops in a Journey


⚡️ Travel, Tourism, Itineraries, Instructions, and Discourse Connectors

> Travel messages require listeners to connect information across time and place. An itinerary may include departures, arrivals, transfers, and exceptions; an instruction may describe where to go and what to do; a tourism report may compare options or add recommendations. This chapter focuses on connectors of addition, contrast, and sequence because these small words reveal the relationship between details. You will listen for the main route or purpose first, then organize specific times, locations, and actions. When authorized after an independent attempt, GenAI may create parallel A2 practice, but you must verify its language and ensure it does not reproduce the assigned material.


## Essential understanding(s)

- How do connectors help a listener understand relationships in a travel message?

- How can a listener organize time and location details without losing the overall route?

## Guiding Questions

- Which connectors signal addition, contrast, and sequence?

- How can an itinerary be represented in a timeline or route map?

- What signals indicate a change, exception, or transfer?

- How can generated parallel practice be checked for A2 appropriateness?

## Learning outcomes

- Identify the main purpose and route in an itinerary, instruction, or tourism message.

- Locate times, places, actions, and transfer points.

- Recognize basic connectors of addition, contrast, and sequence.

- Use a timeline or route map to verify relationships among details.

## Key concepts and vocabulary

**itinerary** • **departure** • **arrival** • **destination** • **transfer** • **route** • **platform** • **addition** • **contrast** • **sequence** • **however** • **also** • **then** • **finally**

## Main chapter content

### Hear the route before recording every stop

Travel listening becomes difficult when the listener starts writing the first time or place and then misses the purpose of the message. On the first listening, identify the overall route or task. Is the speaker explaining how to reach a location, presenting a day tour, announcing a transportation change, or comparing two travel options? A gist statement might be: “The guide explains the morning route from the hotel to two city attractions.”

On the second listening, use a format that matches movement. A timeline represents order; a route map represents location; a table can combine Stop, Time, Action, and Note. Use arrows for movement and circles for transfer points. Record units and labels with numbers: “8:40 bus,” “Platform 3,” or “Gate 12.” A number without a category is easy to misinterpret.

### Connectors reveal relationships

Addition connectors introduce another related idea: and, also, too, in addition, or another. They tell the listener that information is being expanded. Contrast connectors— but, however, although, instead, and rather than—signal a difference, exception, or change. Sequence connectors—first, next, after that, before, then, and finally—organize order.

These words may be unstressed and brief, but they can change the answer. “The museum is open on Saturday, but the guided tour is not available” contains a positive fact followed by a limitation. “Take the train to Central Station, then transfer to Bus 4” contains two stages; stopping after the first produces an incomplete route.

Create a connector margin in your notes. When you hear a connector, write a symbol: a plus sign for addition, a double arrow for contrast, and a right arrow for sequence. The symbol helps you represent the relationship even if you cannot write the full phrase.

### Changes and exceptions

Travel messages frequently correct or update information. Listen for changed, delayed, moved, instead of, no longer, and now. The speaker may mention the original plan before the current plan. Mark old information with a line through it and write the new information beside it. Do not erase completely; seeing the revision helps explain why two details appeared.

Time relationships also require care. “Arrive by nine” means no later than nine. “Leave after nine” means later. “The bus departs at nine, but boarding begins at eight forty-five” gives two related times with different actions. Label each action.

### Practice materials and verification

After an independent attempt, the teacher may authorize GenAI to generate a new itinerary, question set, or A2 audio script for parallel practice. Check the material critically. Are the sentences short enough? Is the vocabulary appropriate? Are times and route relationships logically consistent? Does the practice target addition, contrast, or sequence? Does it avoid copying the assessed audio?

If text-to-speech audio is used for separate practice, consider whether the pronunciation and speed are understandable and whether the voice handles times and place names accurately. Generated materials are not automatically correct. Record any changes you make and compare explanations with course materials or teacher guidance.

### From organized notes to action

After listening, explain the route or instruction in your own words. Use the connectors you identified: “First, passengers take the train to Central Station. Then they transfer to Bus 4. However, on Sunday the bus stops at a different entrance.” This response demonstrates more than isolated detail recall; it shows the relationships that make the message usable.

CAADI Worksheet 10 completes the required CAADI sequence. Apply gist listening, route-based notes, connector recognition, and focused verification. Your notes should allow another person to follow the itinerary or instruction accurately.
