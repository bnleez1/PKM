⚡️ Introductions, Personal Background, and the Before–During–After Listening Cycle

> Listening is not simply waiting for words to enter your ears. Effective listeners prepare for a message, focus their attention while it is happening, and check their understanding afterward. This first chapter introduces that complete listening cycle through short A2-level conversations about names, places, languages, interests, and personal backgrounds. The goal is not to understand every word. Instead, you will learn to build a reasonable picture of who is speaking, what the situation is, and which details matter. You will also begin using simple notes to capture names, numbers, and locations. These habits establish a foundation for the rest of the course because the same cycle can be applied to announcements, interviews, stories, instructions, and real conversations.


## Essential understanding(s)


- How does preparation change what a listener is able to understand?

- What evidence from an audio message is enough to support a basic interpretation?

## Guiding Questions


- What can a title, image, or situation suggest before listening begins?

- How can a listener identify the topic and general idea without understanding every word?

- Which notes are most useful for names, numbers, countries, languages, and places?

- How can predictions be confirmed or revised after listening?

## Learning outcomes

- Apply a before–during–after cycle to a short conversation or introduction.

- Identify the topic and general idea of an A2-level personal introduction.

- Record basic details such as names, numbers, countries, languages, and locations.

- Compare initial predictions with evidence from the audio.

- Complete an initial listening diagnostic and CAADI Worksheet 1 independently.

## Main chapter content

### Listening as an active process

Listening is active because the listener makes decisions before, during, and after an audio message. Before listening, you use what you already know about the situation. During listening, you select information instead of trying to hold every word. After listening, you organize what you heard and decide whether your interpretation is supported. A beginning listener may believe that success means translating every sentence. At A2 level, a more realistic goal is to understand the topic, the main point, and several useful details. For example, in a short introduction, you may need to identify a speaker’s name, hometown, field of study, and interests. Missing one adjective does not prevent successful comprehension if the important information is clear.

### Before listening: build a useful expectation

Preparation gives the brain a direction. A title such as “New Students Meet” suggests introductions, names, programs, and perhaps countries or cities. A photograph of two students near a campus building provides another clue about the setting. Use these clues to activate relevant words, but avoid deciding exactly what the audio must say. A prediction is a temporary expectation, not an answer. Write two or three possible ideas, such as “They may ask where each person is from” or “They may mention their majors.” This creates a purpose for listening and makes familiar language easier to recognize.

### During listening: listen for layers

The first listening should answer broad questions: Who is speaking? Where are they? What are they doing? Do not stop mentally at every unfamiliar word. Listen for repeated words, names, greetings, and the overall tone. On a later listening, focus on selected details. A simple note frame can include Name, Place, Program, Language, and Interest. Write only key words. If the speaker says, “I’m Daniela, and I’m from Zacatecas, but I live near the university now,” your notes might be “Daniela—Zacatecas—near university.” Short notes reduce memory pressure and keep attention on the audio.

Numbers and proper names often require extra attention. A name may be spelled, a phone number may be grouped, or a room number may sound similar to another number. Use context to decide what kind of information is expected. If the conversation concerns a classroom, “two fourteen” may refer to Room 214 rather than a time. Mark uncertain information with a question mark instead of guessing confidently. The mark reminds you what to check during another listening.

### After listening: verify, organize, and explain

After the audio, return to your predictions. Some may be correct, some partly correct, and others unsupported. Revising a prediction is evidence of learning, not failure. Compare notes with a partner and discuss differences by referring to the audio. A useful explanation follows the pattern: “I think ___ because I heard ___.” This moves the discussion beyond choosing an answer and toward justifying comprehension.

Organize the information in a small profile, chart, or concept map. Then write one or two sentences that express the general idea. For example: “Two first-year students introduce themselves and talk about where they are from and what they study.” This sentence combines several details without repeating the entire conversation.

### Responsible use of tools this week

The initial diagnostic and the listening activity itself are completed without GenAI, translation, subtitles, or automatic transcription. When the teacher authorizes preparation support, GenAI may help activate prior knowledge or create a short vocabulary list before a separate practice task. It may not listen for you or provide the answers. Any authorized use should record the tool, date, prompt, and how the preparation affected your listening. The interpretation must remain your own.

This week’s work establishes a repeatable routine: prepare with evidence-based predictions, listen first for meaning, listen again for selected details, and verify the final interpretation. CAADI Worksheet 1 gives you another opportunity to apply this cycle independently.
