# L-Chapter 8: Building a Complete Listening Record


⚡️ Food and Eating Habits, Strategy Consolidation, Project 2, and the Midterm Exam

> Food and eating habits provide a rich context for combining the strategies of Unit II. Speakers may describe routines, preferences, quantities, reasons, cultural practices, and changes in behavior. To understand the message, listeners must identify the main point, select relevant details, and organize notes so that habits are not confused with opinions or examples. This chapter consolidates global and selective listening, keyword notes, tables, repeated listening, and post-attempt reflection. The week includes Listening Comprehension Project 2 and the midterm listening exam. The exam is completed independently without GenAI, translation, subtitles, or automatic transcription. Your preparation should therefore emphasize repeatable habits that can be applied under assessment conditions.


## Essential understanding(s)


- How can several listening strategies work together without increasing cognitive overload?

- What evidence shows that a listener is ready to apply strategies independently in an assessment?

## Guiding Questions

- How can notes separate foods, frequency, quantity, reason, and opinion?

- Which strategy should be used first, and which should be added only when needed?

- How can repeated listening confirm rather than replace the first interpretation?

- What should Project 2 and the midterm exam demonstrate?

## Learning outcomes

- Integrate global and selective listening in messages about food and eating habits.

- Organize quantities, frequency, preferences, and reasons in concise notes.

- Verify answers through focused repeated listening and evidence.

- Reflect on strategy decisions in Listening Comprehension Project 2.

- Complete CAADI Worksheet 8 and the midterm listening exam independently.

## Main chapter content

A message with several information types
Food-related listening may appear simple because many food words are concrete. The difficulty is often relational: how frequently a person eats something, why a habit changed, which choice is preferred, or what quantity is recommended. Before listening, prepare categories that match the task. A table might include Meal or Food, Frequency, Quantity, Reason, and Opinion. Not every audio will require every category, so remove unnecessary columns rather than filling the page with unused space.
On the first listening, determine the main focus. Is the speaker describing a typical day, comparing past and present habits, explaining a cultural practice, or giving advice? A general statement such as “The speaker explains how preparing food at home changed weekday eating habits” provides a frame. On the second listening, collect details that support this frame.
Handling quantities and frequency
Quantities may be exact or approximate: two cups, a few pieces, about half, not much, or enough for three people. Record the unit with the number. “2” alone is not meaningful. Frequency may be expressed as every day, twice a week, once in a while, usually, or hardly ever. Keep quantity and frequency separate. A speaker who eats two pieces once a week has a different habit from someone who eats one piece every day.
Listen for comparison markers such as more than, less than, instead of, as much as, and healthier than. These phrases often explain change. “I drink water instead of soda at lunch” communicates both an old or alternative habit and a current choice. A past–present chart can prevent confusion.
Combining strategies in the correct order
Strategy integration does not mean using every technique at once. Begin with the task and select the minimum useful tools. A practical sequence is:

1. Predict topic categories.

2. Listen for gist without detailed writing.

3. Choose a note format.

4. Listen selectively for required information.

5. Mark uncertainty.

6. Replay only to resolve specific questions.

7. Verify and summarize.
This order protects attention. If you begin with a complex organizer and try to fill every cell immediately, you may miss the main point. If you never move beyond gist, you may lack the details required for action or assessment.
Project 2: make strategy decisions visible
Listening Comprehension Project 2 should show a complete listening record. Include your initial purpose, concise notes, revisions, evidence for final answers, and a decision trace. Explain which strategy addressed which difficulty. For example, “I used separate columns for quantity and frequency because I confused them in the first attempt.” A project is stronger when it explains a decision than when it simply lists strategies.
After the independent first attempt, authorized GenAI may help you reflect on difficulties or create a new parallel practice task. It may not reconstruct the project audio, reveal answers, or write your reflection. Document suggestions and state why they were accepted or rejected.
Midterm assessment conditions
The midterm exam measures your independent listening. Complete it without GenAI, translation, subtitles, transcription, or answer-generating tools. Apply the habits you have practiced: read the task, predict categories, listen first for the main message, note selectively, and use later listenings to verify. If a detail is uncertain, avoid allowing one uncertainty to interrupt the rest of the audio.
CAADI Worksheet 8, Project 2, and the midterm together mark the end of Unit II. The goal is not perfect word recognition. It is controlled, purposeful listening supported by organized evidence.
