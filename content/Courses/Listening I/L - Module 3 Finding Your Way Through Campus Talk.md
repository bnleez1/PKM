# L - Module 3: Finding Your Way Through Campus Talk


⚡️ University Life, Short Conversations, and Evidence-Based Listening

> Campus listening involves more than recognizing university vocabulary. Students must follow short exchanges about classrooms, schedules, services, assignments, and everyday needs while also deciding which details are relevant. This chapter focuses on global and selective listening in A2-level campus conversations and presentations. You will practice identifying the purpose of an interaction, locating practical information, comparing notes, and defending an answer with evidence from the audio. The emphasis is on listening as participation: a listener needs enough understanding to know where to go, what to do, or how to respond. Clear evidence and organized notes are more valuable than a long list of disconnected words.


## Essential understanding(s)


- What does a listener need to understand in order to act appropriately in a campus situation?

- How can evidence from an audio message support or correct an interpretation?

## Guiding Questions


- How can the setting and relationship between speakers clarify the purpose of a conversation?

- Which details are essential in campus messages and which are optional?

- How can partners compare notes without simply copying one another?

- What language can be used to justify an answer with evidence from the audio?

## Learning outcomes

- Identify the setting, speakers, and purpose of a short campus conversation or presentation.

- Locate practical information such as rooms, times, requirements, and services.

- Use global and selective listening across repeated listenings.

- Compare notes and justify an interpretation with specific audio evidence.

- Complete CAADI Worksheet 3 without using AI during comprehension.

## Main chapter content

### Listening for purpose in a real setting

A campus conversation usually has a practical purpose. A student may ask where a classroom is, clarify an assignment, make an appointment, or learn about a service. Before focusing on details, identify what the speakers are trying to accomplish. The same word can have different importance depending on the purpose. In a conversation about a tutoring center, the location and opening hours may be essential; the color of the building is probably secondary. Purpose helps the listener decide what to record.

Use contextual clues to identify the setting and relationship. Formal greetings and a request for assistance may suggest a student speaking with office staff. Casual language and shared references may indicate classmates. A short presentation with one speaker and structured points may be an orientation message. These clues guide expectations about vocabulary and response. However, they should be confirmed by the audio rather than treated as certain before listening.

### Global listening before detail hunting

On the first listening, answer three questions: Where are the speakers? What is the main situation? What result do they need? A useful gist statement might be: “A student asks for information about joining a campus club.” This statement is broad enough to be formed without every detail. It also prepares a selective purpose for the next listening: club name, meeting day, location, and registration requirement.

During the second listening, use a note frame designed for the situation. A campus-information chart might include Purpose, Place, Time, Person to Contact, and Required Action. Write key words and symbols. A question mark can mark uncertainty; an arrow can show movement from one place to another; an exclamation point can mark a deadline or important instruction. Notes should support later action, not reproduce the message.

### Comparing notes as a listening strategy

Pair comparison is most useful after each student has listened and written independently. First, explain what you heard rather than showing your paper immediately. This requires retrieval from memory and reveals your overall interpretation. Then compare specific entries. When notes differ, ask what evidence supports each version. One student might say, “I wrote Thursday because the speaker corrected Monday.” Another may realize that the first day mentioned was an old schedule, not the current one.

Disagreement should lead to a focused listening question. Instead of replaying the entire audio without a plan, listen for the sentence around the disputed detail. Pay attention to correction language such as actually, sorry, I mean, not ___ but ___, or it changed to ___. Real conversations often include revisions, and the most recent information is usually the relevant answer.

### Justifying comprehension

An answer becomes stronger when it is connected to evidence. Use simple frames:

- “The purpose is ___ because the student asks ___.”

- “The meeting is in ___; I heard the speaker say ___.”

- “I changed my answer from ___ to ___ because the speaker corrected the information.”

Evidence does not always mean quoting a full sentence. At A2 level, it may be a key phrase, repeated word, number, or sequence of events. The important point is to show that the interpretation comes from the audio rather than from guessing.

### Independent listening and responsible preparation

GenAI is not used during the assigned comprehension activity. It may not provide subtitles, transcription, translation, or answers. When pre-listening support is authorized, it can help create a campus vocabulary preview or a blank organizer for a parallel task. The learner still makes predictions, listens, and interprets independently.

CAADI Worksheet 3 provides additional campus-based listening. Apply global listening first, selective listening second, and evidence-based verification at the end. The practical test of understanding is whether your notes would help you take the correct action in the situation.
