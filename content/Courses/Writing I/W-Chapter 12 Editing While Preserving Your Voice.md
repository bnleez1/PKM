# W-Chapter 12: Editing While Preserving Your Voice


⚡️ Completing the Unit III paragraph through peer review and critical revision

> The final stage of Unit III is not simply making a paragraph look clean. It is deciding which revisions genuinely improve communication while preserving your ideas, intentions, and language level. In this chapter, you will participate in a peer-review workshop, apply explainable changes, and prepare the final version of an expository or descriptive paragraph. You will also submit a revision trail and a unit reflection. Peer readers can reveal where meaning is unclear, but they should not rewrite the text. Technology can suggest alternatives, but it cannot decide which option represents you. Your final paragraph should show greater precision, coherence, cohesion, and punctuation control than the original draft, while still sounding like writing that you understand and can discuss.


## Essential understanding(s)

- What does it mean to improve a text without losing the writer’s voice?

- How can peer review support independent decision-making?

## Guiding Questions

- Which peer comments describe a genuine reader problem?

- How can writers distinguish revision from unauthorized rewriting?

- What evidence should accompany the final Unit III paragraph?

- How can a unit reflection identify transferable learning?

## Learning outcomes

- Give and receive peer feedback focused on clarity, coherence, cohesion, and precision.

- Make revisions that are understandable and explainable.

- Edit the final paragraph for punctuation, spelling, and sentence completeness.

- Submit a final paragraph with draft and revision evidence.

- Reflect on Unit III learning and identify a goal for argumentative writing.

## Key concepts and vocabulary

| **Term**               | **Working definition**                                                   |
| :--------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **peer review**        | a structured process in which classmates respond to each other’s writing |
| **reader problem**     | a place where a reader becomes confused, unconvinced, or disconnected    |
| **rewrite**            | produce a substantially new version in another person’s words            |
| **preserve voice**     | maintain the writer’s own ideas, style, and understandable language      |
| **explainable change** | a revision the writer can describe and justify                           |
| **final version**      | the prepared text submitted after revision and editing                   |
| **editing pass**       | one focused review for a particular type of error                        |
| **process evidence**   | materials that document planning, drafting, feedback, and revision       |
| **unit reflection**    | an evidence-based review of learning during one course unit              |
| **transferable skill** | a skill that can be applied to a different writing task                  |

## Main chapter content

### Using peer review to identify reader problems

A peer reviewer’s most important role is to report where the text becomes difficult to follow. The reviewer can identify the main idea, summarize each supporting point, and ask questions where details are missing. Comments such as “I do not understand what this pronoun refers to” or “This example seems separate from the topic sentence” reveal reader problems. The writer decides how to respond. The peer should not replace sentences with a more advanced version.

### Protecting voice and authorship

A revised paragraph may contain new words or reordered sentences, but the writer must understand every change. If a peer or tool offers a complete sentence, do not copy it automatically. Identify the underlying issue and solve it with language you can use independently. Ask yourself: Does this sentence express my idea? Is it appropriate for my level? Can I explain its grammar and vocabulary? Would I say something similar in class? If not, revise the idea in your own words.

### Completing focused editing passes

After meaning and organization are stable, edit in separate passes. In the first pass, check sentence completeness and verb forms. In the second, check capitalization and end punctuation. In the third, check connectors and commas. In the fourth, verify spelling and vocabulary. Reading for one feature at a time is more effective than trying to notice everything at once. Use a printed copy or change the display size to see the paragraph differently.

### Assembling the Unit III product

Submit the final paragraph together with the original draft, peer comments, any authorized AI-use record, and the revision trail. Highlight two or three important changes in the final version and match them to explanations in the trail. The evidence should show a sequence: independent drafting, feedback, critical verification, writer decisions, and final editing. The final text must remain understandable to you and consistent with your A2 development.

### Conducting a respectful peer-review conference

A brief conversation can make written peer comments more useful. The writer first states the paragraph’s purpose and one question for the reader. The reviewer reads silently, summarizes the main idea, and identifies one strength before describing one or two reader problems. The writer listens without defending every sentence, asks clarifying questions, and takes notes. Then the writer decides what to change after the conversation. This structure keeps the reviewer from taking control of the text and keeps the writer from ignoring feedback automatically. Use respectful language such as “I understood this part as…” and “I became confused when….” Avoid statements such as “This is wrong” unless you can refer to a specific course criterion. The conference should end with the writer naming the next revision action. That action remains the writer’s choice.

### Reflecting for transfer

In the unit reflection, identify one skill you can transfer to argumentative writing. For example, precise vocabulary will help state an opinion clearly; coherence will help order reasons; specific details will become examples. Also name one risk to manage, such as using connectors mechanically or accepting language that is too advanced. Finish with a practical goal for Unit IV: “I will make sure every example directly supports a reason.” Reflection connects the completed unit to the next writing challenge.
