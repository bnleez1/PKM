# W-Chapter 14: Solving Problems in Your Community


⚡️ Developing contextual reasons, examples, and practical solutions

> Argumentative writing becomes meaningful when it addresses problems that people recognize in daily life. A problem may exist at home, on campus, in transportation, in a neighborhood, or in a wider community. This week, you will move from a general concern to a specific, manageable issue. You will generate reasons, examples, and possible solutions, then select the ideas that fit your context and can be explained in one paragraph. A solution should respond directly to the problem rather than remain a vague wish. If GenAI is authorized for idea exploration, you will evaluate each contribution for specificity, cultural appropriateness, and usefulness. Your first draft will be written individually so that the final argument reflects your own understanding of the problem and your own choices.


## Essential understanding(s)


- How can a writer propose a realistic solution to a familiar problem?

- Why must arguments be specific to the writer’s community and context?

## Guiding Questions

- How can a broad social issue be narrowed to one local problem?

- What reasons and examples show why the problem matters?

- What makes a proposed solution practical and connected to the cause?

- How can AI suggestions be evaluated for cultural and contextual fit?

## Learning outcomes

- Narrow a broad issue to a specific community or daily-life problem.

- Explain why the problem matters using reasons and examples.

- Propose a practical solution connected to the problem.

- Create a graphic organizer and independent first draft.

- Evaluate external ideas for specificity, cultural appropriateness, and usefulness.

## Key concepts and vocabulary

| **Term**                     | **Working definition**                                                  |
| :--------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **problem**                  | a difficult situation that needs attention or change                    |
| **context**                  | the local conditions and circumstances surrounding an issue             |
| **community**                | a group of people connected by place, institution, or shared experience |
| **cause**                    | a factor that produces or contributes to a problem                      |
| **effect**                   | a result or consequence of a problem                                    |
| **solution**                 | an action or plan intended to improve a problem                         |
| **practical**                | possible and useful in real conditions                                  |
| **specificity**              | the quality of including exact and relevant detail                      |
| **cultural appropriateness** | suitability for the values and practices of a context                   |
| **usefulness**               | the degree to which an idea helps address the task or problem           |

## Main chapter content

### Narrowing the problem

Large issues such as pollution, transportation, safety, or inequality cannot be solved in one A2 paragraph. Narrow the issue to a situation you can describe: limited recycling containers in one campus building, crowded buses at a particular time, or students leaving trash in a shared area. A specific problem makes it possible to explain causes, effects, and a realistic response. Name the people and place affected when appropriate.

### Explaining why the problem matters

An argumentative paragraph needs more than a complaint. Give a reason that explains the importance of the issue and an example that makes it concrete. “The buses are crowded” is a statement. “Crowded buses make some students arrive late because they cannot enter the first bus” explains an effect. A brief example from observation can make the point clearer, but avoid inventing statistics or facts you cannot verify.

### Proposing a connected solution

A solution should address the cause or reduce the effect. If the problem is unclear task distribution in group work, a solution may be to assign roles at the beginning. If the problem is limited recycling information, a solution may be signs with simple examples near the containers. “People should be better” is too vague. Explain who should act, what the action is, and how it can help. A modest practical solution is stronger than an unrealistic promise.

### Evaluating outside ideas

GenAI may generate common solutions that do not fit your community. A recommendation to install expensive technology may be unrealistic. An idea from another country may assume different transportation, laws, or cultural practices. Ask: Does this suggestion match the actual problem? Who would carry it out? Is it respectful and culturally appropriate? Can I explain it with A2 language? Verify any factual claim before using it. Record the tool and your decisions when use is authorized.

### Writing solutions with responsible scope

A short paragraph should not claim to solve an entire social problem. Define a solution at the level where action is possible. A student group can create clearer task roles, a department can place signs, or commuters can share schedule information. State the actor, action, and expected result. Then acknowledge a realistic limitation when appropriate: a sign may improve information, but it will not solve every recycling problem. This does not weaken the argument; it shows accurate thinking. Avoid assigning blame to a group without evidence or using language that stereotypes people. Describe observable actions and conditions instead. A context-sensitive solution respects the people involved and recognizes available resources. This is part of evaluating whether outside ideas, including AI suggestions, are culturally appropriate and practical. Before drafting, ask one classmate whether the proposed action seems realistic in the local setting today.

### Planning and drafting independently

Create an organizer with the problem, affected group, cause or effect, proposed solution, reason the solution can help, and one example. Then write the first draft individually. State the problem and your position clearly. Develop the solution with a reason and example. Use connectors such as because, for example, but, and so when they accurately express the relationship. Keep the organizer and draft as evidence for the revision workshop.
