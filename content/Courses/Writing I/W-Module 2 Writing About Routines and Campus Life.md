# W-Module 2: Writing About Routines and Campus Life


⚡️ Using time expressions, connectors, and basic punctuation

> Daily routines may seem ordinary, but they are excellent material for learning to organize information. A routine has a natural pattern: activities happen at particular times, in particular places, and often in a repeated order. Campus life also gives you a meaningful context for practicing English because you can describe classes, study spaces, classmates, transportation, and responsibilities. In this chapter, you will build an A2 vocabulary bank, practice basic punctuation, and use simple connectors to show how ideas relate. You will not begin by writing a full paragraph immediately. Instead, you will collect useful language and organize ideas so that later writing becomes clearer and more efficient. When GenAI is authorized, it may support brainstorming or vocabulary exploration, but you will select, adapt, and organize all ideas yourself.


## Essential understanding(s)

- How do writers make an everyday routine clear and interesting to a reader?

- How does punctuation help a reader understand meaning?

## Guiding Questions


- Which time expressions and frequency words help describe repeated actions?

- When should a writer use a capital letter, period, comma, or question mark?

- How can basic connectors link ideas without creating long or confusing sentences?

- How can a vocabulary bank and graphic organizer support original writing?

## Learning outcomes

- Generate and organize A2-level vocabulary related to routines and university life.

- Write clear simple-present sentences about repeated actions.

- Use capital letters, periods, commas in basic lists, and question marks appropriately.

- Use basic connectors such as and, but, because, first, then, and finally.

- Create an original graphic organizer for a future paragraph.

## Key concepts and vocabulary

| **Term**              | **Working definition**                                            |
| :-------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **routine**           | an activity or group of activities that happens regularly         |
| **frequency**         | how often something happens                                       |
| **time expression**   | a phrase that shows when or how often an action occurs            |
| **connector**         | a word or phrase that links ideas                                 |
| **capitalization**    | the correct use of capital letters                                |
| **period**            | the punctuation mark used at the end of a statement               |
| **comma**             | a punctuation mark used in basic lists and some sentence patterns |
| **sequence**          | the order in which events or actions happen                       |
| **vocabulary bank**   | an organized collection of useful words and phrases               |
| **graphic organizer** | a visual plan that shows relationships among ideas                |

## Main chapter content

### Finding a focus in everyday life

A routine paragraph should not become a timetable with no purpose. Decide what the routine shows. You might explain how you prepare for class, how you manage a busy day, or what campus life is like for you. This focus helps you choose relevant actions. “I wake up at 6:00” may be useful if it begins your preparation for university, but an unrelated detail about a television program may distract the reader. Campus details should also support the focus: where you study, who you work with, and what challenges you manage.

### Using the simple present and time expressions

The simple present is useful for repeated actions: “I take the bus to campus,” “My first class begins at eight,” and “I usually study in the library.” Frequency words such as always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely, and never tell how often an action happens. Time expressions such as every morning, on Mondays, after class, and in the evening tell when it happens. Place frequency words carefully: “I usually eat breakfast before class,” but “I am usually tired after a long day.” At this stage, choose patterns you understand and can use accurately.

### Punctuation as a guide for the reader

Punctuation signals where an idea begins, pauses, or ends. Begin sentences and proper names with capital letters: Monday, English, Universidad Autónoma de Aguascalientes. End statements with periods and direct questions with question marks. Use commas in a simple list: “I carry a notebook, a dictionary, and a water bottle.” Do not place a comma between every short phrase. Read the sentence aloud and ask whether the punctuation helps the reader follow the idea. Punctuation should clarify meaning, not decorate the page.

### Connecting ideas clearly

Basic connectors show relationships. And adds similar information: “I attend class, and I take notes.” But introduces contrast: “The campus is busy, but I can find quiet places to study.” Because gives a reason: “I arrive early because the bus schedule is unpredictable.” Sequence connectors such as first, then, after that, and finally organize actions. Do not use too many connectors in one sentence. Two clear sentences are often better than one very long sentence with several ands.

### From notes to usable language

A vocabulary bank becomes useful when it includes more than isolated words. For each important verb, record one short phrase or sentence pattern: attend class, review my notes, arrive on campus, work with classmates. For time expressions, record their usual position: “I study in the evening” or “After class, I go to the library.” Grouping language in small patterns helps you draft without translating every word separately. Before placing a word in your organizer, say or write a simple sentence with it. If you cannot use it, check a model or dictionary again. You can also mark vocabulary as familiar, new, or uncertain. During planning, choose mostly familiar language and only a few verified new items. This balance allows the final paragraph to show growth without becoming difficult to control. Your organizer should contain ideas, not complete AI-generated sentences, so the wording of the draft remains your responsibility.

### Preparing a vocabulary bank and organizer

Create categories such as times, places, actions, people, and feelings. Add words from class materials, models, and trusted dictionaries. If GenAI brainstorming is authorized, ask for A2 words connected to one category, then verify meanings and select only words you understand. Record the tool, date, prompt, and decisions. Next, use a timeline, flowchart, or cluster to organize your own routine. Your organizer should show the main focus and the order of supporting ideas. This week’s product is preparation for writing: a purposeful vocabulary bank and an original organizer that you can explain.
