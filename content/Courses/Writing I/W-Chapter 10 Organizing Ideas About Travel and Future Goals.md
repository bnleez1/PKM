# W-Chapter 10: Organizing Ideas About Travel and Future Goals


⚡️ Using addition, contrast, and sequence in expository and descriptive writing

> Travel and future goals invite writers to explain places, plans, preferences, and possibilities. These topics also require careful organization. A paragraph about a destination may describe what makes the place attractive, while a paragraph about a future goal may explain the steps needed to achieve it. This chapter introduces three important relationships among ideas: addition, contrast, and sequence. Connectors can make these relationships visible, but they must be chosen for meaning rather than inserted mechanically. You will organize ideas with a map or table and produce an expository or descriptive paragraph. Models, connector guides, dictionaries, and CAADI materials will support your work. Your paragraph should remain clear, focused, and appropriate for A2, even when the topic concerns the future.


## Essential understanding(s)

- How do connectors reveal the relationships among ideas?

- How can writers organize a place description or future plan for an A2 reader?

## Guiding Questions

- When should a writer add information, show contrast, or present sequence?

- Which organizer best supports a travel description or a future-goal explanation?

- How can examples make an expository paragraph more specific?

- How can a writer discuss the future with language that remains clear and manageable?

## Learning outcomes

- Distinguish addition, contrast, and sequence relationships.

- Use basic connectors accurately within and between sentences.

- Organize travel or future-goal ideas in a map or table.

- Write a focused expository or descriptive paragraph.

- Use models and reference resources to verify language choices.

## Key concepts and vocabulary

| **Term**                 | **Working definition**                                       |
| :----------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **addition**             | a relationship in which one idea adds information to another |
| **contrast**             | a relationship that shows difference or opposition           |
| **sequence**             | a relationship that shows order in time or steps             |
| **expository paragraph** | a paragraph that explains or informs                         |
| **destination**          | a place to which a person travels                            |
| **goal**                 | a result a person hopes and plans to achieve                 |
| **step**                 | one action in a process or plan                              |
| **supporting example**   | a specific case that explains a general point                |
| **connector choice**     | the decision to select a connector that matches meaning      |
| **future plan**          | an intended action or set of actions for a later time        |

## Main chapter content

### Understanding three relationships

Addition connects similar information. Useful connectors include and, also, in addition, and another. Contrast shows difference or an unexpected relationship. Basic choices include but, however, and while. Sequence shows order through first, next, after that, and finally. The same topic may use all three. A travel paragraph may add attractions, contrast two seasons, and sequence the steps of a visit. The connector must accurately represent the relationship.

### Avoiding mechanical connector use

Do not add a connector to every sentence. Readers can sometimes understand the connection through repeated key words and logical order. Also check sentence patterns. “However” often begins a new sentence and is followed by a comma: “The city is expensive. However, public parks are free.” At A2 level, “but” may be a clearer choice: “The city is expensive, but public parks are free.” Choose forms you have practiced and can punctuate correctly.

### Planning a travel description

A destination paragraph needs a controlling idea. You may explain why a place is good for a particular traveler or describe what makes it memorable. Organize by categories such as location, activities, food, transportation, and atmosphere, but select only categories that support the central focus. Specific examples improve the paragraph: instead of “There are many activities,” name one or two and explain why they matter.

### Planning a future-goal explanation

A future goal paragraph can explain the goal, reasons, steps, and expected result. For example, a student may want to improve speaking confidence. Supporting sentences can present a weekly practice plan, participation in conversation activities, and a method for tracking progress. Sequence connectors are useful because goals require actions over time. Contrast may show a challenge: “I feel nervous when I speak, but I can practice in small groups.”

### Checking connector meaning with sentence pairs

Practice connectors by beginning with two simple sentences. “I want to visit the city. It has several museums.” Addition can combine the ideas: “I want to visit the city because it has several museums” actually expresses reason, while “The city has several museums, and it has historic parks” expresses addition. “The trip is expensive. I am saving money” can become a contrast or response depending on meaning: “The trip is expensive, but I am saving money for it.” Work with sentence pairs before inserting connectors into a paragraph. Ask what relationship already exists, then select the connector. This prevents the common habit of choosing a connector because it sounds academic. In your final review, cover the connector and read the two ideas. Name the relationship in your own words, uncover the connector, and confirm that it matches. Repeat the check with at least two connectors from your own draft before submitting it carefully.

### Weekly writing task

Choose either a travel destination or a future goal. Create a map or table that identifies the main focus, supporting categories or steps, specific examples, and possible connectors. Then write an expository or descriptive paragraph. Underline connectors and label each as addition, contrast, or sequence. Replace any connector that does not match the intended relationship. Verify unfamiliar vocabulary with a dictionary or course resource, and keep the organizer and draft as process evidence.
