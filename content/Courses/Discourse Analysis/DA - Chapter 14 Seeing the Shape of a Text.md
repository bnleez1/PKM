# DA - Chapter 14: Seeing the Shape of a Text


⚡️ Macro-discourse structures and the interaction of top-down and bottom-up reading

> Readers comprehend more than individual words and sentences. They recognize larger organizational patterns that help them predict what information is likely to appear and how parts relate to a whole. A problem–solution text raises a situation requiring response; cause–effect organizes conditions and consequences; comparison establishes dimensions of similarity and difference; chronology orders events; general–specific movement develops categories and examples. These macrostructures interact with bottom-up decoding and top-down knowledge. This week you will map authentic texts, identify linguistic signals, and examine how structural awareness supports comprehension without replacing careful attention to language. You will also develop questions at multiple levels so that learners move from noticing words and cohesive ties to constructing an evidence-based representation of the text’s overall organization and purpose.


## Essential understanding(s)


How do macro-discourse structures guide prediction, integration, and comprehension?

How do top-down and bottom-up processes interact during reading?

## Guiding Questions


What functional stages define problem–solution, cause–effect, comparison, chronology, and general–specific organization?

Which lexical, grammatical, and visual signals reveal a text’s structure?

When can background knowledge support comprehension, and when can it produce inaccurate predictions?

How can comprehension questions connect local evidence to global structure?

## Learning outcomes

Identify and map major macro-discourse structures in authentic texts.

Locate linguistic and visual signals that mark stages and relations.

Explain the reciprocal interaction of top-down and bottom-up reading processes.

Evaluate how background knowledge and structural cues support or mislead interpretation.

Design comprehension questions that move from local language to global organization and critical interpretation.

## Key concepts and vocabulary

| **Term**                 | **Working definition**                                                                              |
| :----------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Macrostructure**       | The global semantic and organizational representation of a text.                                    |
| **Problem–solution**     | A structure that presents a situation or problem, response, and often evaluation of the response.   |
| **Cause–effect**         | A structure that organizes conditions, reasons, mechanisms, and consequences.                       |
| **Comparison**           | A structure organized around shared dimensions of similarity and difference.                        |
| **Chronology**           | Organization according to temporal sequence or stages in a process.                                 |
| **General–specific**     | Movement from a broad category or claim to details, examples, or subtypes, or the reverse.          |
| **Top-down processing**  | Interpretation guided by purpose, expectations, background knowledge, and global predictions.       |
| **Bottom-up processing** | Interpretation built through attention to letters, words, syntax, cohesion, and local propositions. |
| **Signaling device**     | A linguistic or visual cue that indicates relation, stage, emphasis, or organization.               |
| **Schema**               | An organized knowledge structure used to interpret and predict information.                         |

## Main chapter content

1. Macrostructure as a model of the whole
A text’s macrostructure is not simply its list of headings. It is the reader’s representation of the major ideas and relationships that make the text a coherent whole. Writers support this representation through ordering, repetition, topic sentences, headings, summaries, connectors, and genre conventions.
Readers compress detail. After reading a report, they may remember the problem, proposed response, and outcome rather than every sentence. Macrostructure helps determine which information is central and how details fit. When readers fail to identify the structure, they may retain isolated facts without understanding the argument.
Analysts map functional stages rather than imposing a label based on one connector. A problem–solution text may include background, problem, causes, response, evaluation, and recommendations. Some stages may be implicit or repeated. The map should reflect the actual text and explain departures from a prototypical pattern.

2. Common organizational patterns
Problem–solution organization frames a condition as requiring action and evaluates possible responses. Cause–effect organization explains why something occurs or what it produces; multiple causes and reciprocal effects are common. Comparison requires a shared basis: texts may organize point by point or present one case and then another. Chronology supports narratives, histories, procedures, and developmental explanations. General–specific organization introduces a category or claim and elaborates details, examples, classifications, or evidence.
Texts frequently embed patterns. A problem–solution article may explain causes, compare solutions, and describe implementation chronologically. The analyst should identify the dominant purpose and nested structures.
Linguistic signals include nouns such as problem and consequence, verbs such as results in and differs, conjunctions, temporal expressions, parallel syntax, reference chains, and lexical fields. Visual signals include headings, numbering, diagrams, and paragraphing. No single marker is definitive; patterns emerge from converging evidence.

3. Top-down and bottom-up interaction
Bottom-up processing begins with the linguistic signal: recognizing words, parsing clauses, resolving reference, and connecting propositions. Top-down processing uses purpose, genre knowledge, topic schemas, and predictions. Skilled comprehension integrates both continuously.
A reader who relies only on bottom-up decoding may understand individual sentences but miss the overall point. A reader who relies too heavily on top-down expectations may overlook contradictory evidence or force the text into a familiar pattern. Effective readers test predictions against language and revise their mental model.
For language learners, difficulty at one level affects the other. Unknown vocabulary can disrupt macrostructure, while a clear organizational frame can help infer a word’s role. Instruction should therefore alternate scales: predict the likely structure, read for signals, verify the stages, and return to difficult local passages with a clearer global model.

4. Mapping as an analytical practice
To map a text, first identify its communicative purpose and provisional macrostructure. Divide it into functional units, not simply equal paragraphs. Label each unit with an action such as introduces problem, explains cause, presents option, evaluates result, or gives example. Then locate the signals supporting each label.
Compare the map with paragraph boundaries. A paragraph may contain more than one stage, and a stage may extend across paragraphs. Note where the organization is explicit and where the reader must infer. Evaluate whether the sequence supports comprehension for the intended audience.
Graphic organizers should represent relationships rather than decorate content. Arrows can show causality, timelines can show sequence, matrices can show comparison, and hierarchical maps can show general–specific relations. The chosen form should match the discourse structure.

5. Designing multilevel comprehension questions
Questions should guide learners from evidence to structure. Local questions can ask what a pronoun refers to, which connector signals contrast, or what a key term means in context. Relational questions can ask how two paragraphs connect or which evidence supports a cause. Global questions can ask for the main problem, the stages of a process, or the basis of comparison. Critical questions can examine assumptions, omitted alternatives, or the adequacy of evidence.
Sequence matters. Asking for a theme before learners can decode key propositions may produce guessing. Asking only detail questions may prevent synthesis. A well-designed set moves flexibly between levels and encourages learners to justify answers with text.
Your weekly task should include a macrostructure map and a question set. Each question should identify the intended processing level and the textual evidence learners need. This prepares the instructional design work in Week 15.

6. From map to strategic reading lesson
Choose one text with a clear but not perfectly prototypical macrostructure. Produce a teacher map and a partially completed learner map. Decide which stages learners should predict before reading and which must be discovered from evidence. Identify two bottom-up features essential to the map, such as a causal verb, a contrastive connector, or a reference chain.
Design a three-cycle reading sequence. In cycle one, learners preview the source and predict purpose and structure. In cycle two, they read for stage boundaries and signals. In cycle three, they test the map by summarizing or answering a critical question. At each cycle, require a return to textual evidence.
Include one deliberate prediction that the text later complicates. Ask learners to revise their schema rather than treating the mismatch as failure. This models skilled reading: top-down expectations guide attention, while bottom-up evidence corrects them. Finish by explaining how the lesson avoids both word-by-word dependence and unsupported guessing.

7. Evaluating structural signals
Not all signals are equally helpful. A heading can accurately preview a section, vaguely label a topic, or mislead the reader. A connector can express a relation clearly, redundantly, or incorrectly. Part of macrostructure analysis is evaluating whether the signals and stages align.
Choose five signals from the text and rate their contribution to comprehension. For each one, identify the relationship it proposes, the units it connects, and whether the subsequent content fulfills the expectation. If a paragraph begins “In contrast” but continues the same idea, the problem is not merely vocabulary; the signal creates a false structural prediction.
Learners can revise by changing the signal, reorganizing the propositions, or making an implicit stage explicit. Ask them to justify which solution preserves the intended meaning. This practice links bottom-up attention to global organization and demonstrates why discourse markers should not be taught as interchangeable decorations.

8. Analytical checkpoint
Before submitting, ask whether the proposed macrostructure is supported by several signals rather than one keyword. Confirm that each stage label describes a function, that embedded patterns are acknowledged, and that the comprehension questions require learners to connect local evidence with the global model.
