⚡️ From course orientation to the historical foundations of language-in-context inquiry

> Discourse analysis begins with a deceptively simple observation: people do not communicate through isolated sentences. They use language to act, relate, organize knowledge, position themselves, and respond to particular social situations. During this opening week, you will become familiar with the course’s expectations while beginning to see language as situated action rather than merely a collection of grammatical forms. The week moves from a diagnostic comparison of spoken and written texts to a historical overview of the field. It also introduces the course’s transparent approach to generative artificial intelligence. AI may help locate starting points or stimulate questions, but the responsibility for interpretation, verification, and analytical judgment remains with you. By the end of the week, you should be able to explain why discourse analysis matters to future English teachers and how different traditions have shaped the questions analysts ask.

## Key concepts and vocabulary

| **Term**                   | **Working definition**                                                                                                                |
| :------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Discourse analysis**     | The systematic study of language in use, including how meaning depends on textual, interactional, social, and cultural context.       |
| **Text**                   | A stretch of spoken, written, or multimodal language treated as an analyzable product.                                                |
| **Discourse**              | Language understood as situated meaning-making, social action, and interaction.                                                       |
| **Context**                | The linguistic, situational, social, cultural, and historical conditions that shape interpretation.                                   |

## Main chapter content

1. **Language beyond the sentence**: Traditional grammar provides essential tools for describing clauses, phrases, word classes, and sentence patterns. Yet successful communication depends on more than grammatical well-formedness. A sequence of correct sentences can still feel confusing, inappropriate, evasive, rude, or disconnected. Conversely, speakers regularly produce fragments, repetitions, pauses, and incomplete clauses that listeners understand without difficulty. Discourse analysis addresses this wider level of meaning. It asks how stretches of language become interpretable as explanations, stories, requests, arguments, lessons, interviews, jokes, or classroom exchanges.
The unit of analysis therefore expands. Instead of asking only whether a sentence is correct, an analyst may ask who produced it, for whom, in what setting, after what previous turn, through which medium, and with what likely purpose. Consider the expression “That window is still open.” In a grammar exercise, it is a declarative sentence in the present tense. In a cold classroom, it may function as an indirect request. In a security inspection, it may report a procedural failure. In a story, it may create suspense. Its communicative force emerges from the relationship between linguistic form and context.
This broader view is especially important for future English teachers. Learners need grammatical resources, but they also need to know how those resources work across connected discourse. They must recognize when a speaker is opening or closing a conversation, how a writer guides a reader through an argument, how politeness is negotiated, and how genre conventions shape expectations. Discourse analysis makes those normally invisible patterns available for teaching.

2. **Text, discourse, and context:** The terms text and discourse are sometimes used interchangeably, but a productive working distinction can guide analysis. Text can be treated as the observable record: the words, clauses, turns, paragraphs, images, or other semiotic elements available for examination. Discourse emphasizes the interpretive and social process through which that record becomes meaningful. A transcript is a text; the negotiation of authority, identity, politeness, or shared knowledge within the transcript is part of its discourse.
Context is not a decorative background added after the language has been examined. It actively constrains interpretation. Linguistic context, or co-text, includes what comes before and after an expression. Situational context includes participants, location, medium, activity, and immediate purpose. Social context includes roles and relationships, such as teacher–student, friend–friend, customer–employee, or expert–novice. Cultural and historical contexts include broader conventions and assumptions that participants may share unevenly.
Analysts must avoid treating context as unlimited. An analysis becomes weak when it explains every feature by invoking a vague “culture” or an unknowable intention. Strong analysis identifies the contextual evidence needed for a specific claim. A change from “Could you possibly…?” to “Do this now” can be related to power, urgency, genre, or interpersonal distance, but the analyst must show how the text and situation support that interpretation. Throughout the semester, evidence will come from recurrent linguistic patterns, participant behavior, sequential position, genre expectations, and comparison across examples.

3. **A brief map of the field**: Discourse analysis developed through several overlapping traditions rather than a single unified theory. Structural approaches extended linguistic description beyond the sentence and examined how larger units are organized. Functional approaches emphasized what language does: requesting, informing, persuading, evaluating, maintaining relationships, and organizing experience. Pragmatics examined meaning in context, including implied meanings and speech acts. Conversation analysis focused closely on the sequential organization of naturally occurring talk. Systemic-functional work connected grammatical choices to meanings and social functions. Critical traditions investigated how discourse participates in ideology, identity, inequality, and institutional power.
These traditions ask different questions and often work with different kinds of data. A structural analysis might identify cohesive links across a paragraph. A functional analysis might explain how those links support an argument. A pragmatic analysis might examine an indirect request. A conversation analyst might study how the next speaker responds and whether the request is treated as recognizable. A critical analysis might ask whose interests are served by the interactional norms of the institution.
The purpose of the historical timeline is not to memorize a parade of names. It is to notice how definitions of language and evidence have broadened. Each approach highlights some features while backgrounding others. Future teachers benefit from methodological flexibility: a classroom problem concerning reading organization requires a different lens from a problem concerning turn-taking or culturally inappropriate requests. The course therefore treats theories as analytical resources rather than competing slogans.

4. **Discourse analysis as professional knowledge for ELT**: Discourse analysis contributes to ELT in at least four ways. First, it improves material selection. Teachers can evaluate whether dialogues sound interactionally plausible, whether reading passages display the organizational patterns students are expected to learn, and whether model texts represent realistic genres. Second, it supports instruction. Teachers can make cohesive devices, thematic progression, speech acts, conversational repair, and rhetorical patterns explicit without reducing communication to fixed formulas.
Third, discourse analysis improves classroom interaction. A teacher can examine wait time, questioning patterns, turn allocation, feedback moves, and the balance between transactional instruction and relationship-building talk. Fourth, it strengthens assessment. Instead of evaluating only sentence-level accuracy, teachers can examine coherence, register, stance, audience awareness, pragmatic appropriateness, and the management of extended turns.
Professional application requires caution. An analyst should not present one sample as representative of an entire community, confuse personal preference with a discourse norm, or prescribe a single “correct” style when multiple conventions are legitimate. The course objective emphasizes pluralistic, high-quality English teaching and openness to diverse cultural, social, and ideological contexts. This means using analysis to expand learners’ choices and interpretive awareness, not to erase their identities or enforce unexamined native-speaker norms.

5. **Transparent AI-supported planning**: This week introduces Level 2 AI use: planning support. An approved GenAI tool may help brainstorm historical theories, propose search terms, or locate possible authentic samples. It may not replace the student’s reading, source evaluation, or analytical conclusions. A plausible AI response is not automatically an accurate response. Models can merge traditions, invent references, oversimplify debates, or describe non-existent examples.
The AI planning appendix makes the process visible. Record the exact prompt, the useful ideas returned, the sources you independently checked, and the decisions you made. Distinguish discovery from evidence: an AI suggestion can point toward Cook, McCarthy, Searle, or another scholar, but the assigned text or verified publication must support the claim. Also record rejected suggestions and explain why they were unsuitable. This practice develops scholarly judgment rather than mere tool operation.
Your shared knowledge base in Capacities.io should similarly show relationships rather than a copied list. Connect a historical orientation to its typical questions, preferred data, analytical concepts, and potential ELT applications. The final map should represent your understanding. The technology supports organization; it does not supply ownership of the ideas.
