## 🎯 Understanding

**Instructors will understand that…**

- A **refined thesis statement** creates explicit alignment between key points and research questions, ensuring that each section heading reflects both the argument's structure and the inquiry driving the literature review.

- **Strategic reading** means selecting and reading sources purposefully—not to summarize everything, but to find evidence, counterarguments, and gaps that speak directly to each key point in the thesis.

- A **well-populated research matrix** (15–20 peer-reviewed sources) serves as both a tracking tool and an analytical workspace, helping the writer see patterns, identify gaps, and organize themes before drafting.

- The **organizational pattern** of a literature review (comparison-contrast, problem-solution, thematic, chronological) should emerge from the argument itself—the structure should serve the thesis, not the other way around.

- **MEAL paragraph structure** (Main idea, Evidence, Analysis, Link) provides a disciplined framework for synthesizing sources, ensuring that each paragraph advances the argument rather than merely listing what others have said.

### 🎯 Language Objective

**Students will be able to…**

- **Revise** a thesis statement to ensure grammatical parallelism across key points and explicit alignment with research questions.

- **Evaluate** sources for relevance by asking, "Does this source address one of my key points?" and documenting the connection in the research matrix.

- **Organize** sources thematically in the research matrix using category labels, annotations, and cross-references that reveal patterns and relationships.

- **Signal** organizational patterns using discourse markers appropriate to the chosen structure (e.g., "In contrast," "Building on this work," "A second approach emphasizes").

- **Compose** a body paragraph using MEAL structure: stating a main idea, citing evidence from multiple sources, analyzing how the evidence supports the claim, and linking back to the thesis.

### 🎯 Vocabulary

**Thesis refinement & alignment**

- **Parallelism** — grammatical consistency across items in a series (e.g., all noun phrases or all gerunds)

- **Research question** — an open-ended question that guides inquiry and aligns with thesis key points

- **Alignment** — the logical connection between thesis key points, research questions, and section headings

**Strategic reading & source management**

- **Strategic reading** — reading with a specific purpose (to answer a question, find evidence for a claim, identify gaps)

- Zotero | Your personal research assistant — a citation management tool that helps organize sources, generate bibliographies, and annotate PDFs

- **Annotation** — brief notes summarizing key findings, methods, or relevance of a source

- **Reference management** — the systematic organization and tracking of sources throughout the research process

**Organization & structure**

- **Organizational pattern** — the overall structure of the literature review (comparison-contrast, problem-solution, thematic, chronological)

- **Comparison-contrast** — organizing sources by highlighting similarities and differences

- **Problem-solution** — structuring the review around a central problem and various proposed solutions

- **Thematic organization** — grouping sources by recurring themes or concepts rather than by author or date

- **Discourse markers** — words or phrases that signal relationships between ideas (e.g., "however," "in addition," "similarly")

**Writing & synthesis**

- **MEAL structure** — a paragraph framework: Main idea, Evidence, Analysis, Link

- **Synthesis** — combining ideas from multiple sources to create new insights or arguments (not just summarizing)

- **Topic sentence** — the opening sentence of a paragraph that states the main idea

- **Transition** — a word, phrase, or sentence that connects one idea to the next
