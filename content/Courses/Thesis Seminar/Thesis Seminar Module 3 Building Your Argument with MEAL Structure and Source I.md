# Thesis Seminar Module 3: Building Your Argument with MEAL Structure and Source Integration

[Thesis Seminar: Week 3 Recap](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6zLGwSJi7kM)[Thesis Seminar: Week 3 Recap - Notes](../Weblinks/Thesis%20Seminar%20Week%203%20Recap.md)

## 🎯 Group Feedback for Week 3

- **Update your thesis statement** so it clearly reflects the main objective of your research.

- Use the **2–4 key points at the end of your thesis** to organize your literature review.

    - If you are answering a **how** question, you will usually present the “ways” using **by + 2–4 ways**, and each way becomes a major section.

    - If you are answering a **why** question, you will list **reasons**, and each reason becomes a major section.

- Your number of key sections determines whether you need **subsections (Level 3 headings)**.

    - If you only have **two key points**, consider adding **2–3 subsections** under each main section.

- After headings and subheadings, **draft topic sentences first**.

    - Expect roughly **11–13 body paragraphs**, so aim for **11–13 topic sentences** before fully drafting individual paragraphs.

- When drafting body paragraphs, keep the **overall argument organized**.

    - Check what comes **before and after** each paragraph.

    - Make sure each paragraph fits its **section/subsection** and connects back to the **thesis**.

- **Timeline:** try to complete the literature review in the next couple of weeks so you can begin **data collection on March 2**.

- This week, **contact as many potential participants as possible**.

    - Use informal conversations to learn about teaching practice and perspectives.

    - Ask **follow-up questions** based on what participants say.

    - Avoid introducing your specific study topic too early (for example, do not bring up “music” until the participant does).

    - Goal: gather enough information to **choose participants wisely** and reduce issues during data collection.

- Next week’s focus: writing **MEAL-based body paragraphs** with strong evidence, **paraphrased parenthetical citations**, clear analysis, linking sentences, and **APA compliance** (references and fonts).

- Workflow note: if you leave questions as **comments** in Word, remove comments you have addressed to keep the document clean.

### 🎯 Understanding

**Instructors will understand that…**

- **Body paragraphs are the building blocks of argument**, not just containers for summaries—each paragraph should advance a specific claim aligned with one of the thesis key points.

- **MEAL structure** (Main idea, Evidence, Analysis, Link) provides a disciplined framework for synthesis, ensuring that evidence serves the argument and analysis interprets rather than repeats what sources say.

- **Synthesis differs fundamentally from summary**: synthesis requires comparing, contrasting, and connecting sources to reveal patterns, tensions, or gaps; summary merely reports what individual sources say in isolation.

- **Citation practices signal scholarly credibility**—strategic use of recent sources demonstrates currency, while seminal works anchor the argument in established knowledge; both are necessary for a robust literature review.

- **Coherence emerges from intentional linking**: topic sentences that explicitly connect to the thesis, transitions that signal logical relationships, and a consistent focus on the research questions create a unified argument rather than a collection of disconnected paragraphs.

### 🎯 Language Objective

**Students will be able to…**

- **Compose** a body paragraph for the first major section using MEAL structure: topic sentence (main idea), evidence from multiple sources, analytical sentences, and a linking sentence.

- **Synthesize** sources by using comparison-contrast language (e.g., "While X argues..., Y found that...") rather than sequentially summarizing each source.

- **Integrate** both recent citations (published within the last 5–10 years) and seminal citations (foundational works) to establish credibility and demonstrate knowledge of the field.

- **Connect** each topic sentence explicitly to the thesis statement and key points using transitional phrases and clear conceptual links.

- **Signal** logical relationships within and between paragraphs using appropriate transitions (e.g., "Furthermore," "In contrast," "Building on this evidence," "This tension reveals").

### 🎯 Vocabulary

**Writing body paragraphs**

- **Body paragraph** — a paragraph in the main body of the literature review that develops one aspect of a key point with evidence and analysis

- **MEAL structure (The M.E.A.L. Plan: A Comprehensive Approach to Paragraph Development)** — a paragraph framework: Main idea (topic sentence), Evidence (citations), Analysis (interpretation), Link (connection back to thesis)

- **Topic sentence** — the opening sentence of a paragraph that states the main claim or idea that paragraph will develop (Topic sentence-heading-thesis alignment)

- **Evidence sentence** — a sentence that presents specific findings, data, or arguments from sources to support the topic sentence

- **Analysis sentence** — a sentence that interprets, explains, or draws conclusions from the evidence (not just repeating what the source said)

- **Linking sentence** — the closing sentence of a paragraph that connects the paragraph's content back to the thesis or forward to the next idea

**Synthesis vs. summary**

- **Synthesis** — combining ideas from multiple sources to create new insights, reveal patterns, or identify tensions

- **Summary** — restating what a single source says in your own words (necessary but insufficient for a literature review)

- **Compare-contrast** — identifying similarities and differences between sources' findings, methods, or arguments

- **Integration** — weaving together multiple sources within a single sentence or paragraph to show relationships

**Citation practices**

- **Recent citation** — reference to a source published within the last 5–10 years, demonstrating awareness of current research

- **Seminal citation** — reference to a foundational or highly influential work in the field, regardless of publication date

- **Parenthetical citation** — an in-text citation in parentheses, typically used when the author's name is not part of the sentence structure (Smith, 2020)

- **Narrative citation** — an in-text citation where the author's name is part of the sentence (Smith (2020) argues that...)

- **Currency** — the recency and relevance of sources to current scholarship

- **Scholarly credibility** — the trustworthiness and authority established through appropriate use of high-quality sources

**Coherence & transitions**

- **Coherence** — the quality of logical connection and flow between ideas across sentences and paragraphs

- **Transition** — a word, phrase, or sentence that signals the relationship between ideas and guides the reader through the argument

- **Additive transition** — signals addition of similar ideas (furthermore, moreover, in addition)

- **Contrastive transition** — signals difference or opposition (however, in contrast, on the other hand)

- **Causal transition** — signals cause-effect relationships (therefore, thus, as a result)

- **Sequential transition** — signals order or progression (first, next, finally)
