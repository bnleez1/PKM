# Academic Integrity and Cognitive Ownership

**Course:** [[Academic Writing]]

**Weekly Module:** [AW-Module 1: Academic Integrity and Cognitive Ownership](../Modules/AW-Module%201%20Academic%20Integrity%20and%20Cognitive%20Ownership.md)

**Purpose:** Establish an ethical foundation for research, authorship, and responsible GenAI use.

---

> **Essential question:** What does it mean to be the genuine author of an academic text in an era of AI?

---

## Learning goals

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to:

- distinguish plagiarism, cheating, unauthorized collaboration, and academic dishonesty;

- explain **cognitive ownership**, **authentic voice**, **transparency**, and **originality**;

- use a four-question test to judge possible GenAI uses;

- explain how process evidence can demonstrate authorship;

- begin thinking about an integrity checklist, authorship declaration, and AI-use log.

---

### 1. Academic integrity begins before the first sentence — 2 min

**Key idea:** Academic writing begins with decisions about ownership, responsibility, and the honest representation of ideas.

- Integrity is not only about avoiding penalties.

- It is a set of habits that make learning visible ([Show your Work, by Austin Kleon](https://www.amazon.com.mx/Show-Your-Work-Creativity-Discovered/dp/076117897X/ref=sr_1_1?dib=eyJ2IjoiMSJ9.9IS2uFjadlva76s81fNnCDWezD1BAOqOW3cgdSZ6Zl2KVwdXcHU_L3y4B9YVEXSnJm-tk63rbKP8xeD3OIxsc6H3kPlj2z8qsIeJS7LUbxh0GoVG7yap58Lr6cFkszp7Oy6Jl2sSCVcF3ilTm85bwa1PLxWL-1QivVKoL6pZc7f19Mzr4v58hNPiFxnzrADDpySM8FAWU_w7K2LG_Z9Dy39Ch3xeRU8CKAal0yVy0yDTNnWadyUGVeeCwXvGjKi0fVknZBSjh8eNor55Kb0o9OztpYOZHSiyFowi6Wrm5Ck.dKmWFzkhXiXjOxNYmqAsfqqV4oUKoX9xeLbgezLUnP4&dib_tag=se&keywords=show+your+work&qid=1786383375&sr=8-1&ufe=app_do%3Aamzn1.fos.1f47693a-e1a6-4b06-ba29-28033858cf82)) and academic work trustworthy.

- A writer should be able to explain where important ideas came from, how they were evaluated, and why the final choices belong to the writer.

**Prompt for learners:** *If your name is on a paper, what should you be able to explain about it?*

---

### 2. Four ideas that are related—but not identical — 2 min

**Academic dishonesty** is the broad category that includes forms of deception in academic work.

- **Plagiarism:** presenting another person’s words, ideas, structure, or work as one’s own without appropriate acknowledgment.

- **Cheating:** using prohibited assistance, materials, or methods to gain an unfair academic advantage.

- **Unauthorized collaboration:** working with another person beyond the level permitted for the assignment.

- **Academic integrity:** consistent ethical conduct in learning, research, writing, and assessment.

**Important:** What is acceptable may depend on the assignment. Brainstorming with a peer may be encouraged in one task and prohibited in another.

---

### 3. Authorship is more than a name on the page — 2 min

**Cognitive ownership** means taking responsibility for understanding, evaluating, and making the decisions represented in a text.

A student who owns the work should be able to explain:

- Why did I choose this topic?

- Why did I select this source?

- How did I interpret the evidence?

- What changed during revision, and why?

**Key distinction:** A polished paper is not automatically an owned paper. An imperfect draft may demonstrate a stronger authorship when the writer’s reasoning and decisions are visible.

---

### 4. Authentic voice and originality — 2 min

For an English language learner, **authentic voice** does not mean perfect English.

It means that the language and reasoning genuinely reflect the writer’s:

- knowledge and understanding (see [Understanding by Design:  A Framework for Effective Curriculum Development](../Pages/Understanding%20by%20Design%20%20A%20Framework%20for%20Effective%20Curriculum%20Development.md).);

- decisions;

- current level of English; and

- developing academic identity.

**Originality** is not only a similarity score. It is the degree to which the work reflects the student’s own writing and intellectual contribution.

---

### 5. What counts as evidence of ownership? — 2 min

Cognitive ownership can be demonstrated through a **learning trace**.

Examples include:

- notes and topic maps;

- handwritten or digital drafts;

- audio brainstorming;

- source-evaluation records;

- version histories;

- reflective explanations;

- an authorship declaration;

- an AI-use log.

These artifacts matter when they show how the learner **generated, tested, rejected, and refined ideas**.

---

### 6. GenAI: support the learning, do not replace it — 3 min

The central question is not simply, *“Did you use AI?”*The stronger question is, *“What cognitive work did you retain?”*

**Potentially appropriate planning support may include:**

- generating possible search terms;

- comparing ways to narrow a topic;

- mapping relationships among concepts.

These uses remain acceptable only when the learner evaluates suggestions, verifies information, and makes the final decisions.

**Uses that undermine authorship include:**

- asking AI to write the research proposal;

- generating source-based paragraphs the student has not researched;

- using AI to paraphrase material in order to disguise borrowing.

---

### 7. The four-question GenAI decision test — 2 min

Before using GenAI, ask:

1. **Does the assignment permit this use?**

2. **Does the tool support the learning process, or replace it?**

3. **Can I verify the output using reliable sources and my own understanding?**

4. **Can I document the use honestly?**

If any answer is uncertain, **ask the instructor before proceeding**.

> Transparency does not automatically make an unauthorized practice acceptable, but it prevents concealment and makes guidance possible.

---

### 8. The AI-use log is a record of judgment — 2 min

An effective AI-use log should record:

- date;

- task;

- exact prompt or type of assistance;

- brief description of the output;

- what the student kept, changed, or rejected;

- why the student made that decision.

A rejected AI suggestion can be strong evidence of critical thinking when the learner can explain the rejection.

**The log is not a screenshot archive. It is a record of decisions.**

---

### 9. Learning from an integrity case — 2 min

The **Nuñez Arancibia case** provides a context for examining authorship, public accusation, evidence, and contested intellectual ownership.

When analyzing the case, separate:

- observable facts from interpretations;

- documented similarities from assumptions;

- supported claims from uncertain claims.

Then transfer the issue to your own research proposal: How could similar problems arise through borrowed wording, unattributed ideas, fabricated references, AI-generated text, or excessive assistance?

---

### 10. Closing: Can you defend your choices? — 1 min

A useful test for the entire semester is this:

> **Can I explain the origin and function of every important choice in my text?**

Before leaving, complete this sentence:

**One decision in my academic writing that I should always be able to justify is…**

This week, your integrity diagnostic, case analysis, proposal integrity checklist, authorship declaration, AI-use log, and no-AI topic-ownership reflection begin the learning trace you will build throughout the course.
