# AW-Module 3: Reporting Research with Accuracy and Caution


⚡️ Using verb tense, modality, and hedging to represent knowledge responsibly

> Academic writers do more than report information; they position information in time and indicate how certain a claim should sound. A single verb tense can tell readers whether a statement expresses established knowledge, describes a completed study, or connects past research to a continuing discussion. Modal verbs and hedging expressions further distinguish fact, interpretation, probability, and recommendation. This week begins the article-review unit by treating grammar as a rhetorical resource rather than a collection of isolated rules. Students analyze published research and model reviews, then apply the patterns to sentences describing an article’s background, purpose, method, and findings. The objective is accuracy without exaggeration. A strong reviewer does not weaken every sentence with cautious language, but neither does the reviewer present limited evidence as universal truth. Effective academic stance depends on matching language to evidence.


## Essential Questions


1. How do tense and modality shape the accuracy and credibility of an article review?

2. How can a writer express evaluation without overstating certainty?

## Guiding Questions


1. When should a review use present, past, or present perfect tense?

2. How do modal verbs and hedges distinguish fact, interpretation, and possibility?

3. What language accurately reports an article’s purpose, method, findings, and claims?

4. How can evaluative statements remain clear while acknowledging limits?

## Learning outcomes

• Use present, past, and present perfect tense appropriately when reporting research.

• Employ modal verbs and hedging expressions to calibrate certainty.

• Distinguish statements of fact, interpretation, possibility, and recommendation.

• Draft accurate background, purpose, method, and finding sentences for an article review.

• Revise evaluative claims to avoid unsupported generalization or excessive caution.

## Key concepts and vocabulary

| **Term**              | **Working definition**                                                                                                |
| :-------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Reporting tense**   | A tense selected to show the time and status of information in academic discourse.                                    |
| **Simple present**    | A tense often used for current arguments, textual content, and generally accepted knowledge.                          |
| **Simple past**       | A tense commonly used for completed procedures, data collection, and specific past findings.                          |
| **Present perfect**   | A tense used to connect previous research with a continuing or current scholarly situation.                           |
| **Modal verb**        | A verb such as may, might, can, could, should, or must that expresses possibility, ability, obligation, or inference. |
| **Hedging**           | Language that limits a claim so its certainty matches the available evidence.                                         |
| **Booster**           | Language that strengthens emphasis or confidence, such as clearly, strongly, or demonstrates.                         |
| **Stance**            | The writer’s position toward a claim, source, or degree of certainty.                                                 |
| **Generalization**    | A statement that extends a claim beyond the immediate evidence.                                                       |
| **Epistemic caution** | Careful attention to what can and cannot be known or claimed from evidence.                                           |

## Main chapter content

## Grammar as a system of meaning

In academic writing, grammar helps readers interpret the status of information. Verb tense places actions and claims in time, but it also signals whether knowledge is treated as current, completed, or developing. Modality indicates possibility, necessity, or recommendation. These choices are rhetorical because they influence how the reader evaluates the writer’s accuracy. An article review that shifts tenses randomly can make it difficult to distinguish what the article argues from what the researchers did. A review that uses strong certainty for every claim can misrepresent the evidence.

Students should begin by identifying the function of each sentence. Is it presenting a concept that remains relevant? Describing what the authors did? Reporting a result from a specific study? Summarizing a body of research over time? Evaluating an implication? Once the function is clear, tense and modality become easier to choose. Editing grammar without identifying meaning often produces superficial corrections. The goal is not to apply one tense to an entire review, but to make purposeful shifts that readers can follow.

## Present, past, and present perfect in research reporting

The simple present is often used to discuss what an article says or argues because the text remains available to the reader: “The authors argue that peer feedback supports revision.” It is also common for definitions and claims treated as generally current: “Academic writing requires clear attribution.” The simple past usually describes completed research actions: “The researchers interviewed twelve participants” or “The study found three recurring themes.” This helps separate the published article’s current argument from the historical process used to produce the evidence.

The present perfect connects previous work to a continuing scholarly conversation: “Researchers have examined the role of feedback in second-language writing.” It is useful when summarizing trends or identifying a gap that still matters. However, writers should avoid using it simply because it sounds formal. A specific study completed in 2023 is normally reported in the past, while a body of work extending to the present may be reported in the present perfect. Consistency should be checked within each sentence and paragraph, but accurate meaning is more important than mechanical uniformity.

## Calibrating certainty with modal verbs and hedges

Modal verbs allow a writer to represent different levels and types of certainty. May and might express possibility; can often expresses capacity or a recurring possibility; could may signal a tentative explanation or option; should often introduces a recommendation; must can express strong necessity or a logical conclusion. Because these meanings differ, modal verbs are not interchangeable. “The findings may indicate” is more cautious than “The findings demonstrate,” while “Teachers should provide feedback” presents a recommendation rather than a verified outcome.

Hedges also include lexical verbs and phrases such as suggests, appears, is likely to, tends to, in this context, among the participants, and the findings should be interpreted cautiously. Effective hedging is specific. A writer should identify the source of limitation: sample size, research design, context, measurement, or conflicting evidence. Excessive hedging can make writing vague and indecisive, especially when every sentence contains perhaps or seems. The principle is proportionality: the strength of the language should match the strength and scope of the evidence.

## Separating fact, interpretation, and possibility

Article reviews often combine several layers of discourse. A factual description might state that the study included twenty participants. An interpretation might argue that the small sample limits transferability. A possibility might suggest that the findings could differ in another institution. These layers should not be blended as though they have equal status. Attribution helps: “The authors report…” identifies the source’s claim, while “This limitation suggests…” signals the reviewer’s analysis.

Students can improve accuracy by labeling sentences during revision: F for verifiable fact, S for source claim, I for interpretation, P for possibility, and R for recommendation. The labels reveal whether the review relies too heavily on summary or presents opinions without evidence. They also support tense decisions. Facts about the research process usually take the past tense; current textual claims often use the present; interpretations and possibilities require appropriate stance markers. This form of coding turns grammar practice into critical reading.

## Applying tense and stance to the article-review draft

The first article-review sentences should establish the article’s topic, purpose, context, and method accurately. A useful sequence is to identify the current focus of the article, state what the researchers sought to examine, describe what they did, and report the principal findings. The reviewer should then begin evaluating how convincingly the evidence supports the authors’ claims. At this stage, evaluation may remain provisional because the full review is still developing.

This week’s assignments include analysis of tense use in published research, a tense-editing task, a hedging exercise, and draft sentences for the background, purpose, method, and evaluative claims of the article review. Revision should involve more than replacing verbs. Students should explain why a tense or hedge is appropriate. GenAI may be used later in the unit as an editor and critical friend, but the initial sentences and reasoning should be independently drafted. This preserves authentic language and ensures that grammatical choices develop from the student’s understanding of the article.

## From concepts to this week’s deliverable

The week’s editing work should begin with classification rather than correction. Students can label sentences according to their purpose: established knowledge, description of the reviewed article, report of a completed procedure, statement of a finding, interpretation, evaluation, or recommendation. Once the purpose is visible, tense and stance choices become easier to justify. A present-tense sentence may describe what an article argues or what a theory explains; a past-tense sentence may report what participants did or what researchers found in a completed study; present perfect may connect a continuing line of research to the current discussion. The objective is not to force every sentence into a fixed rule but to make grammatical choices consistent with meaning.

The deliverables also require students to calibrate the strength of evaluative claims. Compare an absolute statement such as “The method proves that the intervention works” with a bounded alternative such as “The findings suggest that the intervention may have supported improvement in this context.” The second version separates the evidence from the writer’s interpretation and acknowledges limits of setting, sample, and design. During the hedging exercise, students should identify which words change certainty, scope, or frequency. During revision, they should check that caution does not become vagueness: a claim can be precise and appropriately qualified at the same time. The final background, purpose, method, finding, and evaluation sentences should demonstrate both grammatical accuracy and epistemic caution.

## Suggested weekly learning sequence

A productive learning sequence for this chapter begins with a diagnostic response to the essential question, “How do tense and modality shape the accuracy and credibility of an article review?” Students should first record an individual answer and then compare it with examples, course criteria, and peer interpretations. A short concept-sorting activity using Reporting tense, Simple present, Simple past, and Present perfect can make important distinctions visible before students work on a full draft. The instructor can then model one decision aloud, showing not only the completed example but also the questions, alternatives, and evidence that led to it. Students apply the same process to a small portion of their own project and receive formative feedback before extending the work independently.

The second part of the week should move from practice to production. Students use the guiding questions—“When should a review use present, past, or present perfect tense?” and “How do modal verbs and hedges distinguish fact, interpretation, and possibility?”—as checkpoints while working toward the stated outcomes, especially the ability to use present, past, and present perfect tense appropriately when reporting research and to employ modal verbs and hedging expressions to calibrate certainty. A brief peer conference should focus on one high-impact feature rather than attempting to correct everything at once. Students then revise, annotate the evidence of their decision-making, and complete the week’s deliverable: Tense analysis and editing assignment; background, purpose, method, and finding sentences; hedging exercise; revised evaluative claims. The final five minutes of class can be used for an exit reflection identifying one decision the writer can now justify and one question that must be carried into the next chapter.
